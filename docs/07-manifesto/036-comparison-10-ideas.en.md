### 85.1. Purpose of the section

This section studies 10 ideas that appeared over the last 20 years (2005–2026) and fall into the category "reforms of democratic mechanics with a possible economic element". The goal is not to describe the ideas but to extract stable patterns of success and failure, so that AB-EXIT can rely on the empirical experience of its predecessors rather than be built from scratch.

The section also records one specific observation: AB-EXIT surpasses the nearest ideologically related idea (Plurality / quadratic voting) in clarity of communication and in having a ready implementation mechanism.

### 85.2. Ten ideas with analysis

**1. Ranked Choice Voting (RCV).** Adopted in San Francisco 2004, in Maine 2016 (by initiative), in Alaska 2020. By 2026 — 7 states have RCV, 19 states have banned it by law. *What worked:* a centralised advocacy organisation, FairVote, with 30+ years of expertise, the strategy "local pilots → states", anchor donors, an anti-party narrative. *What did not:* partisan blocking in red states, the difficulty of explaining the mechanics to an ordinary voter. *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* local pilots create empirical proof before a state-level launch.

**2. UBI / Yang's Freedom Dividend.** Yang launched a campaign in 2017 around $1,000/month. Personally paid several families from campaign funds in 2019. By 2026 — UBI not adopted at the federal level, but pilots in dozens of cities (Stockton first). *What worked:* a bright simple message, a candidate-evangelist as the face of the idea, empirical demonstration through real payments, cross-party appeal. *What did not:* the lack of a realistic fiscal model for scaling; the idea got stuck at the local level. *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* a human face makes an idea recognisable, but without a fiscal model it stays local.

**3. Citizens' Assemblies / Sortition.** Ireland 2012–18 (the abortion referendum via an assembly, a success), France 2019–20 (the climate convention). By 2026 — dozens of assemblies worldwide. *What worked:* the bingo signal "ordinary people can", parliamentary approval in Ireland, concrete cases with measurable results. *What did not:* assemblies give recommendations, have no legislative force; parliament can gut them (Macron with the French proposals). *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* partnership with parliament gives legitimacy, but if parliament keeps the last word, the idea is easily diluted. This is an argument for citizen initiative (Route A, section 68).

**4. Quadratic Voting / Plurality (RadicalxChange, Glen Weyl).** The book Radical Markets 2018, the RxC Foundation 2018, use in the Colorado House 2019 and Gitcoin Grants. By 2026 — academically known, no governmental adoption. *What worked:* intellectual elegance attracted Buterin, Tang, the academic circles of Microsoft Research, Harvard, MIT; the open-source book Plurality (2024) spread free of charge; a strong theoretical base. *What did not:* the concept is too complex for the broad public, "quadratic" requires explaining mathematics, no bright message, use remained in niche contexts. *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* intellectual elegance ≠ public success.

**5. Top-Two / Top-Four Primaries.** California 2010 (Prop 14), Washington 2008, Alaska 2020 (with RCV). By 2026 — works in 3 states. *What worked:* alliance with anti-partisan movements, the promise to reduce extremism, empirical support after adoption (Mary Peltola, Lisa Murkowski in Alaska). *What did not:* parties actively block — it is an attack on their primary monopoly. *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* an attack on party monopoly works through direct elections. AB-EXIT goes further — it attacks the mobilisation base of both parties (the apathetic) economically.

**6. Independent Redistricting Commissions.** California 2008 and 2010 (Props 11 and 20), Michigan 2018, Colorado, Utah, Ohio. By 2026 — about 12 states with independent commissions. *What worked:* a clear anti-corruption narrative, partisan balance (both parties could be victims), anchor donors (Chan-Zuckerberg, Hewlett), submission via ballot initiative as a bypass of parliament. *What did not:* party resistance in red states. *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* the structure "bypass parliament via ballot initiative" works (our Route A). An anti-corruption narrative attracts all sides.

**7. Vote-by-Mail.** Oregon 1998, Colorado 2013, Washington 2011, expansion after COVID-19. By 2026 — 8 states with universal mail-in; restrictions introduced in red states. *What worked:* COVID-19 as a catalyst, existing models (Oregon), active Democratic promotion. *What did not:* partisan polarisation after 2020 (the Trump-MAGA narrative about fraud). *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* a reform can become a partisan battle; it is important to avoid association with one party. AB-EXIT via the narrative "money for everyone" is structurally harder to partisanise.

**8. State-level UBI Experiments.** Stockton SEED 2019 ($500/month × 125 residents × 2 years), the Mayors for a Guaranteed Income coalition 2020. By 2026 — 100+ cities in the USA. *What worked:* Stockton's empirical results (published 2021), the mayor-to-mayor network, association with Yang's UBI. *What did not:* the pilots remained pilots, funding private, scaling to a state UBI did not happen. *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* local pilots with empirical data are a strong legitimation tool.

**9. Worldcoin (Sam Altman).** Launch 2021, open launch July 2023. By 2026 — tens of millions of users, but regulatory problems (Kenya, Hong Kong, Europe). *What worked:* Altman's venture funding ($240M+), global spread, the link to the AI narrative. *What did not:* privacy problems (biometrics as a mandatory condition), regulatory bans, perception as "a billionaires' technological utopia". *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* avoid association with biometrics and the control of one corporation. AB-EXIT through citizen initiative and state administration is more legitimate. Minimising technology in the public description (section 78.10) is the right direction.

**10. Liquid Democracy (Pirate Party Germany).** The LiquidFeedback platform since 2010, 8.9 % in Berlin 2011. By 2026 — the Pirate Party has practically disappeared, the tool remained an academic concept. *What worked:* intellectual appeal, transparency, a working technology. *What did not:* no scaling happened, difficulty of explanation, technical problems of the platform. *Lesson for AB-EXIT:* idea + technology is not enough. An emotional face, a simple message, an anchor political partner are needed.

### 85.3. What works (common patterns of successful ideas)

**First: a simple memorable 5-second message.** RCV: "rank the candidates". UBI: "$1,000 to everyone". Top-Two: "the two best go through". The more complex the explanation, the worse the result. AB-EXIT: "money or a strengthened vote" — works.

**Second: local pilots before big victories.** RCV — cities first, then states. UBI — Stockton, then other cities. Anti-gerrymandering — one state at a time. Without local proof an idea remains theory.

**Third: an anchor donor + a mass base.** All successful reforms had an anchor donor (Action Now Initiative, Chan-Zuckerberg, Dorsey). A pure grassroots campaign without money does not work. A purely donor campaign without a local base does not work either.

**Fourth: bypassing parliament via ballot initiative.** Every successful reform of the last 20 years passed through direct democracy, not through legislation. The parliamentary path is blocked almost always. Validates Route A (section 68).

**Fifth: empirical verification after the first adoption.** RCV in Maine → data → spread. Stockton SEED → published results → spread. Without measurable data after a pilot the idea does not scale.

**Sixth: a cross-partisan narrative.** Anti-gerrymandering worked because it hit both parties. AB-EXIT via section 80.7 (works for all three camps) does this structurally.

### 85.4. What does not work (common patterns of failure)

— Dependence on one political party (Vote-by-mail became "Democratic" — a ceiling on support). — Complex technical concepts without a simple explanation (Quadratic Voting, Liquid Democracy). — Control by one actor (Worldcoin as Altman's project). — Absence of a first real adoption (Liquid Democracy outside the Pirate Party).

### 85.5. AB-EXIT vs the whole class of reforms — structural superiority in the combination of parameters

Comparing AB-EXIT with one idea taken separately (for example, Plurality) shows superiority in several respects. But a more precise analysis shows that AB-EXIT surpasses the WHOLE class of studied reforms in a unique combination of parameters that none of the ten ideas of section 85.2 has.

**Five parameters by which the ideas are compared:**

1. **Clarity of message** — can the essence be explained in 5 seconds without special knowledge?
2. **Material incentive for the voter** — is there a direct personal benefit from adoption?
3. **A ready implementation mechanism** — is there a proven path from idea to real adoption?
4. **Universal applicability** — does the idea work in different types of political systems?
5. **Complementarity with other reforms** — does the idea reinforce other reforms or compete with them?

**Comparison matrix by these parameters:**

— **RCV.** Clarity: medium (ranking must be explained). Material incentive: none. Mechanism: ballot initiative works in states with the right of initiative. Universality: medium (mainly English-speaking systems). Complementarity: competes with plurality voting.

— **UBI / Freedom Dividend.** Clarity: high. Material incentive: high (direct payments). Mechanism: absent (Yang lost the primaries on the question "where's the money from"). Universality: high in principle, but unrealised without a mechanism. Complementarity: neutral.

— **Citizens' Assemblies.** Clarity: low (requires explaining deliberative democracy). Material incentive: none. Mechanism: parliamentary, but parliament can gut it. Universality: high in developed democracies. Complementarity: high.

— **Quadratic Voting / Plurality.** Clarity: low (requires explaining mathematics). Material incentive: none. Mechanism: absent after 8 years of public activity. Universality: medium. Complementarity: high, but theoretical without a mechanism.

— **Top-Two Primaries.** Clarity: medium. Material incentive: none. Mechanism: ballot initiative works. Universality: low (the specifics of the American two-party system). Complementarity: complements RCV.

— **Independent Redistricting.** Clarity: high (anti-corruption narrative). Material incentive: none. Mechanism: ballot initiative works. Universality: low (the specifics of American districts). Complementarity: neutral.

— **Vote-by-Mail.** Clarity: high. Material incentive: none. Mechanism: legislative, but partisanised. Universality: high. Complementarity: neutral.

— **State-level UBI Experiments.** Clarity: high. Material incentive: high locally. Mechanism: pilots via mayors, no scaling. Universality: medium. Complementarity: with a federal UBI.

— **Worldcoin.** Clarity: medium. Material incentive: present but small. Mechanism: corporate, regulatorily problematic. Universality: global, but banned in a number of countries. Complementarity: none (control by one corporation).

— **Liquid Democracy.** Clarity: low. Material incentive: none. Mechanism: absent after 15+ years. Universality: theoretical. Complementarity: none (requires full replacement of the electoral system).

**Where AB-EXIT stands on all five parameters:**

1. Clarity of message: high. "Money or a strengthened vote, you choose" — 5 seconds without special knowledge.

2. Material incentive: high. Direct payments tied to the local economy (about 300 dollars on world average). The incentive works for the whole electorate, not only the poor strata.

3. A ready implementation mechanism: TWO routes. Route A via citizen-initiated statute (section 68, the ready Oregon-2028 plan). Route B via parliamentary scandal + private referendum (section 84, ready for most of the world).

4. Universal applicability: global. Works in the USA, Switzerland, Italy via Route A. Works in Poland, Hungary, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Japan via Route B. Works even in semi-authoritarian systems.

5. Complementarity: high. AB-EXIT works at a level above other reforms (section 82). First it removes the apathetic, creating an electorate capable of quality deliberation. Then the tools of Plurality (quadratic voting, Polis), RCV, Citizens' Assemblies work more effectively.

**Structural superiority:**

Not one of the ten studied ideas has ALL five parameters at a high level simultaneously. Every successful reform (RCV, Independent Redistricting) is implemented but has one or two weak parameters, because of which it spreads slowly. Every intellectually elegant reform (Plurality, Liquid Democracy) has strong theoretical parameters but a critically weak implementation mechanism.

AB-EXIT is the only reform in the studied class that has a high rating on all five parameters at once. This is not self-praise — it is an operational fact that can be checked through the same comparative analysis.

And it is important to stress: not one of the ten studied ideas even approaches such a combination. Those that are implemented (RCV, Independent Redistricting, Vote-by-Mail, State UBI Experiments) are implemented partially, slowly, or have been partisanised. Those that are intellectually elegant (Plurality, Liquid Democracy) have no mechanism. Those that are popular as an idea (Freedom Dividend) have no path.

AB-EXIT combines intellectual elegance with a ready implementation mechanism through a referendum — a unique combination that none of the projects of the last 20 years had, even those that were implemented.

### 85.7. The irreversibility of AB-EXIT as a sixth comparison parameter

In comparing AB-EXIT with the ten reforms by five parameters (section 85.5) one critically important sixth parameter was omitted: irreversibility. This parameter determines WHETHER the reform will TAKE HOLD after adoption, or be constantly subjected to rollbacks.

Empirical data from the studied reforms show that most of them are under constant threat of rollback or are already being rolled back:

— **RCV:** implemented in 7 states but banned by law in 19 states. Trump-MAGA supporters actively work on rollback in the states of adoption. — **Vote-by-Mail:** expanded massively after COVID-19, but many red states passed laws restricting mail-in voting after 2020. — **Independent Redistricting Commissions:** in several states parties are working to dissolve them. — **State-level UBI Experiments:** funded by private donors, close when the funding ends. — **Citizens' Assemblies:** parliament guts the recommendations (the French climate example). — **Worldcoin:** banned by a single regulatory act in a country. — **Liquid Democracy:** disappeared with the decline of the Pirate Party.

That is, most reforms of the last 20 years are under constant threat of rollback; many are already being rolled back.

**AB-EXIT is structurally irreversible for five reasons:**

**First — a mass class of dividend recipients.** After the first cycle a group of tens or hundreds of millions of people receiving direct payments forms. Any attempt to abolish AB-EXIT means taking their money from this mass. In politics there is not a single precedent of a state successfully abolishing a mass payment to citizens accustomed to receiving it.

**Second — the remaining electorate is structurally stronger.** Pragmatists and Ideologues with tripled vote weight are able to analyse and resist rollback attempts. This is not an apathetic electorate manipulated by populism but a conscious electorate defending its new opportunities.

**Third — benefit for business.** AB-EXIT creates a coalition of citizens and honest business (section 80.7). Business after AB-EXIT works in a more transparent environment. Abolishing AB-EXIT returns business to a corrupt system. Honest business becomes a natural ally of preserving AB-EXIT.

**Fourth — measurable economic improvements.** After the first cycle measurable improvements appear: growth of the median wage (and therefore of the dividend), a fall in corruption, growth of long-term investment. These improvements become the argument "roll back = lose the achievements". Norway shows that a transparent accountable system creates a reinforcement loop.

**Fifth — the formula is fixed in a smart contract via citizen initiative.** Section 73 (protection from the poison pill) and section 78 (technical architecture) show that changing the formula requires a new referendum. Parliament cannot abolish AB-EXIT through a by-law. This is structural protection against quiet erosion.

**A sixth reinforcement — the international demonstration effect.** If AB-EXIT is implemented in one country and works, other countries will want it. Rollback becomes an international reputational catastrophe.

**Comparison with historical precedents of the irreversibility of mass payments:**

History has rare examples of reforms that after first adoption proved practically irreversible:

— **Social Security in the USA (1935):** after 90 years of existence nobody seriously proposes abolishing it, despite structural problems. Too many recipients. — **Medicare in the USA (1965):** the same. — **FDIC deposit insurance (1933):** built into the financial infrastructure. — **The NHS in the United Kingdom (1948):** any party that seriously tries to abolish it loses the election. — **The GI Bill (1944):** after adoption became politically untouchable.

What do these reforms have in common? All created mass groups of recipients with a direct material benefit. Once the recipients formed, any attempt at abolition becomes political suicide.

AB-EXIT falls into this category. After the first cycle of implementation it becomes as untouchable as Social Security or the NHS. This is not theoretical durability; it is the empirically tested pattern of the irreversibility of mass payments.

**The structural significance of the sixth parameter:**

All the other five parameters (clarity, material incentive, mechanism, universality, complementarity) determine WHETHER the reform will REACH adoption. The sixth parameter — irreversibility — determines WHETHER it will TAKE HOLD after adoption.

Many reforms pass the first barrier but not the second. AB-EXIT is unique in that after first adoption an attempt at rollback meets structural resistance of such force that the probability of a successful rollback is close to zero.

This turns AB-EXIT not merely into "a reform with the best starting set of parameters" but into **a reform that stays after adoption**. Most reforms of the 20th–21st centuries are battles that must be fought continuously. AB-EXIT is a battle that is decided once and takes hold.
