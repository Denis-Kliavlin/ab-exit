## 1. Man-made — yes; a catastrophe — not in the numbers but in the horizon

| Indicator | As of 14 October 2026 | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | 3.7 % year on year (September), gasoline +9.1 % in the month | BLS |
| Fed rate | 3.75–4 %, the first hike since 2023, unanimous | Fed |
| Unemployment | 4.2 %; hiring sluggish, layoffs low | BLS |
| GDP growth 2026 | 2.4–2.8 %, carried by consumption and AI investment | US Bank, Vanguard |
| Tariff cost per household | ~$1,000 in 2025, $600–920 in 2026; the bottom quintile pays more than the top as a share of income | Tax Foundation, Yale, TPC |
| IEEPA tariffs | struck down by the Supreme Court 6–3 (20 February 2026), replaced under other statutes | Skadden, CRS |

The economy grows and unemployment is low — not thanks to the policy but despite it: the wave of capital investment in AI covers the tariff tax and expensive money. Without that boom the same decisions would have produced stagflation. The damage is real, and it is paid by the most productive sector — the one that does not vote (1f §3).

Three sources of damage — decisions, not shocks. *Tariffs*: a tax on the consumer, 96 % of the burden on US importers and buyers (Kiel Institute); the court struck them down — the administration restored them under another statute. *Pressure on the Fed*: Warsh appointed in May, a demand for a 1 % rate at 3.7 % inflation, the board called "hostile", Waller publicly on the threat to independence. *Immigration*: the labour market does not grow because there is nobody to hire. There was no external shock, as in 2008 or 2020.

## 2. The form of the damage follows from the voter

All three measures have the same form: a visible benefit now, the bill later. A tariff looks like a blow to foreigners and is paid at the till six months on; a low rate looks like cheap credit and is paid in inflation; deportations look like order and are paid in missing hands. It is an economy with a three-day horizon, and it has an old name — Nordhaus's political business cycle (036e §2). A president elected by short horizons makes policy for short horizons (059i §3), and the hall approves it until gasoline is +9 %. The bill comes to the same hall: the bottom quintile pays more for tariffs than the top. The brought-in pay for the show they were brought to.

Where the catastrophe nonetheless is — in the horizon. Tariffs and inflation can be rolled back within a cycle; the Fed's independence and the predictability of trade law are rebuilt over decades and cost more than all tariffs together — in the risk premium on US debt. That damage is invisible to the voter with a short horizon (it is not on the price tag) and visible to the burned (it is in the rate on their loans and in the dollar). The catastrophe is exactly where the hall does not look — and that is the mirror again.

## 3. Diesel: the blow that lands on the core

Record $6.53 a gallon (21 September 2026); mid-October $6.18–6.28, +72 % in a year; gasoline meanwhile $4.33 — below the spring peak. The divergence shows it is not only about crude: were it the raw material, gasoline and diesel would rise together. Three components, all three decisions: Canada, the largest supplier of crude and refined products, under a tariff war; the world distillate market squeezed by a war with Iran begun without an aim and abandoned halfway (Hormuz — day 226 of restrictions); the strategic reserve at its lowest since 1982.

Diesel cars are 2.5 % of the US fleet, but all logistics — locomotives and trucks — and all agriculture run on diesel. Katz puts the difference exactly: expensive gasoline hits the nerves here and now, expensive diesel hits the wallet later — through the price of every good and through the interest rate. To the apathetic it is an invisible tax: at the pump gasoline is cheaper than in spring, and he does not see diesel in the price of a hammer. The Kansas farmer sees it — in a balance sheet he cannot pay. The farmer is the party's core, Dalton's "ritual partisans" (040aj §0), those whose votes "can be counted on without looking". The result: races in Kansas and Nebraska, where no campaign had been run for half a century, became competitive. Losing the midterms is the norm; losing the base is a catastrophe, and, in Katz's words, "made by Trump's own hands".

The "diesel deal" with Putin for 300,000 tonnes (per Katz): US daily consumption exceeds half a million tonnes; the whole announced volume with continuations, 4.8 million tonnes over several months, is nine days of consumption spread across the world market; the market did not react, and the price rose after the announcement. It is "$5,000 for everyone" (059c, 059i §2) in fuel form: a number from the head, impressive to one who does not know the scale and empty to one who does. As a fact, the deal is unconfirmed in open sources as of 14 October 2026; per Katz it has already cost Ukraine launch data and Starlink at the front.

The farmer today has two moves: stay home or vote for a stranger. The third — "not me, and that is counted" — does not exist. Ballot line B (048m art. 15) was written for him.

## 4. Forecast and check

- Midterms, 3 November 2026: the Republicans lose the Senate (by the models Katz cites — 53 seats for the Democrats; unverified by the assistant), and at least one race in Kansas or Nebraska proves competitive in the result, not only in the polls.
- Diesel does not return below $5 before the end of 2026 without the lifting of restrictions at Hormuz.
- The tariffs restored under other statutes after the court's ruling are not repealed before the midterms — the grab does not retreat before a rule (059i §4).

## Weak point

The data are a snapshot on one date; the AI boom's share of growth is analysts' estimate, not a factor decomposition; the "diesel deal" and the midterm forecasts are Katz's, unverified; the regressivity of tariffs is by TPC's calculation, and methods differ. The video transcript is automatic.
