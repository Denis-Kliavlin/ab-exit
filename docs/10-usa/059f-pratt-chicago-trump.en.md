## 2. Spencer Pratt, Los Angeles 2026

Verified data from the primary of 2 June 2026: Karen Bass 34.3 % (292,593), Nithya Raman 29.0 % (247,781), Spencer Pratt 25.5 % (217,977); Bass and Raman advanced to the 3 November runoff. Pratt — a reality-TV star, a complete outsider without a machine or party base, on anti-system noise. The profile of an ideal carrier by the model: a real underdog with nothing to defend in the old model; a city with enormous apathy and irritation (homelessness, bureaucracy, fires, "everything is broken"); an audience that loves one hard formula, not a multi-page platform; what worked against him was precisely the machine and the habitual frame — what the protocol strikes at.

| Indicator | Without the protocol | With the protocol as the central theme |
|---|---|---|
| Personal support | 25–26 %, third place, eliminated | 38–48 %, guide ~42 % |
| Chance of reaching the runoff | Low | High |
| Chance of winning in the end | Very low | Medium and above |

Why the jump: the apathetic get a comprehensible personal interest for the first time and some come; pragmatists hear "your vote will get heavier" and some flow over; opponents shout "vote buying" and spread the formula for free; in a field of identical politicians the underdog becomes the only one with unique mathematics. Against the mechanism itself among those who came — 12–15 %. Of the real underdogs of recent years this is one of the cleanest cases where the protocol could have turned a "loud third" into a candidate with a trajectory to victory. Important: Pratt's real campaign was not the protocol; this is a counterfactual.

## 4. What incumbent candidates will say and how it hits the underdog

Nobody will tell the truth ("we lose our base and our budgets"); they will strike with masks: "vote buying" (the most frequent and strongest strike); "a blow to the poor — they'll be bought out, the rich will stay to vote"; "the vote is sacred"; "it destroys legitimacy — the winner is elected not by the people but by those who didn't sell their vote"; "fiscal irresponsibility — where's the money from"; "a dangerous precedent — tomorrow we'll pay for anything". By type: a systemic Democrat — "vote buying" + "a blow to the poor and minorities"; a systemic Republican — "destruction of civic duty" + "handing out money"; a populist — "a scheme for the rich" or "utopia"; an incumbent — "dangerous for stability and legitimacy".

For an underdog this barely hurts and often helps: **the Streisand effect** (the louder "vote buying", the more people hear "$1,000 in your account or a x3 vote" for the first time); **nothing to defend** (a systemic politician fears the label, the underdog is already "strange", the label strengthens him); **the attack reveals interest** ("if they're this furious, the system really bothers them"); **concrete beats abstract** ("vote buying" is an abstraction, "$1,000 in your account" is concrete). Where the attack does hit: if the underdog starts justifying himself and explaining at length; if he lets the frame "he wants to buy the election" be hung instead of "he gives a choice"; if he does not hold the formula. The right reaction is not justification but repetition: "they are against because they lose control; for you — money or a stronger vote; for them — neither is needed". Underdog support of ~48 % and "against the idea 10–15 %" do not contradict each other: the remaining votes are ordinary competition between candidates, habits, brands, local factors, not an army of the protocol's enemies.

## 5. Trump's fourteen days

Scenario: the topic runs for 14 days straight from an account with an audience of over a million. Days 1–3 — ignoring or mockery ("another strange idea", "technocrats"), the topic lives in a niche audience. Days 4–7 — if people repeat the fork, the campaign and the media begin to count: it hits "money for the people", it hits "the system is rotten through", it can be stolen. For Trump what matters is not the content of the theory but whether the topic takes attention and hits the elites. Days 8–14 — the fork:

| Scenario | Probability | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| A — interception | 55–65 % | Not the pure protocol but his own version: "I'll give people a choice", "the elites fear the strong vote of real citizens", "let those who don't care take the money and not get in the way" — an anti-elite weapon in his rhetoric |
| B — attack | 25–30 % | If opponents took the topic first or it sounds like "weaken the base": "vote buying", "bribing America", "breaking the elections" |
| C — hybrid | 10–15 % | Mockery, then a reversal at the sight of support figures: "I said this from day one" |

Systemically over 14 days: the topic leaves the chat and enters the political air; the media argue no longer "what is it" but "for or against"; both camps try to appropriate a piece of the formula — one "money for the people", the other "the vote of real citizens weighs more" (059d.6: Graham); Trump reacts not to the repository but to reach and polling. For the protocol both outcomes are useful: the system's silence ends, the formula enters the mass dispute. What is inevitable is not Trump's love for the idea but the topic's being drawn into the big game: with enough noise, ignoring costs more than reacting.

## 6. Is there another single idea with such odds

To work like the protocol an idea must be understood in three seconds, give personal benefit to a large group, hit the interests of a narrow layer rather than the whole people, and be repeatable in one phrase. The nearest analogues: Andrew Yang's UBI ("$1,000 a month" — clear, but expensive, easy to attack as a "handout", no second side of the fork); "make the rich pay" (a comprehensible enemy, but it splits society and stays a slogan without a mechanism); radical simplification of the state (anti-system, but the benefit is less concrete than $1,000 in the account); legalisation with money for the city (not in every city, quickly mainstream); "take back control" through recall, term limits, direct budget votes (anti-elite, but without instant personal benefit). Ordinary one-idea platforms have one benefit for one group; the protocol has two at once — money for those who don't need participation and weight for those who do. The full matrix — 036, 059d.2.

## 7. Weak point of the case

All "with the protocol" percentages are model projections under the architect's pressure (037b.5); the 70–85 % estimate of an underdog's victory rests on the assumption "support for the carrier ≈ support for the idea", which ignores a candidate's personal minuses and local competition. Pratt and Chicago are counterfactuals, not observations. The federal contour is closed by §597 (059b.8); everything described is municipal. And Trump's 14 days presuppose an account with a million-strong audience, which the protocol does not have. 🟡
