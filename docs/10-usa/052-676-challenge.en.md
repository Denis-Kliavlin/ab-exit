### 90.1. Purpose of the section

This section records the operational plan of a viral launch campaign for AB-EXIT through a social-media challenge. The strategy is developed with regard to the current cultural trends of 2025–2026 and an empirical analysis of ten successful viral campaigns of the last 20 years.

The challenge is positioned as **the main tool for moving AB-EXIT from a theoretical concept into mass public consciousness**. Without a viral campaign the concept will remain in academic and political niches. With a viral campaign — it becomes a national topic of discussion.

### 90.2. Seven mechanisms of virality

From Wharton research (Jonah Berger) and analysis of specific cases:

1. **Social currency.** Participation makes a person look better in the eyes of others.
2. **Nominate mechanics with a deadline.** Naming specific people with a 24-hour window — a structure integral to all successful social challenges.
3. **Calling out — refusing is almost impossible.** When a friend nominates you publicly, the social price of refusal is higher than the price of participation.
4. **A simple action.** Can be done in 30–90 seconds.
5. **Video format.** Easy to record and share on any platform.
6. **A combination of humour and seriousness.** Fun to do, but with an important cause behind it.
7. **Celebrity involvement.** Bill Gates, Oprah, LeBron took part in the Ice Bucket Challenge — this critically boosted virality.

### 90.3. Analysis of 10 successful challenges

— **ALS Ice Bucket Challenge** (2014) — $220M in 6 weeks; a simple action + an important cause + nominate. — **Mannequin Challenge** (2016) — groups of people frozen mid-action. — **Renegade Dance** (2019) — Charli D'Amelio + music + 15 seconds of dance. — **10 Year Challenge** (2019) — "then/now" photos, zero cost. — **Bottle Cap Challenge** (2019) — launched by Jason Statham, a physical skill. — **Kiki Challenge** (2018) — dancing beside a car to Drake. — **Don't Rush Challenge** (2020) — a transformation of look. — **Movember** — growing a moustache for prostate cancer, a serious cause. — **It Gets Better** (2010+) — Dan Savage, video messages. — **MrBeast "Last To Leave"** (2020+) — endurance, a prize, elimination.

The common pattern: visually simple mechanics plus social connection through nominate plus a visible signal of participation.

### 90.4. The structural superiority of the AB-EXIT challenge over previous political challenges

All political challenges of the last 10 years (Black Squares for BLM, #MarchForOurLives, Climate Strikes, #StopTheSteal) **intensified partisan polarisation** rather than reducing it. Each became tribal — one side takes part, the other rejects.

The AB-EXIT challenge is potentially the first political challenge that **REDUCES polarisation**, because:

— The question is not "whom to vote for" but "money or the vote". That is not a party question. — The answer is not determined by party affiliation. — Trump supporters and Biden voters may end up on either side of the choice. — The discussion does not reduce to "are you on my side or against".

This is a qualitatively new positioning of a political challenge in the USA.

### 90.5. The sum $676 for the USA — the link to the viral trend "6-7"

**$676 as a mathematical quantity.** By the AB-EXIT formula (D = M × 1.5 × 1 %) for a US median wage in the range $42,000–$52,000 the computed dividend is $640–$780. The figure $676 lies in this range and is valid for current US economic data.

**$676 as a viral hook through the "6-7" trend.** In 2025 "six seven" (6-7) became Dictionary.com's Word of the Year. Origin — Skrilla's 2024 rap song "Doot Doot (6 7)", virality through LaMelo Ball basketball videos (height 6'7"). On TikTok more than 2 million posts with the hashtag #67. In-N-Out Burger removed number 67 from its ordering system because teenagers came to the restaurants en masse to hear "six-seven". South Park is making a parody episode.

**The "6-7" gesture = a gesture of choosing between two options.** It accompanies the phrase: the hands move up and down, showing the weighing of two options. This is **literally the gesture of AB-EXIT's mechanics** — money or the vote, a choice between two.

**A structural coincidence, not a forced one.** The sum $676 contains the digit pattern "6-7-6", which Gen Alpha and Gen Z recognise instantly. The "6-7" gesture already means "I'm weighing two options" — exactly what AB-EXIT offers. This semiotic coincidence works at the subconscious level of millions of people already inside the trend.

**A critical time window.** The "6-7" trend is a narrow time window. Dictionary.com Word of the Year 2025 is the peak. In 6–12 months the trend will start to fade. The launch of the $676 Challenge must be tied to the moment while the trend is alive, i.e. within the next 3–6 months from the recording of this strategy.

### 90.6. Three legitimate positions of participants

In the challenge three legitimate positions arise empirically, and each has a full identity and arguments.

**Position 1 — "I vote with weight ×2".** The electoral elite. A citizen who refused $676 for a strengthened vote. Their identity: "I make a real difference. I don't trade citizenship for money."

**Position 2 — "I take the money as protest".** Politically active citizens critical of the system. Their identity: "I'm the first honest citizen in this deal. The state promised for forty years and didn't deliver. Now it delivers — I take."

**Position 3 — "I take the money because I don't care".** Functionally apolitical citizens. This is the structural majority, which for the first time gets a public voice. Their identity: "I don't need a political role. Pay — I'll take it. I work, I raise kids. Politics isn't my life."

The third position is critically important. This is the category for which AB-EXIT structurally exists (section 80 — working with human nature). A video with an honest "I don't care" is a living illustration of the concept, which these people themselves publicly confirm.

**For those taking as protest:**

### 90.8. The prank format as the key amplifier of virality

The self-recording format has a virality ceiling because of prepared answers. A person filming himself has time to think through a politically acceptable position.

The prank format (an interview without warning) gives qualitatively different mechanics:

— **Real surprise on the face.** The most valuable content on social media. — **A real struggle of values in real time.** Not prepared rhetoric. — **Natural answers.** Including "I don't care, give me the money" — the most honest ones. — **Drama with a friend or a stranger** adds an emotional layer.

This format collects all three positions naturally, because there is no time to politically adjust the answer.

### 90.9. The final video structure, 90–120 seconds

**1. Opening (5 sec):** the winking Franklin as intro. The pranker on the street, in a café or at home. "I have $676 in my hand. Six. Seven. Six." The "6-7" gesture.

**2. Explaining the premise (15–20 sec):** "Imagine AB-EXIT has already been adopted as law. The state offers a choice before every election: take $676 and skip this cycle, or refuse and vote with a vote ×2 (part of the electorate has exited). In 4 years you choose again. The money is from the state, not from me. Voting is completely normal, legal."

**3. The pranker's bet (5 sec):** "I'm playing too. If you choose the money — I give $10 on top. If you choose the vote — you give me $10. Real money, to make it serious."

**4. Countdown (15 sec):** "You have 15 seconds. 15... 14... 13... six... seven..." (somewhere in the countdown the "6-7" gesture).

**5. Decision and the $10 exchange (5 sec):** the choice gesture, the physical hand-over of real $10.

**6. Defending the choice (15–20 sec):** natural dialogue. Why so? And what matters more?

**7. The final question (10 sec):** "And the last question regardless of your choice — would you want AB-EXIT actually adopted as law?" A simple answer "yes/no/on these conditions".

**8. Explaining the position (10 sec):** "I want/don't want because..."

**9. Nomination (5 sec):** "Now you prank three friends. 24 hours. #676Challenge #67."

Total 90–120 seconds.

### 90.10. The hybrid money model

— **The main $676 — symbolic.** An envelope marked "$676 (AB-EXIT compensation)" or 8 printed bills in frame. Legally clean, not a hand-over of cash for a promise.

— **The $10 bet — real.** In both directions. Preserves the dramatic moment. $10 is too small a sum for the suspicion "they gave it back afterwards", but enough for the choice to be real.

— **Conceptual accuracy.** This exactly reflects AB-EXIT: the main sum from the state (in the real system — a bank transfer), and the personal price of the choice — the real $10 bet between friends.

### 90.11. The winking Franklin as the visual brand

Benjamin Franklin on the hundred-dollar bill gives four associations at once: money, elections and democracy, common sense (his aphorisms), historical legitimacy (a founding father).

Animation: the standard Franklin portrait from the banknote, a serious face, and suddenly — a wink or a smile. A variant where he shows a thumbs-up or holds a bill.

— Branding of the challenge (a 2–3-second animation at the start of every video). — Humour + seriousness (a joke + a reference to American history). — Cross-partisan appeal (Franklin is perceived as neither left nor right). — A merchandise opportunity (T-shirts, stickers, AR filters).

**Localisation for other countries.** Each country gets its own national portrait from a banknote. Russia — the corresponding historical figures. The United Kingdom — the royal family. Poland, Moldova — national symbols.

### 90.12. The cross-partisan structure of the question

This is the unique structural advantage of the AB-EXIT challenge over all political challenges of the last 10 years.

All previous political challenges (Black Squares, March For Our Lives, climate strikes, Stop The Steal) intensified polarisation because the question was partisan — "for our side or against".

The AB-EXIT challenge asks a question that **cannot be translated into party conflict**: — Not "whom will you vote for". — Not "what is your political identity". — But "will you take the money or vote".

A Trump supporter and a Biden voter discover that they may have **a shared experience** — both are ready to take the money, or both are ready to vote. Party identity does not predict the choice. This destroys the main mechanism of polarisation.

The goal of mass virality is reinforced by this: — Videos are shared by people from all camps. — Cross-partisan trends scale faster than partisan ones. — Comments become substantive, not tribal. — Social-media algorithms promote cross-partisan content. — It satisfies society's enormous hunger for cross-partisan content after 20 years of polarisation.

### 90.13. The final question "do you want AB-EXIT?"

This question turns every video from an individual drama into **a public referendum** on social media.

**The effect at the video level:** the position becomes complete. Not only "what I chose" but "how I feel about the reform as a whole".

**The effect at the virality level:** the challenge becomes a living mass referendum. Thousands of videos are thousands of public votes for or against the reform. Journalists can write "According to the $676 Challenge on TikTok, X % of participants support the adoption of AB-EXIT".

**The effect at the political level:** creates a public mandate that is hard to ignore. Especially across the partisan divide — Trump supporters and Biden voters simultaneously say "I want AB-EXIT". Any politician-bearer gets a ready base of support.

**The effect at the level of the individual participant:** forces a transition from personal choice to civic position. These are two different levels of thinking. A mass civic awakening through a simple final question.

### 90.14. Scenarios of refusal to participate

Some people will refuse to take part, and that is normal. Five types of refusal, each giving valid content:

1. **"I don't want to be in a video"** — a normal privacy reaction. Content — a short refusal clip.
2. **"This is a provocation"** — suspicion of a hidden purpose. Content — a calm explanation that the concept is public.
3. **"Why should I play"** — a refusal of the format, not the concept. Content — switching to a serious conversation.
4. **"Explain in more detail first"** — a wish to understand before deciding. Golden content — an educational video.
5. **"I'm busy"** — a simple excuse. Content — confirmation that people have no time for politics.

Every refusal is part of the virality if presented right.

### 90.15. Surprises among friends and relatives as the dramatic core

The friend model has a unique advantage I initially underestimated: **the uncertainty of the answers of close people**.

Most people have never discussed with their loved ones in such a concrete form: will dad take $676? What will mum do? Will the Democrat husband refuse the money for the sake of voting?

The surprises will be massive: — A patriotic veteran takes the money. The grandson is shocked. — A Democrat activist takes the money. Her friends are shocked. — An apolitical working guy refuses the money and goes to vote. Everyone is shocked. — A libertarian goes for the double vote.

Every surprise creates two new prankers: the pranker himself, who now wants to test others, and the pranked one, who wants to test third parties. The reproductive rate jumps to 2.5–3.

The AB-EXIT challenge is not a ritual but an **investigative** challenge. Every video is a small sociological study. This is a different category of content, and it works by different rules of virality.

Every video gives four levels of self-knowledge at once: — The pranked person learns something new about himself. — The pranker learns something new about a close person. — The video's viewers learn something new about society. — The pranked person's family and friends learn something new about a person they thought they knew.

**The hybrid strategy (recommended):**

### 90.17. Legal positioning for the USA

Critically important is correct wording to avoid legal risks.

Federal law (52 USC 10307) prohibits offering money for voting or not voting in federal elections. This is a serious norm.

**Correct wording** — a hypothetical discussion protected by the First Amendment:

— "Imagine AB-EXIT has already been adopted as law. What would you choose?"

**Incorrect wording** — potentially illegal:

— "Promise not to vote for $676" (this is buying a promise, close to bribery).

The exact wording must include: — Money from the state (not from the pranker personally). — A hypothetical scenario (AB-EXIT as law). — Reversibility (the next cycle the choice is made anew). — Voting is completely normal and legal when "the vote" is chosen.

Far cheaper than traditional political campaigns and far more effective than AI-generated clips with real faces.
