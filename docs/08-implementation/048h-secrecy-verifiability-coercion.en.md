## 1. What the German court decided — and what it did not

The court found the use of Nedap machines in the 2005 Bundestag election incompatible with the Basic Law and derived the principle of the public nature of elections: the essential steps of voting and of determining the result must be examinable by the citizen reliably and without specialist knowledge. Two details usually lost in retelling: the court did not ban electronic voting as such — it required verifiability; and it did not annul the 2005 election, judging the possible effect of the errors on the Bundestag's composition marginal. The Netherlands decertified the same machines on 1 October 2007 after the campaign "We do not trust voting computers" and from 2008 returned to paper and a red pencil. Estonia went the other way: in 2023, 51 % of the votes in the parliamentary election were cast online.

The architect's correction: the principle "verifiable by anyone without knowledge" is a legal fiction for paper too. A blind person does not see the tick, an illiterate one does not read the ballot, and both must trust a helper. Any system is a balance of three requirements that cannot all be met at once: **verifiability, secrecy, accessibility**. The dispute is not about whether there is trust but about *to whom* and *under what audit* it is handed.

## 2. What the protocol does to existing elections

At the first stage — it breaks nothing. Polling stations, ballots, commissions and the secret ballot for those who stay to vote are preserved; the protocol works as a filter before the election, taking the uninterested off the stations. This is fundamental, and it diverges from the wording in 048f.3.3 ("the remaining core votes openly, like a shareholders' meeting"): open voting by those who remain is a possible distant consequence, not a norm of the protocol. The secrecy of the choice of candidate stays; only the A or B status may be public (033c.4).

Hence the honest boundary of double entry (048f). It closes: painting turnout, dead souls, substitution of the A and B shares — everything that runs into the identity [dividend budget] = [number of B] × D. It does not close: the content of the ballots of those who stayed. The architect's formula is more precise than the model's: exit for money **shrinks the field for manipulation but does not make the count exact**. The field shrinks substantially: lists of non-voters are the main resource for ballot stuffing, and precisely they disappear when non-attendance becomes a recorded transaction.

## 3. Why a vote cannot have a tracking number

The architect's analogy: "I ordered goods, they travelled three days and did not arrive — I have a receipt and a tracking number; why not the same with a vote?" Because a receipt proving *how* you voted proves it not only to you. With it a vote can be sold with a guarantee and an account can be demanded: by a boss, a husband, a local strongman. That is why the law gives no right to *voluntarily* disclose one's ballot: voluntariness under pressure cannot be told from coercion, and one permitted signed ballot cancels the protection for all. This property is called receipt-freeness.

The hybrid the architect proposed ("whoever wants verifies by phone, whoever doesn't uses a ballot") exists as a class of end-to-end verifiable systems (Helios, Scantegrity, STAR-Vote): the voter gets a receipt proving that his vote is *included in the count* but not showing *for whom* it is. The model brushed them aside too quickly; their real weakness is different — only a handful understand the verification, and a dispute over a mismatch is won by whoever holds the server. Estonia solves coercion another way: an online vote can be re-cast any number of times, the last one counts, and a paper ballot overrides the electronic one — someone who voted under supervision can later re-vote alone.

## 4. Coercion: how much of it there really is

The thesis "in developed countries nobody has been killed for a vote for a long time, pressure is below the margin of error, secrecy has become a relic" is wrong in two places. First, the data: in the US in 2015 roughly one worker in four received political contact from an employer, and 46 % of managers reported that their firms mobilise employees; OSCE observers in individual missions recorded family and group voting at about a quarter of the polling stations visited; in 2015 an English election court voided the Tower Hamlets mayoral election for bribery and undue spiritual influence. Second, the logic: a low level of coercion is a *consequence* of secrecy, not proof that it is unnecessary. Removing the lock because there have been no burglaries for a long time is survivorship bias. People with placards at a rally refute nothing: public support for someone you like and a provable vote against someone you depend on are different situations.

## 5. What the protocol really handles: an exit for the coerced and a floor price for a vote

The architect's reply to the scenario "the boss demands proof": "then I'll just take the money". This is a new property the old system lacks. Today a dependent voter has two options: obey or take the risk. The protocol gives a third — a lawful, economically explicable and socially safe exit: "I am not against you, I just need the payment more" (056d.4).

The economics of coercion change in four places:

- **Coercion stops being free.** The order "go and vote as required" cost the worker half an hour; the order "give up D and vote as required" costs him D. The instigator has to compensate D and add a premium for the risk of complicity.
- **Scale makes it visible.** A thousand workers is a thousand D plus premiums; such sums cannot be handed out in envelopes or passed off as a "bonus" without a trace for tax and financial oversight.
- **Profitability goes negative.** When the price of one coerced vote is a multiple of D, the seat being fought over stops repaying the purchase; that is the floor price of a vote (059d.5).
- **Collective refusal.** If the boss has frightened everyone, everyone will take the money; he cannot fire them all — production stops.

The architect's observation about the mafia: it is strong in poor neighbourhoods, that is, exactly where D weighs most (019b, 040c.1.5). In the old system the strongman *gave* a hand-out and looked like a benefactor; by demanding that people give up D for his candidate he *takes* a large sum from the poor and turns from patron into robber. The district can be held only by paying each person his D from one's own pocket — and that business model was built on taking, not on handing out.

## 6. The mirror attack: forced exit — compared with what exists now

The objection. Everything in §5 is true of coercion *to vote a certain way*, but the protocol opens the reverse move — coercion *to exit*. Status B is provable by a bank statement, it costs the instigator nothing (the state pays), and it looks not like coercion but like "everyone takes it, take it too". It benefits an employer with a disloyal workforce, a domestic tyrant (039b.3.3), a local strongman.

The architect's correction: "you compared with the ideal again. Bribery and intimidation are just as possible now, and so is a phone photo of the ballot in the booth. The protocol at the very least turns a +1 vote into zero. And forcing a worker to lose money is much harder than now, when it is free." The correction is accepted. The first version of this section called the attack "the most serious unclosed spot", having compared the protocol with a receipt-free abstention — and no such abstention exists. The comparison with the system in force:

| | Now | Under the protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Coercion to vote for the required candidate | Free for the instigator; verified by a ballot photo, home and postal voting, electronic voting under supervision; the result is +1 for the instigator, and if the person was opposed — a swing of two votes | Costs D plus a risk premium; visible at scale; negative return (§5) |
| Coercion not to vote | Already possible, free and verifiable: turnout is visible in the rolls, and in many countries participation history is public data; the coerced person gets nothing | Possible and verifiable by a statement; the coerced person gets D; the result is zero instead of a vote against |
| What the instigator gets | A full vote in his favour | At best, someone else's vote removed |

Two conclusions. First: forced abstention is not a new capability, it exists and is verifiable today; the protocol adds to it only a payment to the victim. Second: the instigator is forced to move from a strong attack to a weak one — from "+1 for me" to "0 for him". On an indifferent worker the effect is nil; on an opponent — half the former swing.

The honest remainder. In votes the attack is weaker; in shares — not always: in a shrunken pool each removed opponent weighs 1/(N − M). With half exiting this equals today's swing from a coerced vote (2/N); with 70 % exiting — more. What limits the attack is something else: those who can be coerced are mostly the dependent, and the dependent, by the protocol's logic, take the money voluntarily anyway; the circle "dependent, yet would refuse D on principle" is narrow, and the instigator rarely knows by name who is against — the order "we all take it" removes his own supporters from the election too. Countermeasures narrow the circle further: a window for changing the decision on the Estonian model (chose B in front of the boss, switched to A before the deadline, the last choice counts), secrecy of status as a right, crediting only to a personal account with a ban on deductions, a criminal norm on pressure over the choice of status. The size of that circle is a question for a pilot.

## 7. "Dictatorships are inevitable": a comparison with what works now

The architect's thesis after the broadcast: the count is opaque, public-sector workers will outvote, the majority will endure, the only way out is through hunger and empty shelves. The first version of this section objected: double entry protects an *adopted* protocol, and a regime that controls the count also controls the treasury. The architect's correction: "so let them control the fact that 80–90 % took the money. Autocracies want to show that elections are lawful — but if 90 % did not come, how do they paint turnout? For an autocracy any current method is barely effective, and the protocol too, but at least somehow." The correction is accepted: the objection measured the protocol against a tool that does not exist.

| Method | What it yields under autocracy |
|---|---|
| Boycott | Dries up turnout and eases the dependent electorate's victory (056d.1) |
| Voting for the "lesser evil" | Legitimises; the vote goes to those who will vote as told |
| Spoiling the ballot | The percentage is counted from valid ballots |
| Observation and a parallel count | Records but does not stop: in Belarus in 2020 forgery was proven at almost 300 stations, the result did not change (048i.2) |
| The protocol as a demand | A non-political question nobody can be jailed for (048e.4); the fork "forbid — take money from your own core, allow — lose turnout" (056d.3) |
| The protocol, if adopted | Turnout cannot be painted: 80–90 % received money and a bank notification, the total paid is a budget line; what remains to paint is only the content of a small pool's ballots |

What is left to the regime. Not adopt — and explain why people may not receive their money. Adopt a fake with commissions and delays (042, 059e.3). Or adopt and present the mass exit as trust ("the people are so calm they took the dividend") while painting the content of the remaining ballots. The third is the most likely, and the link with 048i works against it: the pool has shrunk to the motivated, and the motivated readily present tokens. The 2020 parallel count covered less than a tenth of those who voted; in a pool of the 10–20 % most motivated the coverage can become a majority, and the lower bounds will begin to overlap the official figures. After that the regime is left with naked force — and that is the subject of 048d.

The elite channel is not an alternative but a continuation. Autocrats most often lose power not to hunger or uprising but to their own entourage: by Svolik's data on dictatorships of 1946–2008, removal by insiders is the most frequent cause of exit, well ahead of popular uprisings. The first version of this section concluded from this that the working channel is the elite's calculation "rather than" the electoral one. The architect's correction: "it is easier for the elites if it is plainly visible that the autocrat is naked and has no support; they will certainly get the secret protocols of who took the money." The correction is accepted: this is one chain, not two. An autocrat's main asset before his own entourage is the appearance of mass support; the literature on electoral autocracies explains inflated results exactly so — not for the sake of winning but as a signal of invincibility that keeps insiders from defecting. Each of them does not know what the others think, and believes the figure. The protocol takes that signal away: the number who took the money is not propaganda but a line in the treasury and the banking system, and the first to see it are precisely those who sit on those flows. Turnout can still be painted for television; it cannot be painted for the finance minister who signed the payments. Then 048d.6 takes over: when the entourage knows the king is naked, a golden parachute becomes more rational than loyalty.

Both channels are described in the repository as models without precedent — and that remains so; adoption of the protocol by an autocracy is still the narrowest spot (057b.5, 056d.8), but a narrow spot is not the same as "does not work".

## 8. Weak point of the section

In their first version §6 and §7 repeated the error analysed in 037b.4: comparison with the ideal instead of comparison with the system in force — in §6 with a receipt-free abstention, in §7 with a tool that would work under a consolidated autocracy. After the correction what remains is not a hole but a limitation: the relative weight of a removed vote in a shrunken pool and the unmeasured size of the circle "dependent, yet motivated". The estimates in §4 come from different countries and years and do not add up to an overall share of the coerced; they also cut both ways — they confirm that coercion exists now, that is, that the comparison must be made with it rather than with its absence. And §7 rests on Svolik's general conclusion without verification of the exact figures. 🟡
