## 11b.1. The theory: Seligman, 1967

Learned helplessness (Martin Seligman) is a discovery on animals, confirmed on humans: an organism that has repeatedly experienced **uncontrollability** (action has no effect on outcome) stops acting — even when control returns. Dogs that received shocks regardless of their behaviour later did not jump the barrier beyond which there was no shock: they lay down and whimpered. They had not learned "the barrier is insurmountable" — they had learned "my actions change nothing".

The three deficits of learned helplessness: **motivational** (does not initiate action), **cognitive** (does not see the link "action → result" even where it has appeared), **emotional** (apathy, cynicism, a depressive background). And the key point from the later work: the cure is not persuasion but **the experience of contingency** (mastery): a guaranteed link "did → received", repeated enough times to overwrite what was learned.

## 11b.2. Elections as a machine for producing learned helplessness

Voting in a large democracy is almost laboratory uncontrollability: the action (an hour's journey, a queue, a tick) and the result (the same mayor; a policy that has not changed one iota for you personally) are linked in no observable way. The latency of feedback is years; the contribution of one vote is a statistical zero; the outcome is indistinguishable from inaction. The system meanwhile demands that the action be repeated and shames refusal — a perfect protocol for producing helplessness by Seligman: non-contingency + compulsion to repeat + denial of the subject's experience.

Two subtypes from the repository are two stages of one syndrome. **The apathetic** (§19) — second-generation helplessness: never tried himself, learned it by observation (parents went — nothing changed). **The burned** (§19, §28) — first-generation, classical: tried, and more than once. The young Korean (§28): came at 18 — 200 pensioners in the queue, the same mayor; came at 22 — the same; at 25 stopped. He is not indifferent — he has **learned precisely** the lesson he was taught: you cannot outvote an army of pensioners. Chichvarkin (§19, §21) — a burned man of the upper floor: motivated, rich, angry — and blocked by the same conclusion. The gap in §22 ("I want a third party — 62 %; I will vote — 15 %") is learned helplessness measured by sociology: the cognitive deficit in pure form, disbelief in the link before the action begins.

Hence why traditional campaigns of "go and vote, your vote matters" **aggravate** the syndrome: they demand the repetition of a non-contingent action (one more session of shock) and simultaneously gaslight the experience ("it only seems to you that nothing changes"). The burned man hears: "you jumped badly — jump again". Seligman showed that this deepens passivity.

## 11b.3. The mechanism of therapy: five circuits

**Circuit 1 — the first guaranteed experience of contingency.** Button B is the first political action in a person's life with one-hundred-per-cent, fast, personal feedback: pressed → the money arrived → in the app, in days rather than years, to you rather than to "the country". By Seligman this is exactly a mastery experience: the link "my action → my result" in a domain where it never existed. Importantly, it is the fact of the link that is therapeutic, not its content — helplessness is cured not by persuasion that "the system is good" but by the experience that **in this system my actions have consequences**.

**Circuit 2 — choice as the restoration of agency.** Even the unchosen option changes the psychology of the chosen one: whoever stayed to vote while button B existed stayed **by choice, not by hopelessness** — and whoever left did so by an act, not by absence. Seligman's dog was helpless because it had no lever; AB-EXIT puts a lever into the cage — and its mere presence changes the state before any pressing. (Link to §11.5: the symbolic act of resolution.)

**Circuit 3 — recognition instead of gaslighting (for the burned).** For the first time the system tells the burned man not "you are wrong, go again" but: **"You were right. Your vote really had no price — here it is, we are paying it."** This inverts the attribution of helplessness: his experience is not denied but validated and compensated. Per §11.4 — a state gesture of recognition; in therapeutic terms — the removal of the cognitive dissonance that sustains cynicism. Trust in the system begins not with promises but with the acknowledgement of a debt.

**Circuit 4 — a second attempt with an amplified effect.** The burned man's helplessness rests on the learned constant "I will be outvoted anyway". AB-EXIT physically changes it: the exit of part of the electorate (in Korea, §28 — the buy-out of part of the "army of pensioners" by button B) multiplies the weight of the returning voter by 1/(1 − exit). The Korean thirty-year-old returns not to the cage in which he was beaten: the barrier really is lower, his jump really is longer — and §22 shows the mechanics of the conversion "I want → I will vote": personal gain does not depend on a party's victory, contingency is guaranteed under any outcome. The return from a new position (§11.6) is the generalisation of a small victory: having gained control in the domain of money, a person re-tests control in the domain of the vote.

**Circuit 5 — a change of attribution and constant feedback.** Learned helplessness lives on the attribution "permanent, global, about me" ("nothing can ever be changed, and I am nothing"). The cyclicality of AB-EXIT rewrites it as "temporary and specific": not "I cannot influence" but "this cycle I chose the money; next cycle I will choose afresh". And the dividend derived from the median (the thermostat, §13.9) gives the voter what he never had — **a regular personal indicator of the quality of governance**: a number that grows or stagnates in his app. Feedback from elections (once in years, unreadable) becomes annual and personal — an environment in which helplessness is not reproduced, because uncontrollability has stopped being true.

## 11b.4. The honest boundary

AB-EXIT is environmental therapy, not psychotherapy: it removes the **objective** non-contingency that produced the syndrome, but for some of the deeply burned, distrust will outlive the first and second payment (a residue of 3–7 % refusers, 08_045.4). The order of treatment is correct by Seligman: first real control, then belief in it — not the reverse. All previous reforms offered belief without control; AB-EXIT offers control from which belief grows by itself, at the speed of a bank notification.
