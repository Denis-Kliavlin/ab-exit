# 40ag. Charles Tilly: *Democracy* — Five Arguments: the Network That Does Not Want to Govern, Choice B as a Decision, "Understands" Is Not a Category, the Trap for Coercion

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites

**Source:** the architect's assignment of 12 October 2026: "find five more political scientists not in the repo" — and "one at a time". The fifteenth is Tilly: *Democracy* (2007), *Trust and Rule* (2005), *Coercion, Capital, and European States* (1990). A reconstruction from the texts; the architect's moves on all five arguments are recorded as moves.

---

*How to read. Tilly defines democracy not by procedure but by the relations of state and citizens: broad, equal, protected, mutually binding consultation. His main mechanism is trust networks integrated into public politics. The repository described "the core" as such a network; the question to Tilly is whether that is trust or dependence.*

## 1. Democratisation is the integration of trust networks into public politics

**The argument.** People move their trust networks — family, trade, community, mutual-aid fund — from the private sphere into the public: they entrust the state with their long-term risks — pension, children, the courts. De-democratisation is the reverse: networks leave politics for the patron, for the mafia, and the future is insured through "one's own" rather than through the state.

**The strike.** "The core" in the repository is a trust network integrated into politics through dependence on the budget (039 §88.9, clerks and budget employees; 036e, Goldin — "the state as husband"). By Tilly that is democratisation: people entrusted the state with their risks. The protocol pays them to leave politics — it pulls the trust network back into the private: de-democratisation by his definition.

**The answer.** Tilly distinguished two ways of integration: a network can enter politics *as a participant* — bargaining with the state as an equal — or *as a hostage*, when its risks are already with the state and it votes so that they are not taken away (1f §5). "The core" is the second: it did not entrust the state with its future, it is deprived of the possibility of taking it back, and the vote is the price of not losing the allowance. That is not trust in Tilly's sense but dependence, and for Tilly himself such integration — "through coercion" — leads to de-democratisation. The protocol does not pull the network out of politics — it changes the condition of integration from hostage to contract: the payment is the state's first obligation to "the meek" that does not depend on how she voted (048m Arts. 4, 16), and that can be taken in secret. That is "mutually binding consultation" in its shortest form: the state owes her regardless of her vote.

**Tilly's continuation and the architect's move.** Tilly would ask: what happens to the trust network when its members take the payment en masse — has it left the public for the private? The architect: "and handed the right to govern to others through the ×2 vote? What kind of network is it that does not want to govern politics?" The question turns against Tilly's premise. A network that takes the payment en masse and hands the decision to others with a ×2 share is a network that did not want to govern politics before either: it was brought there. Tilly's trust network integrates into politics because it has something to protect in the public; this one protected only the allowance, and decisions were of no use to it — the payment shows it: offered a choice, it chose the money, not the wheel. There is no "pulling the network out of politics" — what is pulled out is the fiction of its participation. And the network that stayed at the polls with a ×2 share is the one that wants to govern, and Tilly gets exactly what he described: an integrated trust network, for the first time distinguishable from a network of dependence. Before the protocol both looked the same — both voted; the protocol is the first instrument that separates them, and separates them by their own choice.

**Residual.** Tilly measured democracy by the breadth of consultation, and "a consultation one can decline for money" is a question for him. The answer: declining does not narrow the consultation — "the taker" is in the counter, with a vote in the referendum on the rule and a public obligation of the state towards him; what narrows is not the circle but the fiction. But that the network which took the payment *did not want* to govern rather than *could not* (poverty, 040p §1) is a threshold the pilot will measure.

## 2. The four dimensions of democracy

**The argument.** Democracy is measured not as "yes/no" but along four axes of state–citizen relations: *breadth* (how many people the consultation covers), *equality* (whether the state treats categories alike), *protection* (from the arbitrariness of the state itself), *mutually binding obligation* (the state must deliver what it promised). Democratisation is movement along all four; a retreat on any is de-democratisation.

**The strike.** On the first axis the protocol retreats: the breadth of consultation falls when two-thirds take the payment; for Tilly breadth is measured by coverage, not voluntariness.

**The answer along the four axes.** *Breadth:* coverage does not fall — for the first time everyone is covered: those who voted, took and abstained are counted (048m Art. 10), and "the taker" keeps his vote in the referendum on the rule; today two-thirds are covered by nothing — neither consultation nor count. *Equality:* the protocol is the first rule that treats alike categories unequal in fact today — the brought-in and the one who came by himself: one price, one ballot (040u item 2). *Protection:* the secret payment, coercion criminal (Arts. 9, 11) — protection from arbitrariness where there was none: at the polls under the boss (040af item 5). *Mutually binding obligation:* the payment is an unconditional obligation of the budget, like a tax for the citizen (048m Art. 8) — the state's first obligation that does not depend on the vote. Three axes up; the first not down but measured for the first time.

**Tilly's continuation and the architect's move.** Tilly would specify: breadth is about participation in the *decision*, and "the taker" does not take part in this cycle's decision; counted — yes, consulted — no. The architect: "that was their decision. They do not want to take this decision — for their own reasons, and perhaps they knew they could not take it adequately." Choice B is a decision too, and the first truly one's own: "the taker" took part in the consultation exactly once and fully — he decided that he will not take this decision, knowing about himself what nobody else knows. That is not a refusal to participate but participation with the most honest answer possible — "not me". Today that answer is not provided for: a person is either brought and answers "as told" (040ad item 1, minus one), or stays home and others answer for him. Breadth under the protocol does not narrow — for the first time it includes the answer "I abstain consciously, and it cost me a choice", which Tilly's consultation lacked because it had no price. Tilly wanted the state to ask everyone; the protocol is the first rule under which the answer "do not ask me this time" counts as an answer, not an absence.

**Residual.** None.

## 3. Categorical inequality

**The argument** (*Durable Inequality*, 1998; *Democracy*, ch. 5). Democracy is possible only if public politics is insulated from categorical inequality: sex, ethnicity, class, place in the employer's hierarchy must not translate directly into influence on decisions. De-democratisation is when a category ("boss — subordinate", "budget employee — private") becomes a political unit and people vote by category.

**The strike.** The protocol divides voters by income and dependence: "the takers" will be poorer and more dependent, "those who stay" richer and freer; the category "poor/dependent" translates directly into "does not decide". Categorical inequality written into an electoral rule for the first time.

**The answer.** Today a category is already translated into politics — the very one Tilly named first: the employer's hierarchy. "The core" votes by category — budget employees under the boss, the brought-in in columns (1f §2, §5; 039 §88.9) — and this inequality is invisible because it wears the same dress: one ballot. The protocol does not create a categorical division — it breaks the existing one: the subordinate can take the payment in secret, and the category "boss — subordinate" stops voting as a unit (040af item 5). And the protocol knows no categories at the entrance: one payment for all (048m Art. 4), a state is chosen, not assigned (1h); the poor man to whom the vote is worth more stays, the rich man to whom it is worth less takes. Tilly demanded the insulation of politics from categories; the protocol is the only rule with not a single category at the entrance, only a price, the same for all.

**Tilly's continuation and the architect's move.** Tilly would reply: if the payment is taken out of poverty, the category "poor" leaves the decision de facto — "categorical inequality dressed as choice". The architect: "well yes, the poor man understands the governing of a state less well and, understanding that clearly about himself, does not push into governing. But if he reads, learns and wants to understand, he can always do so — and for that he need not become rich." This separates two things Tilly glued together. A category for Tilly is what one cannot leave by one's own effort: sex, ethnicity, place in a hierarchy. "Understands / does not understand" is not a category: a boundary anyone crosses himself, by reading, not by money, and the door is open to everyone at the next election (048m Art. 5 part 2). The protocol selects not by income but by what a person has decided himself about his own understanding and his own stake (item 2); income correlates with that decision, as it correlates with anything, but does not determine it — and the poor man who wanted to understand stays at the polls without having grown rich. It is the only boundary in electoral law that anyone can cross without permission, without an exam (040aa item 3) and without money; hence it is not categorical by Tilly's definition, and the demand for understanding is exactly what the protocol creates (040ae item 2).

**Residual.** None by the definition of a category. The threshold of 040p §1 remains as a measurement — what share of takers took out of need rather than decision — but that is a question about the size of the payment, not about the rule being categorical.

## 4. Coercion and capital

**The argument** (*Coercion, Capital, and European States*, 1990). States were built on two resources — coercion (army, police, extraction) and capital (towns, merchants, credit) — and the form of the state was set by what was at hand. Democracy grows where the ruler needs the townsmen's money for war and must bargain with them for taxes: representation is the price power pays for capital. Where coercion is cheap and capital not needed, there is no bargain.

**The strike.** The protocol is a payment from the budget, that is, from the same coercion (tax); the government does not bargain with the citizen, it buys him off with a handout from what was collected from him.

**The answer.** Tilly described where representation comes from: power bargains for what it needs and does not have. Today the government needs the vote, and it has it — free, through the brought-in (029 §29.11: "convenient when everyone guards for you, free of charge, what only you can use"). There is no bargain not because coercion is cheap but because the resource is gratis. The protocol makes the vote a resource that must be bargained for — for the first time since turnout became free: a government that needs turnout must offer more than the payment, and to the one who decides for himself. That is Tilly's mechanism in its most exact form: representation arises when the citizen has what power needs and cannot take for nothing. The capital of seventeenth-century townsmen was money; the citizen's capital under the protocol is a vote with a price. "From his own tax" — true, and it is an argument for: Goldscheid (040o) — the budget returns to the one from whom it took for free not only the tax.

**Tilly's continuation and the architect's move.** Tilly would ask: what stops the government, as in the seventeenth century, from finding cheap coercion instead of a bargain — forcing people not to take? The architect: "to force is to violate the protocol adopted by referendum; the opposition will highlight it at once and it will be clear and visible to everyone. And if the government pays its own people extra illegally — the opposition will say: since you pay illegally, we can put a legal increase of the payment to a referendum." It is a trap for a coercing government from both sides. To force people not to take is to break a rule adopted by the very voters the government represents; and it is visible not by denunciation but by the counter: a precinct with full turnout and an abnormally small share of takers (048m Art. 10, the precinct balance) is evidence in itself. And if the government, to keep turnout, pays its own on top, in an envelope — it confirms that the price is too low, and the opposition puts a legal increase to a referendum (Art. 3). Any move by the government against the protocol either breaks the law in plain view or proves the payment is too small — and in both cases feeds the referendum. It is the fork of 1f §5 ("every move by the government costs") applied to coercion: there is no cheap coercion under the protocol, because every coercion leaves a trace in the numbers and every top-up in the price.

*The trap's third side — the architect's continuation.* "Those who legally received little will very much favour raising the payment, knowing that the government is already paying. And those who receive more illegally will prefer to receive it legally rather than under threat of criminal prosecution." The coalition for a rise forms by itself, from both groups: the outsiders because they see that the insiders are paid more; the insiders because the same sum legally beats an envelope under a criminal article (048m Art. 11). By its top-up the government creates a majority for a referendum on raising the payment (Art. 3) out of its own and the others at once, and the only way not to feed that majority is not to top up — that is, to keep the protocol.

**Residual.** None. The limit is the same as for all: where commissions are occupied and there are no numbers (040af item 2), there is no evidence either; but that is a limit of the referendum, not of the protocol.

## 5. De-democratisation

**The argument.** Democratisation is reversible, and the retreat runs through the same mechanisms backwards: trust networks leave the public for the private, categorical inequality returns to politics, autonomous centres of power — the army, oligarchs, security services — escape public control. The main sign is not a coup but a slow narrowing of consultation with the procedures intact; described ten years before Runciman (040aa) and Przeworski (040v item 4).

**The strike.** The protocol accelerates de-democratisation through all three mechanisms at once: trust networks leave politics for money (item 1), the poor drop out as a category (item 3), and a government without brought-in turnout turns to the autonomous centres of power — those who need not be brought.

**The answer.** The first two mechanisms are turned over: a network that takes the payment was not taking part in politics (item 1); "understands / does not understand" is crossed by reading (item 3). The third is exact in Tilly: a government deprived of mass turnout seeks support in force. But that is today's position, not tomorrow's: "the core" is already brought by force — the employer, fear, the apparatus (1f §5) — and the autonomous centres of power are already in politics, because turnout is their product. The protocol takes that product from force: nobody can be brought, forcing people not to take is in plain view (item 4). Under the protocol the government does not turn to force — it loses what force was needed for at elections. What remains is force outside elections — a coup — and that is the limit Tilly, Linz (040i) and Runciman set alike: no electoral rule protects against tanks, and to demand it is the bounty trap (040ac item 1).

**The architect's move — to whom the government really turns.** "How is it that Tilly did not say the government turns to the centre — to the returned, clever 'burned'? There are more of them than radicals and autonomous centres of power." The reconstruction above looked only at what the government loses — half the picture, and the worse half. Under the protocol the government loses brought-in turnout; what does any government that needs turnout do? It goes where turnout is. It is with "those who stay": the returned "burned" with a ×2 share are the largest group at the polls — more than the radicals, more than the fanatics, and incomparably more than any autonomous centre of power, which has no votes at all. Force gives the government turnout only while turnout can be brought; when it cannot, force is useless at elections, and the centre is the only one who decides. The government turns not to the tanks but to the centre, because tanks do not vote and the centre votes with a ×2 share. This is the promise of a future (040w item 5) and the change of the menu's customer (040ae item 5) seen from the government's side: not "whom else can it turn to" but "whom is it now profitable to turn to". Tilly's third mechanism under the protocol is not accelerated but reversed: the autonomous centres of power lose their only buyer — a government that needed their turnout.

**The architect's second move — and if the government does turn to force.** "The government turns to force — and what will it tell the soldiers, who themselves, in the rank and file, most likely took the money? 'Let us cancel the protocol'? Under the protocol force too is, as it were, on the people's side, and will not get into crude suppression without a referendum. Say the police go in for the government, and the army says 'we are, as it were, for the protocol' — and then what?" Three things. *A soldier cannot be told "we cancel":* the army's rank and file took the payment not as "the people" but personally, into their accounts; an order to suppress is an order to take money from those being ordered, and the government has no explanation of why he should go against his own pocket (048d §2–3: the soldier is the cash guard, "suppress the uprising for the dividend" = "kill the source of your own income"). *A legitimate ground not to obey:* the private and the people are one set of "takers"; suppression without a referendum breaks a rule the soldier himself has already carried out, and that gives the army what it usually lacks — the law on its side against the order. *The split between police and army:* a known historical fork — Egypt and Tunisia 2011, Serbia 2000, Romania 1989, Ukraine 2014: the army refused to shoot, the police and security services held to the government longer; Belarus 2020 is the reverse case, the riot police sufficed because the army was not brought in. The police are closer to the government, local and smaller; the army is a cross-section of the country, national and from the "rank and file" (048d §1). The protocol changes nothing in this fork but one thing: the army no longer "sympathises" — it has a personal, countable interest written into the rule. "We are, as it were, for the protocol — and then what?" — and nothing: a government without the army, against a people with the army, does not suppress but bargains (item 4).

*Why the fork runs this way — the mechanism, the architect.* "The army is for the people because it is the people, and its rank and file are always paid little. The police and security services are fed by the government, but they have few weapons and are few in number; under large discontent it is precisely the army that will be called in, and the army is usually conscript — that is, from the people." Three reasons, all three about money and numbers, not sympathy. *Composition and pay:* conscription draws from every family, the rank and file are paid little, and the payment is a noticeable sum for a private, not "participation". *The fed are few:* the police and security services suffice for targeted suppression, not mass suppression — that is the boundary of Belarus 2020, where discontent stayed at a scale the riot police could handle. *Large discontent requires the army:* the government has no other hands, and the army is conscript — from the people who took the payment. The fork is a function of scale: the small is suppressed by the fed, the large requires the army, and under the protocol the army is bought not by the government but by the rule; the larger the protest, the closer the government comes to the force that is on the protest's side.

*The closure — the architect.* "If the government cuts the payments or otherwise tampers with the protocol's rules, both those who are for the money and those who are for the vote will come out, and the opposition, with its motive, will lead them all. There will be no small protests here. And most likely the army will be called — or the government, foreseeing that line-up, will not want to touch the protocol." The protocol itself sets the scale of protest against itself. Any encroachment on the rule — cutting the payment, shifting the percentage, narrowing the circle — strikes both sets at once: "the takers" lose money, "those who stay" lose their share of the vote, and for the first time they have one grievance and one adversary. It is a rare case of discontent that is not split: usually a government suppresses a protest because it is somebody's — budget employees against the private sector, the young against the old — whereas here it is everyone against one, led by an opposition that has both the motive and a ready slogan (item 4: "since you pay illegally — we raise it legally"). So a small protest about the protocol does not happen by construction: it either does not start, or is large at once. The large requires the army; the army is from the people who took the payment; and a government that can count sees this chain in advance and does not touch the protocol. The rule is protected not only by the lock of the referendum (048m Art. 15) but by the fact that breaking it gathers everyone against the breaker at once — the self-reinforcement Tilly looked for in trust networks and which the protocol delivers through a price.

**Residual to the second move.** The split is not guaranteed: where the army is contract-based, small and fed separately — a guard, praetorians, the IRGC (057c) — it is not from the "rank and file", and the fork does not work; the same limit as 048d §8. But even there the protocol makes suppression dearer by one for every soldier who took the payment.

**Residual.** One, and it is Tilly's: his de-democratisation is slow, and the protocol's effect is slow too — parties change the menu from the second cycle (040ae item 5), "the burned" do not return at once (040s). If the retreat is faster than the thermostat, the protocol will not be in time; that is a question of speed, which the pilot measures in its second cycle (048l).

## Weak point

A reconstruction; Tilly wrote about centuries and states, not turnout rules, and the transfer of his networks to "the core" is ours. The distinction "participant / hostage" exists in Tilly as "integration through coercion", but he did not apply it to voting under dependence. And the architect's move is an inference from choice: whoever took did not want; it holds if the payment is not so large that those who wanted take it too — that is, at the threshold of 040p §1, which until the pilot is an estimate.
