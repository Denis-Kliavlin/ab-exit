## 1. The logic of a spin dictator in six theses

1. **Two types.** A dictator of fear (Stalin, Pinochet) holds on by terror and ideology. A spin dictator (Fujimori, Chávez, Orbán, Erdoğan, Putin before 2022) holds on by popularity: he imitates democracy — elections, a parliament, courts — represses selectively and quietly, and hides censorship behind changes of media ownership. The share of the second type has grown since the 1990s, the share of the first has fallen.
2. **Two classes of citizens.** An informed minority — the educated, with access to independent sources — knows that the ruler is incompetent or steals. An uninformed majority judges the ruler by what it sees: the economy and television. The ruler lives on the majority's approval.
3. **The main task is to keep the informed from convincing the uninformed.** For that: censorship, co-optation, selective imprisonment, pushing people into emigration ("let them leave"). Terror against everyone is unnecessary and harmful: mass violence itself tells the majority that the ruler is not what he claims.
4. **Elections must be real, with fraud at the margins.** A spin dictator wants to win with 60–70 % in a way that stays plausible; large-scale falsification destroys the image of a popular leader. He needs honest polls — for himself.
5. **Why spin rather than fear.** Modernisation: the more educated the population, the dearer fear and the more profitable spin. The international environment: open repression costs sanctions and isolation.
6. **How it ends.** When the share of the informed grows so much that spin stops paying, the ruler either leaves or turns to fear. The authors name this transition in advance; Russia after February 2022 is its example.

## 2. Where the authors' logic and the protocol's coincide

Both start from the same observation: a ruler is held up neither by the elite nor by the army but by a majority that does not understand and votes. For Guriev and Treisman these are "the uninformed"; for the protocol, the apathetic, the brought-in and the meek (011b). It is one and the same person, and the portrait is already in the repository (1f §2): a woman of 55–75, a village, television, the budget. The authors explain why a spin dictator values her more than the informed: she is his turnout and his approval.

## 3. What the protocol does with each thesis

| The authors' thesis | What the protocol changes | Where |
|---|---|---|
| The ruler lives on the approval of the uninformed majority | The protocol separates approval from the vote. Approving stays free; voting costs the payment one gives up. The woman of 1f can go on approving the ruler on television and at the same time take the money and not come. Her approval stays with the pollsters; her ballot leaves the box. | 013f; 1f §7 |
| The main task is to keep the informed from convincing the uninformed | The protocol requires no convincing. The question "the parties are paid for my vote — why not me" carries no information about the ruler's incompetence and needs no proof: it only needs to be heard. It spreads through loyalist channels — Bonya, the Z-bloggers, the staff room (056f §13g) — bypassing the very channel "informed → uninformed" that the dictator blocks. Censorship is built against knowledge; it is not built against a question about money. | 056f §13e–13i |
| The informed are co-opted or pushed out | The protocol does not care who raises it (056f §13l). It needs no carrier from the informed minority; co-optation and the emigration of the intelligentsia do not act on a question about money. | 056f §13l |
| Elections must be real, with fraud at the margins | This is the spin dictator's weakest point, and the protocol strikes it. If the uninformed majority takes the payment and exits, those left at the ballot box are the informed and those with something to lose. To keep the usual 60–70 %, fraud at the margins must become fraud across the whole width — which is exactly what a spin dictator cannot afford: large-scale falsification breaks the image he stands on. The counter and the reconciliation of two records (048f) make the width of fraud visible as a number. | 048f; 048i |
| Spin is more profitable than fear | To ban a question about money is to show fear: censorship that can be seen destroys spin by the authors' own logic (§1, point 3). To permit it is to release one's own majority from the ballot box. To promise the money oneself is to bow (056f §13j). All three moves push a spin dictator towards a dictator of fear, that is, towards the regime the authors call expensive and unstable in a modernised country. | 056f §13n, §13p |
| Spin ends when the informed become too many | The protocol brings the same end by another road: it reduces not the share of the uninformed but the share of the uninformed *in the ballot box*. The authors expect this from education — a generation; the protocol proposes a payment — one referendum. It is modernisation without the wait. | 1f §7 |

## 4. Where the protocol is powerless — and this must be said plainly

1. **Against a dictator of fear.** The protocol is introduced by referendum and lives by the counter. Where no referendum can be held and the counter will be drawn, only the question remains — not the rule (056f §13i). Russia after 2022 is, in the authors' classification, moving from spin to fear; that is why the repository proposes for Russia to spread the question, not to introduce the rule, and §7 of the same chapter distinguishes the risk of a legal signature collection from that of an anonymous count.
2. **Against a controlled reconciliation.** Double entry (048f) works while the treasury and the electoral commission are independent of each other. Under a spin dictator they are formally independent and in fact subordinate. In such a country the honesty of the counter is not a property of the protocol but a condition of its introduction; the protocol does not create that condition.
3. **Against the co-optation of the protocol itself.** A spin dictator loves popular measures and may introduce the payment himself. That is a bow (056f §13j), and for the protocol it is not a loss, but not a victory either: a counter the ruler controls will show him what he wants. The defence is the same as in point 2: the independence of the reconciliation, which the protocol does not create.
4. **Against knowledge.** The protocol does not make the uninformed informed. The woman of 1f who took the payment does not learn that the ruler is incompetent; she only stops voting for him. The asymmetry of knowledge at the centre of the authors' theory remains — what changes is its weight in the ballot box, not the asymmetry itself.
5. **Against patronage.** Where the ruler's majority are not television viewers but recipients — public-sector employees, public works from the mayor (Hungary), contracts — they have a stake, and may not take the payment if it is less than what they get from the ruler. Then the protocol filters out fewer than expected, and that is a question of the sum, not of the rule (048n: the limits of the sum).

## 5. What the authors would give the protocol if they read it

Their answer would probably be this: "You are right that a spin dictator stands on an uninformed majority, and that to separate its approval from its vote is a blow to the support. But you assume an honest counter in a country where the authorities do nothing but draw numbers without looking as if they draw them. Your protocol is not a weapon against a spin dictator; it is a description of what will destroy him if someone manages to hold a referendum and protect the reconciliation." And that description is correct: the protocol is not an instrument of overthrow but a rule under which spin stops paying — because the approval it grows by television no longer turns into ballots for free.

What in this is confirmed by fact: Hungary, April 2026. Orbán — in the authors' book a model spin dictator — lost not to an informed minority but to his own majority, which was presented a bill in money (039; 1f §2, point 9). There was no payment under the protocol there; there was the same mechanics — a vote that acquired a price.

## 6. Weak point of the chapter

The summary of the book is made from memory of the text, without rereading; the authors have not commented on the protocol, and §5 is a reconstruction, not a quotation. The main conclusion — "the protocol separates approval from the vote" — follows from the theory of self-selection, not from data: nowhere has it been measured what share of those who approve of a ruler will take the payment. The point about fraud "across the whole width" assumes that the counter cannot be drawn — which is precisely the condition not met in a spin dictator's country (§4, point 2).
