# AB-EXIT and the Problem of Rent: an Answer to Henry George

**Chapter:** 06 — Structural Hypocrisy and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File version:** v1
**Date:** 12 June 2026

## Purpose of the section

This section answers the only line of criticism that tests AB-EXIT not for logical holes (6 AI models searched for them in 449+ rounds and found none) but for **the boundary of its domain of application**. This line is set by Henry George — the author of "Progress and Poverty" (1879), one of the most influential economic thinkers of the 19th century, whose idea of a citizen's dividend from common rent became the direct ancestor of the Alaska fund on which §14 relies.

George is the best adversarial critic of AB-EXIT precisely because he is not an opponent of the idea but its predecessor. His criticism strikes at the place where the project really does have a boundary. And examining this criticism does not weaken the project but attaches to it a whole intellectual tradition — Georgists, supporters of the land-value tax (LVT), anti-corruption campaigners, left progressives.

## George's criterion

George explained the central paradox of the industrial age — "the richer the society, the deeper the poverty of its lower strata" — by one cause. The growth of productivity is captured by **rent**: the income of the owners of what they did not create. First of all land, but more broadly — any monopoly asset that allows extracting an unearned share of others' labour.

From this follows his criterion for assessing any reform: **does it attack the root — the appropriation of unearned rent — or treat a symptom, leaving the extraction mechanism untouched?** Through this lens George would judge AB-EXIT too.

## What George would approve

**The recognition of structural hypocrisy.** George all his life denounced exactly this: society declares as the norm what benefits rent-holders, and as an anomaly attempts to touch it. The argument of §29 ("48 % non-turnout = the norm, but compensated non-turnout = the destruction of democracy") is a pure Georgist move of exposing a double standard. George would recognise it as his own.

**The dividend as a right, not alms.** Here the coincidence is almost literal. George proposed distributing land rent to society as a direct dividend to every citizen. He sharply distinguished a handout, which humiliates, from **the citizen's share in the common heritage**, which restores justice. AB-EXIT, presented in the second frame, is a direct descendant of his idea.

## George's three blows

### First blow — "you pay rent from the same barrel"

George would ask: where does the money for the dividend come from? If from the common tax pot — then from taxes on labour and capital, i.e. on production. And the rent-holders again stay aside. Then AB-EXIT is redistribution among producers while the rentiers watch.

### Second blow — "you repair politics, not the economy"

George's whole book is about the futility of political reform without the elimination of rent. Give people an ideal democracy on top of a system where wealth leaks into rent — and they will ideally vote for the continuation of poverty. AB-EXIT improves the quality of the voter, but even an ideal voter will not vote against what the reform has not presented to him as the cause.

### Third blow — "you buy silence from the robbed"

The most uncomfortable, signature moral reversal of George. The poor man takes the dividend and leaves the field. The rent taken from him is measured per year as many times more — and you bought him off with a small sum and got his signature under exit from the struggle. In George's mouth it would sound almost like an accusation: AB-EXIT is the most elegant instrument for conserving rent, because it demobilises precisely those with the most reasons to demand redistribution.

## Why George's premises broke in the 21st century

All three blows rest on one silent premise: **the poor man understands who robs him and, given a tool, will vote against.** In George's world the poor man had a visible antagonist — the landowner with the boundary post. Remove the barriers to the ballot box — and justice will be restored.

The empirics of the 2020s break this premise in two places.

**First: rent has become invisible.** It is no longer a landlord visible to the eye. It is financial, platform, regulatory and **administrative** capture — a leak that cannot be seen without analytical work. This includes what George in his 19th century almost never observed because of tiny state budgets: **administrative rent** — embezzled state contracts, kickbacks, sinecures, tenders "to the mayor's friends". This is his own beast — the appropriation of the unearned — but in a new skin he did not name.

**Second: the silent poor man has been replaced by the loudly deceived.** Today's poor man is not silent — he actively votes. For the populist behind whose back stand the rent-holders. The populist sold him the migrant as the culprit, an abstract "elite", a cultural enemy — anyone but the rentier. This is **worse** than Georgian silence. The silent one at least does not legitimise the robbery. The mobilised deceived is an active accomplice of his own robbery, and his turnout serves as a mandate for those who milk him.

## The counter-reversal: three blows become supports

### Answer to the third blow — the silence is already bought, but by the robber and for free

George is right that silence has a price. But in 2026 this silence **is already bought — for free, and bought by the rentier**. The rent-holder through the populist gets the poor man's vote for nothing, in exchange for the illusion of a fight against a false enemy.

AB-EXIT for the first time puts a price on this transfer and changes the payee. Now **the state pays, openly, by formula** — not the rentier, by hidden manipulation, with nothing. The poor man no longer gives his vote as a cheap battering ram against his own interests. This is not buying silence — it is **paying for a silence that has already occurred, while freeing it from exploitation**.

### Answer to the second blow — the dividend-from-the-median as a rent sensor

George for forty years tried to explain invisible rent to people in words — and lost, because it cannot be touched. AB-EXIT solves exactly the task George could not.

The dividend is tied to **the median wage**. It is not merely a payment — it is **a sensor**. When rentiers squeeze the economy, the median stagnates, and every citizen's dividend does not grow. This is visible to all, in a concrete figure on a personal card, every cycle. AB-EXIT for the first time makes rent capture **measurable on the voter's personal wallet** — turns George's invisible abstraction into a number the poor man can finally see and defend.

Plus the effect of clearing the electorate (§17, §23): with the exit of the deceived-apathetic the vote weight of those remaining grows by the formula 1/(1−exit), and among those remaining are pragmatists and ideologues able **to see rent**. A real land reformer or anti-corruption campaigner receives a strengthened vote on a field cleared of populist noise — the very noise in which George's own agenda drowned.

### Answer to the first blow — the money is taken from the captured flow, not from production

This destroys the main blow, because George posed a false fork. He argued with an ideal system where budget money works for society and AB-EXIT withdraws it from there. In the real system there is a third option George did not consider: **the money has already been collected from labour and capital — and already stolen.**

It does not lie in the pot feeding schools and roads. It leaks out through administrative rent — "to the mayor's friends". Then the question "where's the money from" gets an answer George is obliged to approve by his own logic: AB-EXIT redirects part of this flow **from the administrative rentier — directly to the citizen, by a transparent formula that cannot be pocketed**. This is not a new tax on production. It is the interception of an already existing leak and its return to the source — the taxpayer.

The moral structure is identical to George's. He demanded returning land rent to society through a tax. AB-EXIT returns administrative rent to society through a dividend. And does it **bypassing the official-distributor — the eternal weak point of George's own scheme**: the dividend goes straight to the card by formula, bypassing the link where rent settled. George dreamed of returning rent to the people — AB-EXIT returns it bypassing the one who in George's scheme always intercepted it on the way.

## The honest boundary — what AB-EXIT does not do

Intellectual honesty requires fixing the limit.

AB-EXIT **does not eliminate the source of land rent**. On his own territory George is irresistible: only a land-value tax (LVT) strikes the root. AB-EXIT is not an alternative to the land tax and does not claim to be.

In **clean jurisdictions with low corruption** part of the dividend is not covered by intercepting theft and will fall on the general budget — there George's first blow partly retains its force. A caveat: even there the dividend competes not with "money for schools" but with electoral rent ($26B to political technologists per cycle in a large economy, direct state funding of parties at roughly €1 per vote in a number of countries) — also an unearned share.

But George's final verdict — "you taught people to vote without giving them anything to vote for" — **is overturned**. AB-EXIT for the first time gives *something to vote for*: a measurable personal indicator instead of an invisible abstraction the poor man could neither see nor defend. This is not a land tax. It is **a sensor** that makes rent visible to the one it robs — the very condition without which the Georgian reform could never have happened.

## Conclusion — AB-EXIT as an amplifier of the Georgist agenda, not its competitor

The correct frame for the relationship of the two ideas is not "either-or" but "sensor and treatment".

AB-EXIT is the diagnostic layer: it makes rent visible on the wallet, clears the electorate of populist noise, strengthens the vote of those able to recognise rent. George's land tax is the therapeutic layer: it eliminates the source. The first creates the political conditions under which the second can be voted for at all.

George lost his historical battle not because he was wrong but because he could not make rent visible to the majority and could not clear the electorate of those who voted against their own interests. AB-EXIT solves both these tasks. In this sense it is not a negation of George but **the condition George lacked for his own victory**.

## A ready formulation for dialogue with a Georgist / progressive

> "You are right: AB-EXIT does not touch the source of rent — the land tax does that, and here we are allies, not competitors. But George lost not because he was wrong, but because he could not make rent visible to those it robs and could not stop them voting for its guards. AB-EXIT solves exactly these two tasks: the dividend-from-the-median turns invisible rent into a number on a personal card, and the clearing of the electorate strengthens the vote of those able to recognise it. We do not replace your land tax — we create the political conditions under which it can finally be voted for."
