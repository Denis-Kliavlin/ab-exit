# 40bz. Seven Arguments against the Referendum and One for the Lot: Zero Sum, the Turnout Threshold, the Autocrats' Toy, the Competence of Government, Concentration, the Dissenting Half, Switzerland — and Why All of Them Strike the Question, Not the Form

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File:** 06_040bz · v1 · 11 November 2026
**Source:** a public discussion of November 2026 on a law on national referendums (Czech Republic: initiative at 300,000 signatures, binding at a turnout of at least 35 %, EU and NATO membership, defence and the constitutional order excluded) and on the lot instead of elections. The arguments are given as common positions of political science, without attribution to an author — by the architect's decision. The architect's moves of 11 November 2026 are recorded verbatim.

---

*How to read. This is the most frequent set of arguments against direct decision, and each is true in its own way. The chapter does not dispute them — it shows that all seven describe properties of the question, not of the "yes/no" form, and therefore strike exactly the two questions the rule does not ask: "shall we hand out money" and "shall we only increase the vote". The argument for the lot is examined separately: it is more honest than it looks, and it gives away what a supporter of elections cannot say.*

## 1. Seven arguments

| № | Argument | What is true | What the protocol does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A referendum is a zero-sum game: one position wins, the other loses entirely; parliamentary elections are not: a pool of seats is at stake, and everyone gets a share | For a question of policy — true | Seats are shared by parties, not voters: one whose candidate lost gets nothing, and in majoritarian systems elections are pure zero sum ([040i §1.1](040i-linz-shulman.md), Linz). Zero sum is a property of the question, not of the form (§2) |
| 2 | A turnout threshold is necessary: otherwise "the active majority decides for everyone else", the least democratic thing of all | For free voting — true: the "active" there are the brought | A threshold gives those who did not come a free "no" — a vote without price and without presence (§3). The rule has no quorum ([048m art. 15](../08-implementation/048m-charter.md)): one who did not come is not silent, he chose D, and it is recorded |
| 3 | The referendum is the autocrats' favourite toy: on confidence in himself, on his constitution; "yes" is the position of the question's author, "no" has no shades, a negative campaign is hard to run | True | The autocrat asks about himself; the rule asks about money for the voter, and that question the autocrat will never put ([040bs](040bs-paine.md), non-political judo). "No" here has a shade — the number p. In an autocracy a referendum on the rule will not take place — an argument about the route ([040ae §1](040ae-sartori.md)) |
| 4 | Taxes, foreign policy and security are not put to referendum: they are the competence of central government | True, and the repository agrees | The rule takes one item from the elected — the right to write the electoral law for themselves ([029 §29.11](029-hypocrisy.md), a judge in his own case); the rest stays with parliament: the last referendum on the complex ([040ae §1](040ae-sartori.md), the architect's move) |
| 5 | Democracy is not decision by majority but the distribution of power and an obstacle to its concentration; a referendum gives an unambiguous answer and therefore concentrates | The definition is right | The unambiguous answer here is a lock on concentration: the elected lose the key to the electoral law, and every voter gets a choice every cycle. The distribution of power is literal |
| 6 | Half disagrees with the answer to a question of national importance — what will that half do? | True for a question of policy | The dissenting half loses nothing: adopted — the dissenter takes the payment or votes; rejected — everything as it was. And the architect's move on "it was not we who chose them" (§5) |
| 7 | Switzerland is an exception, "one of a kind" | Yes | The repository does not argue from Switzerland; the positions coincide |

## 2. The first blow: "the referendum is zero-sum" — and the architect's move: the sum belongs to the question, not to the form

"A question can be zero-sum, but a referendum cannot. So the AB-EXIT question is not a zero-sum question but a balance with mutual exclusion: either the money or the vote — not 'shall we hand out money' or 'shall we only increase the vote'."

The "yes/no" form has no sum; the question does. "Shall we hand out money" is a zero-sum question: payers against recipients, and the working vote it down ([021](../04-electoral-dynamics/021-centrist-void.md): UBI repels the most active stratum). "Shall we only increase the vote" of those who come is zero-sum too: the weight of some grows at the expense of others, and that is how the rule is read by those who see a "multiplier" in it ([1g](../01-introduction/001g-objections-index.md): "×2 / ×3"). The rule's question is neither. Everyone is offered a choice, and the mutual exclusion — one cannot take both the money and the vote — makes it a balance in the accounting sense ([048f](../08-implementation/048f-double-entry-elections.md)): the taker books the money and gives up the vote, the stayer books the weight and gives up the money, and each person's sum balances by itself. That is why the referendum on the rule has no loser under any outcome: "yes" gives everyone a choice, "no" leaves everything as it was. The zero-sum argument is right and strikes exactly the two questions the rule does not ask.

The fork for the argument's supporter: either parliamentary elections are not zero-sum for the voter too — then show what one whose candidate lost receives (Linz showed: nothing, [040i §1.1](040i-linz-shulman.md)); or every decision in which the loser receives nothing is zero-sum — and then the only vote without it is the one in which the loser receives D.

## 3. The second blow: "without a turnout threshold the active majority decides for everyone else"

The argument honestly names what it fears: those who came. For free voting the fear is right — the "active" there are not the most interested but the brought: budget employees, the carousel, the "core" ([1f](../01-introduction/001f-core-electorate.md), [030b](030b-ten-tricks.md)). But the remedy — a threshold — gives those who did not come a "no" without price and without presence, that is, the freest vote possible. By the [accounting standard](040br-ten-propaganda-devices.md) it is an off-balance item counted as a posted entry: one who did not come chose nothing, yet he was counted. For an autocrat a threshold is doubly convenient: bring his own to cross it, or keep it from being crossed if the question is someone else's.

The rule answers not by abolishing the fear but with a price. There is no quorum ([048m art. 15](../08-implementation/048m-charter.md), [1g](../01-introduction/001g-objections-index.md): "a supermajority is needed"), because after adoption one who did not come ceases to be an off-balance item: he chose D, and that is a posted entry. The "active majority" with a price is those who refused the money, and the argument does not propose to fear them. The threshold is a substitute for the price the argument does not name.

**The architect's move — what the threshold stands on logically.** "Before AB-EXIT the turnout threshold said that the rest had perhaps not heard about the referendum. But if at an election after AB-EXIT 99 % took the money or voted, yet those voting are fewer than the threshold, that is clearly not the same thing." A threshold has two possible foundations, and both are about the invisibility of the one who did not come. First: he did not know — he had not heard of the question, and a decision without him is not his. Second: he is against — abstention is read as "no", and the threshold protects against a decision in spite of the silent. After the rule the one who did not come is visible: taking D is participation in the decision "money or the vote", recorded and paid for; turnout in this sense is the share of those who made a choice, that is ~99 %, not the share who dropped a ballot. The first foundation disappears by construction: one cannot take the money without having heard the question. The second reduces to the counter: if abstention must be read as "no", then the number of takers is that "no", published by district — and the threshold that would like to measure it has already been measured. So after the rule the threshold is either met automatically or asks about what is already known. The threshold stood on invisibility; the rule removes it.

## 4. The third blow: "do not despise the lot — chance among an already selected pool removes the unhealthy passions inherent in elections"

The argument comes from a supporter of elections: elections are the best thing humanity invented and must not be given up, but mechanical rotation is useful, turnover is beneficial in itself; the Greeks loved the lot, Venice built it into the choice of the doge.

**The architect's observation** (the opponent's name omitted by his decision): "…for elections but not for the referendum — that is, does not admit it, but considers the choice unreasonable. And it also matters that a lot among those already elected is considered more honest than elections. There is a catch here too: in both cases the voters are not trusted 100 %, but apparently this cannot be said honestly."

The pool is selected by the voters — this step the argument trusts. The last step — who exactly — it wants to hand to chance, because the "passions" are exactly there: the brought, the charismatic, the mobilisation. That is, the voter is trusted with sorting but not with the decision. This is the position of the mini-publics school, and the architect's move on it is already recorded: "a steam engine for want of a combustion engine" ([040au §3](040au-landemore.md)) — seeing no honest census, they take the lesser evil, chance, which at least cannot be bought. For Manin ([040x §1](040x-manin.md)) the lot is the democratic mechanism and elections the aristocratic one; here it is the reverse: the lot is a protection from the demos.

The fork. Accept it whole: the voter cannot be trusted with "yes/no" (the referendum) or with the last step (the lot), but can be trusted with selecting the pool. Then the distrust attaches not to the voter but to the fact that in the decisive step the vote costs nothing: "passions" and "the active majority" are properties of the free vote, not of people. Two ways out: take the voter out of the decisive step — the threshold and the lot, the argument's way, which cannot be named aloud, because "the voter cannot be trusted" is the autocrat's thesis; or take the free ride out of the decisive step — the rule's way. Both of the argument's devices are substitutes for the price it does not name. And the lot is compatible with the rule: a mini-public of those who stayed is better than a mini-public of everyone ([040au §3](040au-landemore.md), remainder).

## 5. "Half disagrees — what will that half do?" — the architect's move: "it was not we who chose them"

"Today it is even worse: the dissenting half that did not come to the election has every moral right to say 'it was not we who chose them'. But if half took the money, they too can say they were forced — yet this logic breaks almost completely, all the energy is channelled, and the excuse is much smaller: only for the destitute, and there are not so many of them."

Today the argument's question has the answer "nothing, and with every right": one who did not come is an off-balance item, bound by nothing, and "it was not we who chose them" is irrefutable ([040aa](040aa-runciman.md)). After the rule the taker is a posted entry: he chose himself, under a rule adopted by all at a referendum, and for the cycle. "We were forced" works only where D was need, not choice: by the official poverty line that is about 8–12 % (an estimate from Russian and US poverty statistics, not a measurement under the rule), not half. The energy of dissent gets a channel — come and refuse D, campaign for a higher p, choose anew after a cycle ([1i](../01-introduction/001i-consequences.md), [1g](../01-introduction/001g-objections-index.md): "takers will not recognise the government").

## 6. What these arguments give the protocol

- **An exact name for what the rule's question lacks:** zero sum. "Shall we hand out money" and "increase the vote" have it, and the argument helps tell the rule from both.
- **An honest list of what must not be decided by referendum** — taxes, war, the constitution — and confirmation that the rule is not on it: it is about the price of non-participation, that is, the one question that cannot be entrusted to those elected under it.
- **An admission that the fear of the "active" and of "passions" is a fear of the free vote.** The supporter of elections does not name it, but his devices — the threshold and the lot — measure exactly that.

## Weak point

The arguments are paraphrased from one public discussion and without a name — by the architect's decision; a reader who recognises the source may check the paraphrase against the recording. The answers to arguments 2 and 5 are built through the accounting standard — the assistant's construction. The honest limit is the same as in [040ae](040ae-sartori.md): a free hall will come to the referendum on the rule too, the price appears after adoption; the answer is a fork for the turnout machine, not a denial. 🟡
