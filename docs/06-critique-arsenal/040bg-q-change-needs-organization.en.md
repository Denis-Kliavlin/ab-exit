# 40bg. Political Scientists' Question 5: "Change Requires Organisation"

*How to read. The fifth premise: to change anything you need an organisation — a party, an office network, a council; an institution is an organisation of people that outlives the change of people. So the government fears organisations and destroys them. The protocol disputes the premise itself: it is an institution without an organisation.*

**The series frame — the architect's move.** Political science wants the same thing the protocol delivers: a change of power by ordinary procedure, without bloodshed, through a law that cannot be repealed. It understands that the apathetic and the impulsive must be removed from the decision, but it knows no honest franchise qualification. And within the magnitudes and parameters that qualifications have had for two thousand years, it is right: every known qualification took the vote away from outside and hit the weak, and non-participation was a loss of the vote, that is, an illness ([006](../02-history/006-2500-years.md)). The architect: "AB-EXIT somehow went around all of this. I am in shock myself." The protocol is not a subtype of the known qualifications but another dimension: a qualification each person passes himself, in secret, by refusing money, and a balance in which nobody loses — the one who leaves gets the payment and the right to return, the one who stays gets double weight.

## 1. Who says so

- **E. Shulman** (2026, theses 6 and 7). An institution is a repeated practice that remains when people change. Navalny's offices were built as an institution — a network with local autonomy — and were physically destroyed. Memorial is a real institution: archives taken abroad, reputation, people. The government are spontaneous institutionalists: they fear not the crowd but organisation.
- **G. Almond** (in Shulman, thesis 17): discontent must be aggregated into a programme, and that is the business of parties.
- **C. Tilly** (040ag): change is made by organised networks.

## 2. The architect's move: the protocol is a basic rule, hence a referendum

On thesis 6: "purely about Russia and of minor importance. The protocol is altogether a basic rule, almost constitutional, and that is exactly why a referendum."

The protocol is not built as an organisation that must outlive its people. It is a rule of constitutional rank, adopted by referendum and changed only by referendum (048m art. 14). It needs no leader, no office, not even an author (059l §4: the protocol is nobody's).

## 3. The architect's move: supra-institutional, belongs to nobody, organisers are replaceable

On thesis 7: "That is exactly the protocol's trick: it is supra-institutional and universal for any organisation, it belongs to nobody. It is closer to the constitutional articles saying power belongs to the people. Organisers are always vulnerable, but what matters is that they are replaceable."

The government, per Shulman, fears organisations and destroys them. The protocol deprives it of what it knows how to fear: it has no network, office, council, members or leader. Each makes the choice himself, in secret, on his own card (048m art. 9.3); its memory is the bank records of payments and the budget line (art. 10). An organisation can be crushed; millions of personal choices and a budget line have nothing to crush. Before adoption an initiative has organisers, and they are vulnerable like any — but replaceable: the rule belongs to none of them. After adoption the rule works without the people who support it.

## 4. The architect's move: turnout in an autocracy does not matter

On thesis 10 ("turnout at the Duma election is unpredictable: depression and driven public-sector workers against the wish to express oneself out of spite"): "Turnout at elections in an autocracy does not matter. AB-EXIT lifts the question above voting at elections — to a referendum."

Shulman cannot predict turnout because one number glues together the driven, the aggrieved, the depressed and the indifferent. Political science guesses how many will come to elections that decide nothing. The protocol moves the decisive question to the level of the rule under which any elections are then held, and after adoption it splits turnout into two honest numbers: how many took the payment and how those who stayed voted. The driven worker secretly takes the payment, the aggrieved stays and weighs double, the apathetic leaves with money — and that shows as a share of takers, not as silence.

## 5. What follows

| Premise | What is true | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| an institution is an organisation that outlives people | for organisations | the protocol is a basic, near-constitutional rule, hence a referendum (the architect's move) |
| the government crushes organisations, change is vulnerable | yes, for organisations | the protocol is supra-institutional and belongs to nobody; organisers are vulnerable but replaceable (the architect's move) |
| turnout must be predicted to understand society | no | turnout in an autocracy does not matter; the question is lifted to a referendum, turnout splits into two honest numbers (the architect's move) |
