# 40bg. Political Scientists' Question 5: "Change Requires Organisation"

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites

*How to read. The fifth premise: to change anything you need an organisation — a party, an office network, a council; an institution is an organisation of people that outlives the change of people. So the government fears organisations and destroys them. The protocol disputes the premise itself: it is an institution without an organisation.*

**The series frame — the architect's move.** Political science wants the same thing the protocol delivers: a change of power by ordinary procedure, without bloodshed, through a law that cannot be repealed. It understands that the apathetic and the impulsive must be removed from the decision, but it knows no honest franchise qualification. And within the magnitudes and parameters that qualifications have had for two thousand years, it is right: every known qualification took the vote away from outside and hit the weak, and non-participation was a loss of the vote, that is, an illness ([006](../02-history/006-2500-years.md)). The architect: "AB-EXIT somehow went around all of this. I am in shock myself." The protocol is not a subtype of the known qualifications but another dimension: a qualification each person passes himself, in secret, by refusing money, and a balance in which nobody loses — the one who leaves gets the payment and the right to return, the one who stays gets double weight.

## 1. Who says so

- **E. Shulman** (2026, theses 6 and 7). An institution is a repeated practice that remains when people change. Navalny's offices were built as an institution — a network with local autonomy — and were physically destroyed. Memorial is a real institution: archives taken abroad, reputation, people. The government are spontaneous institutionalists: they fear not the crowd but organisation.
- **G. Almond** (in Shulman, thesis 17): discontent must be aggregated into a programme, and that is the business of parties.
- **C. Tilly** (040ag): change is made by organised networks.

## 2. The architect's move: the protocol is a basic rule, hence a referendum

On thesis 6: "purely about Russia and of minor importance. The protocol is altogether a basic rule, almost constitutional, and that is exactly why a referendum."

The protocol is not built as an organisation that must outlive its people. It is a rule of constitutional rank, adopted by referendum and changed only by referendum (048m art. 14). It needs no leader, no office, not even an author (059l §4: the protocol is nobody's).

## 3. The architect's move: supra-institutional, belongs to nobody, organisers are replaceable

On thesis 7: "That is exactly the protocol's trick: it is supra-institutional and universal for any organisation, it belongs to nobody. It is closer to the constitutional articles saying power belongs to the people. Organisers are always vulnerable, but what matters is that they are replaceable."

The government, per Shulman, fears organisations and destroys them. The protocol deprives it of what it knows how to fear: it has no network, office, council, members or leader. Each makes the choice himself, in secret, on his own card (048m art. 9.3); its memory is the bank records of payments and the budget line (art. 10). An organisation can be crushed; millions of personal choices and a budget line have nothing to crush. Before adoption an initiative has organisers, and they are vulnerable like any — but replaceable: the rule belongs to none of them. After adoption the rule works without the people who support it.

## 4. The architect's move: turnout in an autocracy does not matter

On thesis 10 ("turnout at the Duma election is unpredictable: depression and driven public-sector workers against the wish to express oneself out of spite"): "Turnout at elections in an autocracy does not matter. AB-EXIT lifts the question above voting at elections — to a referendum."

Shulman cannot predict turnout because one number glues together the driven, the aggrieved, the depressed and the indifferent. Political science guesses how many will come to elections that decide nothing. The protocol moves the decisive question to the level of the rule under which any elections are then held, and after adoption it splits turnout into two honest numbers: how many took the payment and how those who stayed voted. The driven worker secretly takes the payment, the aggrieved stays and weighs double, the apathetic leaves with money — and that shows as a share of takers, not as silence.

## 4b. The architect's move: protest over a stolen result is no longer needed

Shulman (thesis 16): in 2011 people were brought onto the streets by having taken part in the election — "we voted, everyone around us voted, and they showed a different result"; those who did not vote do not protest, because they did not invest themselves by acting. In the repository this is the pizzeria formula (018 §40.2): no investment, no demand for transparency.

**The architect's move.** "The protocol changes the type of fraud so that this no longer matters." The 2011 protest was born of a forgery that could be neither proved nor disproved: votes were added in the names of those who did not come and totals were rewritten. Under the protocol that type disappears: more than 90 entries in 100 are verified by the person himself, the stuffing reserve is about 3 % of the list, and votes cannot be added — only genuine ones substituted (048i, art. 10 part 2). The result cannot be stolen on a scale that changes the outcome, and the grievance "we were cheated of the result" does not arise (1i: the unprovability of the absence of fraud disappears). Shulman explains that protest is born of a stolen result; the protocol does not make protest more effective — it removes its cause. And the investment she speaks of becomes, under the protocol, the condition of the vote: the one who stays has refused the payment, and a hall made only of those who invested cannot be cheated quietly.

## 4c. Aggregation in three dimensions — the architect's move

Shulman (thesis 17), after Almond: discontent is first expressed — articulation — then turned into a programme and a demand — aggregation, the business of parties. Autocracies allow articulation but keep aggregation for themselves; hence protests as petitions: you complained, you got an answer. In 2011 the answer was "we will beat you, jail you, cancel elections"; the conservative turn of 2012 has defined Russian politics ever since.

The protocol needs no intermediary for aggregation: its question is already aggregated — "is this government worth your vote, or is the payment worth more to you" — and the answers add up to one number without a party or a congress. It is not a party that aggregates but addition; this is aggregation an autocracy cannot keep for itself, because it is produced by the bank register, not by an organ. And it cannot be answered with a baton: nobody demanded anything, people took a lawful payment.

**The architect's move.** "I had not even thought of such complex aggregation — this is already 3D. They also increase the vote of those who stay — you forgot it because this never existed before." One act of "taking" adds up in three dimensions at once:

| Dimension | What the taker does | What it used to be |
|---|---|---|
| demand | the share of takers is an assessment of the government needing no translation: this many people consider a vote for you not worth the payment | a petition, a complaint, a poll — words that can go unheard |
| bill | every payment costs the budget money and is visible by precinct | a complaint costs nothing |
| weight | every leaver increases the share of the vote of every one who stays | not voting affected nothing, the vote was simply lost |

A petition has one dimension, words. Party aggregation has one dimension, a programme. In the protocol one act is at once a demand, a price and the strength of those who stay. An autocracy can take the programme for itself and ignore the complaint, but cannot cancel any of the three: the demand and the bill are produced by the bank register, the weight by the arithmetic of shares.

*Boundary.* The protocol aggregates only the assessment of the government; the programme "what to do about petrol" is formulated by candidates for those who stay.

## 4d. The Coordination Council and the protocol: the same aim, simpler and safer — the architect's move

Shulman (thesis 18): the Opposition Coordination Council after 2011 was an attempt to institutionalise protest; that is why its members are still persecuted with particular fury — nearly all have received grave statuses, some are no longer alive.

**The architect's move.** "This is important. The essence is the same, but AB-EXIT makes protest far simpler and safer."

| | The Coordination Council | The protocol |
|---|---|---|
| what a person must do | go into the street, vote for council members, belong, sign | one choice before an election: take the payment or stay |
| who is visible | members with names, organisers, rally participants | nobody: the choice is secret, only precinct totals are seen |
| what threatens | statuses, persecution, sometimes life | nothing: the payment is lawful, it cannot be seized or counted against a person (048m arts. 8, 9.3) |
| what it adds up to | statements of a body that can be ignored | three dimensions at once: a demand, a bill to the budget, the weight of those who stay (§4c) |
| what there is to crush | the body, the members, the network | nothing: a bank register and the arithmetic of shares |

The protocol does not make street protest safer and calls for nothing (1g) — it replaces it: disagreement is expressed not by going to the square but by a lawful secret choice that is counted and costs the government money. Protest stops being a feat and becomes an ordinary act open to everyone, including those who would never go into the street.

## 4e. "Smart voting" and continuous conversion

Shulman (thesis 19): Navalny thought politically — he continuously converted protest energy into electoral participation; "smart voting" is the most effective electoral tactic under unfreedom, it gathered the spilled water of discontent into one hose and could knock out almost any candidate (2018 — four regions lost governors, 2019 — the Moscow City Duma); it was stopped only by police terror and war.

The repository already makes the comparison: [056f §13i](../09-other-countries/056f-russia-september-2026.md) — "smart voting" needs coordination, carries the risk of association with an "extremist" structure, dies on voting day and is measured inside the government's count; the 2026 version bought 3–4 % reach and 0 % result, in Shulman's own words "one cannot speak of effectiveness". Her two assessments meet in one point: while there was an organisation, hands held the hose; when the hands were taken away, the hose fell (§2–4d).

The protocol is the same continuous conversion of discontent, but built into the rule: every "take" at every election is a demand, a bill and weight (§4c), with no leader, no app to block, no list of candidates, and it cannot be stopped by terror or by war, because it is a lawful payment from the budget. "Smart voting" knocked out a candidate; the protocol knocks out the base of a government that rested on ritual turnout.

**The architect.** "And all of this is logically reasonable and admissible, and Russia's leading social scientist of the last twenty years cannot understand it — strange." The answer is the series frame: not "cannot" but does not see — within the parameters of two thousand years of qualifications exit was a loss of the vote; the discipline counts votes one to one and puts no price on exit; what is new here is a combination of known parts (Hirschman's exit, the Olson inversion, Pacioli's double entry, the referendum), and such inventions are the hardest to see from inside a field.

## 5. What follows

| Premise | What is true | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| an institution is an organisation that outlives people | for organisations | the protocol is a basic, near-constitutional rule, hence a referendum (the architect's move) |
| the government crushes organisations, change is vulnerable | yes, for organisations | the protocol is supra-institutional and belongs to nobody; organisers are vulnerable but replaceable (the architect's move) |
| turnout must be predicted to understand society | no | turnout in an autocracy does not matter; the question is lifted to a referendum, turnout splits into two honest numbers (the architect's move) |

## Weak point

Shulman's quotations follow automatic subtitles. The reach estimates for "smart voting" come from open data (056f §13i).
