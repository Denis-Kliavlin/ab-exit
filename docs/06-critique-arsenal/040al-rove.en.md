## 1. Turnout wins elections — and you pay my voters to stay home

**Argument.** Rove's whole fame is the ground game: find your own, frighten them with the right issue, get them to the polls in 72 hours. The protocol removes this machine's product: the one who is brought in takes B.

**The blow.** Straight at 001b: if turnout is everything, a rule that lowers turnout loses to anyone who brings it in.

**Answer.** Rove's machine buys turnout with volunteers and "wedge issues" — the cheapest fear of the ten tricks (030b). The protocol puts a price on that bringing-in which a door-knock cannot outbid: money now, for a short horizon, is more reliable than duty later (013 §13.2, 040ac). And his own column of 2026 answers for us: the outcome is decided by independents and soft partisans, not the base. His machine mobilises the base; his column says the base does not decide. The protocol is his column, not his machine. The ground game does not vanish — it changes its product: "stay, because it is worth it" (040v item 1).

**Residual.** None.

## 2. Realignments are made by leaders and money, not rules: McKinley, 1896

**Argument.** Rove's book: the new majority of 1896 was built by persuasion, Hanna's money and an outreach to immigrant workers whom both parties had ignored. A rule persuades nobody and reaches out to no one.

**The blow.** At 019c (reform by subtraction): can an electorate be realigned by adding nothing?

**Answer.** 1896 was the first nationwide bringing-in: Hanna's $3.5 million on workers' turnout, the model of "the brought-in", not a refutation. Bought turnout is rented: Rove's own "permanent majority" (2004) lasted until 2006. The protocol realigns by subtraction (019c): those who had to be rented leave, those who do not stay, and the lease is not renewed every two years. And reaching out to those "both parties ignored" is exactly the protocol's move to the abandoned middle (040aj, "the catch"; 040ak §8, Bannon's aim): McKinley changed the mirror by purchase, the protocol by price.

**Residual.** None.

## 3. The apathetic are reachable: we find them by seventy traits

**Argument.** Micro-targeting proved that "non-voters" can be found and brought. The protocol assumes the apathetic is lost; he is not lost, he is not found.

**The blow.** At 1f §4: if the apathetic can be driven to the polls by data, why pay him to leave?

**Answer.** This is the campaign engineering of 013g §2: it got better at finding and bringing, not at governing. Whom Rove finds by seventy traits, the protocol finds by one number — and he takes B. Reachable by money to the polls, reachable by money to stay home, and the protocol pays more than the ride is worth. Micro-targeting does not refute the apathetic, it measured him: the one who is brought is the one who has to be driven.

**Residual.** None.

## 4. The protocol is asymmetric: whichever base takes B, that party loses forever — and the architect's move

**Argument.** Rove will count first: the old, the rural, the churchgoing vote out of duty and will not take; the young, the poor, the minorities will — a structural Republican advantage, and the Democrats will never agree. Or the reverse: frightened evangelicals take first. Either way the rule is not neutral, and one party will block it.

**The blow.** Rove's strongest, because it is not about the protocol but about adoption: the party that counts itself the loser will not allow the ballot line.

**Answer.** The matrix of 059g §4: both parties lose the brought-in, and nobody knows which loses more; the protocol does not see colour. The frightened take the money (040ac); duty keeps both rights (1g, "against pensioners"; 048m art. 16). And his own admission: if independents decide, then under the protocol they are the whole electorate, and the winner is not the party whose base took less but the one that addressed them first (040aj, "the catch"; 059g §4, the takeover).

**The architect's move.** "Record this: I had not even supposed that the protocol scrambles everything this much — who from which party will take the money, and how shaky and thin that line is, and how hard to predict. I thought the poor and the dependent would take. But that is not the question; the question is what the not-very-poor and not-very-dependent will do — and there are more of them." This removes Rove's argument more deeply than the matrix does. Asymmetry presumes that the takers are known in advance: the poor, the dependent, the brought-in. But they are a minority. The deciding group is the band around the median: not very poor, not very dependent, and the largest of all. For them D = p × M is a share of exactly their own income (048m art. 2): the number is set so that the choice is on the edge precisely for them. That edge is thin and shaky by construction, and it is not partisan: the band around the median cuts across both parties — Lipset's "cross-pressured" (040ac item 4) and the abandoned middle (040aj). So partisan asymmetry cannot be predicted — not for lack of data, but because the deciding group has no party sign. That band is what the thermostat calibrates (040v §6: the percentage settles where about half take) and what the architect's yardstick called "desirable for 40–50 % of the population" (1h). Rove asks whose base will take; the answer: not a base but the middle, and the middle is nobody's.

**The assistant's estimate — a guess until the pilot.** The band around the median will split not by party or income but by horizon. Within the band income changes D by tens of percent, not by multiples; what decides is whether the person has a tomorrow he wants to vote for: a mortgage, children, a business, a move. The takers will be the meek (money is the first offer without fear), people with a settled life and no plans, and part of the pensioners around the median (relative to a pension D is larger — if an asymmetry exists anywhere, it is by age, not party, and it hits the Republicans' old base, not the Democrats' poor base). Those who stay: the young with a long horizon (the strongest filter group, 040ai §4), families with children at school, and the returning burned. Numbers: in the first cycle the band takes 55–60 % (the money is new), by the third 45–50 %; the partisan split within the band is within five points of even. A 70:30 split would mean p was set off the band, and the thermostat corrects it within a cycle. The shakiness of the line is not a flaw but a sign that the price stands in the right place: a predictable line would mean D is too small (only the destitute take) or too large (everyone takes). The first thing the pilot (048l) should measure is not "how many took" but "who in the band around the median took, and why"; the instrument is Dalton's two axes (040aj §6) plus a question about horizon.

**Along and across — the architect's move.** "I see you have understood that the number cuts across the parties: not along but across, and all at once. And understood how convenient it is to change everything at once with the payment number." All party instruments cut the electorate along the left–right axis, and every cut goes to someone: districting, ranked-choice voting, primaries, the roll (059i §5). The number D cuts across, by horizon, and the cut goes to nobody, because the band around the median exists in every party, every state, every age. Three consequences. *One lever for everything:* a ten-point shift of p moves the line simultaneously for both parties, in all states and ages, in one direction; no other reform changes the composition of the electorate with one number — all the others change it piecemeal and by the hand of one side. This is "the scale with one beam" (1h). *The lever cannot be captured:* p has no party owner — a party that shifts p in its own favour shifts it in the other's by the same amount; the only one with an interest in touching p is the median band itself, and it touches it by referendum (048m art. 14); the regulator is in the hands of those it regulates — which is why the thermostat works (040v §6). *The cut does not become a split:* one cuts along to get one's own and the others'; cutting across yields neither a "party of takers" nor a "party of stayers" — the choice is secret (art. 9.3), the takers are scattered across all camps, no tribe can be made of this line. That is the answer to Bannon (040ak §7.1) without a word about Bannon.

**Residual.** Rove is right in one thing: the party that counts itself the loser will block. But it will not be able to count — precisely because the line is thin — and it stays in the matrix of 059g §4 without a good move.

## 5. Money and power: whoever holds the budget sets D for the election, and we outbid with advertising

**Argument.** Rove is a super-PAC man: the incumbent will time D to the election or tweak the median, and the opposition will answer with hundreds of millions on advertising, and the money machine beats the rule.

**The blow.** At the stability of the number (040v §6) and at "money is unlimited" (059g §4, Bellotti).

**Answer.** D = p × M from the tax base (the fifteenth decision, 048m art. 2 part 2): to inflate M is to raise taxes, to understate it is to lose revenue; p changes only by referendum (art. 14); there is no room for an election-timed manoeuvre. And advertising without bringing-in loses its product: Rove himself writes in 2026 that ads and rallies "will not win over voters" — that is the middle that judges by substance (1f §6b). Money does not vanish, it changes its product (059g §4): the super-PAC that bought turnout will buy arguments.

**Residual.** None.

## 6. What Rove gives the protocol

*A precedent.* 1896: an electorate was realigned, so the mirror can be changed; McKinley changed it by purchase, the protocol by subtraction.

*An admission.* The man who spent thirty years building the base machine writes in 2026 that the middle decides. That is 1f §6b from the builder of bringing-in.

*The map against the protocol.* His column "gerrymandering isn't enough" is 059g §6 in his own words: districting does not save a party whose base is leaving.

## Weak point

Rove has not written about the price of the vote; the five arguments are reconstructed from the craft. The 2004 figures are from standard accounts of the campaign; the McKinley book was read through reviews, not in full; the 2026 columns are known from press retellings and should be checked against the WSJ. The architect's move in §4 is a consequence that was not in the plan (1i): the thinness of the line around the median makes the partisan balance of takers unpredictable, and no pilot has yet measured it.
