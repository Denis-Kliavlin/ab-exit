*How to read. Carville is a technologist of one rule: elections are won by whoever talks about the economy more simply and more loudly. His arguments are simple, and that is not a weakness but a method: a technologist optimises not truth but the sale to the hall (030b). So he strikes not at the protocol's premise, as the arsenal's philosophers do, but at its sale — and his one argument that is hard to remove is also about the sale: who will buy the rule if his party counts itself the loser (040al §4).*

## 1. "It's the economy, stupid": elections are about prices, not procedures

**Argument.** Nobody votes for a rule. A procedural reform is the very "constipation" in place of a message; the voter wants higher wages and lower prices, not charter articles.

**The blow.** At 048n: how to sell a referendum on a rule if elections are always about the economy?

**Answer.** D is the most economic message possible: a number in every pocket. To the voter the protocol is not a procedure but a cheque, and the ballot line literally says "it's the economy" — D = p × M (048m art. 15). By Carville's own rule the economy issue belongs to whoever puts the number on the ballot first — that is the takeover of 059g §4 in his words. Lawyers read the charter; the voter reads the sum.

**Residual.** None.

## 2. One line repeated a thousand times: the protocol cannot be explained in a sentence

**Argument.** Message discipline is his whole science. "Paid not to vote" kills the protocol on the first word, and nobody listens to a three-paragraph explanation.

**The blow.** At the first reading (001d) — the most frequent blow in the arsenal.

**Answer.** The 1992 sign had three lines; the protocol has one: "the vote is worth nothing — today" (039b §4b), and the number explains itself. The first reading is dangerous, and 1d and 1g exist for it; but Carville himself proved that a line beats an explanation — and the protocol has the line, while the explanation has a website.

**Residual.** The first reading stays first; the defence works for whoever reaches the line.

## 3. The takers are mine: you buy off my rage

**Argument.** The poor, the young, the minorities, the "economic rage" he wants to raise for the midterms — exactly those who will take B. The protocol pays his coalition not to be angry at the polls.

**The blow.** At 1f §4: is the Democratic coalition the apathetic?

**Answer.** Rage is the fuel of the first move; it produces the infrastructure (059g §4). Today rage has one outlet: vote for his party once and wait. The protocol gives rage a price and a weight: the taker got money instead of a promise, the stayer a share. The band around the median (040al §4) — not very poor, not very dependent — is his target audience, and it is not partisan; a "platform of rage" under the protocol must be costed against D (040v item 1), the one honest test of whether he has a measure or only a message. If his coalition takes B, he learns who it was (040ak item 3).

**Residual.** None.

## 4. "Play dead": the pendulum returns by itself

**Argument.** The strategy is to let the opponent collapse; the protocol breaks the pendulum every opposition strategist lives on.

**The blow.** At 039b: the populist cycle is the pendulum, and Carville counts on it.

**Answer.** The pendulum is the apathetic swinging from fatigue to fatigue (040aj item 4), not a mandate. *40 More Years* lasted a year because a bought majority is rented — the same lesson as Rove's (040al item 2). The protocol replaces the pendulum with a thermostat with memory: a government is removed for what it did, not for being tiresome, and a party that can only wait for the other's fall is not needed at all under the protocol (059g §4, the matrix: waiting is a slow sale of position).

**Residual.** None.

## 5. The register: the protocol sounds like an elite scheme to "pay the plebs"

**Argument.** The Democrats lost the workers with a "preachy" tone. The protocol in that tone is "we will pay you not to interfere" — poison for those he wants back.

**The blow.** At the register (040ab).

**Answer.** Mudde's test — "punishment or price". The Kansas farmer on diesel (059j §3) understands a cheque faster than a sermon, and the protocol speaks to him in a figure, not a tone. Carville is right that the register decides — and that is an argument for the protocol: a number has no tone.

**Residual.** None.

## 6. What Carville gives the protocol

*Honesty.* To him the party is the economy and nothing else; the protocol makes that mandatory for all parties at once (040v item 1: programmes against the number).

*An admission.* "Play dead" — the party has no product except the opponent's failure; that is the fork in which the one who takes over wins.

*A second proof of renting.* *40 More Years* after Rove's "permanent majority": both strategists promised a generation and got a cycle.
