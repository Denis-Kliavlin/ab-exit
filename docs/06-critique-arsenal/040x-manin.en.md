# 40x. Bernard Manin: *The Principles of Representative Government* — Five Arguments on Elections as Aristocracy and on the Voter's Memory

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File:** 06_040x · v1 · 12 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's assignment of 11 October 2026: "each — five arguments from his book, separately". The sixth is Manin: *The Principles of Representative Government* (1997). A reconstruction from the text; the architect's move on the fifth argument is recorded as a move.

---

*How to read. Manin is a historian of representation: he showed that elections are an aristocratic, not a democratic, mechanism, and that representative government has passed through three forms — parliamentary, party and "audience". He neither criticises nor cures; he describes what elections actually do. For the protocol this is a test along an axis the other five lack: not "who votes" but "what choosing is at all".*

## 1. Elections are an aristocratic, not a democratic, mechanism

**The argument.** Athens filled offices by lot; elections by definition select those the voter considers best, that is, unequal. Representative government is a mixed form: aristocratic in its method of selection, democratic in that everyone selects.

**The strike.** The protocol shifts further towards the elite: those vote who do not need the money.

**The answer.** Elections are aristocratic anyway — Manin proved it, he did not assume it; the protocol changes not the principle but the composition of the selectors, and makes it visible: a counter in place of the fiction "the people elected" (040u item 2, equality among those who cast). Those vote not "who do not need money" but those to whom the vote is worth more than the payment — and at a sum desirable to 40–50 % of the population that is an elite not by income but by horizon (036e §6; 040p §1, the threshold for selection by income). The lot as an honest alternative remains: the repository is about a different axis and does not reject it.

## 2. The four principles of representative government

**The argument.** Representatives are elected periodically; they retain independence from voters' instructions; the governed are free to express opinions; decisions pass the test of discussion. All three historical forms kept these four principles.

**The strike.** The protocol touches none of them.

**The answer.** True — and it is a strength, not a weakness: one line on top of all four, repealing nothing (040o §3, minimal launch; 040t item 4). The protocol is compatible with any of Manin's forms because it changes not the design of representation but the price of taking part in it.

## 3. "Audience democracy"

**The argument.** Party democracy gave way to a democracy of spectators: the voter reacts to the candidate's image rather than the programme, decides by the media and at the last moment; parties became the candidate's instrument rather than the reverse. Manin described this in 1997.

**The strike.** The protocol does nothing about the media and the image.

**The answer.** Manin's spectator is Przeworski's layer (040v item 1), described twenty years before the populists: decides last, by image, with a short attention span. The protocol does not fix the media — it takes the spectator out of the hall for a payment and leaves the one who came with a question; audience democracy remains the form, but the audience changes, and an image designed for the spectator stops paying off.

## 4. Periodicity is the only real accountability

**The argument.** The representative is not bound by instructions, and the only bridle is the next election: the voter judges in hindsight.

**The strike.** The one who took the payment takes no part in accountability.

**The answer.** He takes part with money: his non-participation costs the budget, and that is a line the government sees next to the contracts (040t item 2; 040o). And judgement in hindsight becomes stricter, because those who stayed are the judges — see item 5.

## 5. The representative is free of instructions — and therefore can betray

**The argument.** Freedom from instructions is a principle, not a defect; but it also means that what was promised before the election does not bind after it.

**The strike.** The protocol does not prevent betrayal.

**The answer — the architect's move.** It does not prevent it, but it changes whom one betrays and how it ends. "Reputation becomes very important for the politician. The apathetic with a short memory have left, and the clever and the remembering have stayed. They will not forget what the representative said before the election, how he behaved and what he did — and at the second election they will certainly remind him of all of it and check." Manin's bridle — repeated elections — works only with a voter's memory; the apathetic's short memory is the betrayer's resource: he promised, did not deliver, and four years later nobody remembered, because those who could remember were burned long ago and at home (1f §6). The protocol removes the forgetful and keeps the remembering: betraying a thinking voter with a ×3 vote costs more than betraying a brought-in one — not because he is more easily offended but because he will come the second time with the same question. Reputation turns from a word in a programme into an asset checked at the second election, and Manin's fourth principle — the test of discussion — gets those who discuss with memory. Freedom from instructions remains; cheap it ceases to be.

## Summary

| Manin's argument | What is true | Answer | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| elections are aristocratic | yes, proven | the protocol changes the composition, not the principle; makes it visible; selection by horizon, not income | the income-selection threshold — 040p §1 |
| the four principles | yes | none touched — one line on top | — |
| audience democracy | yes, it is the layer | the spectator leaves for a payment, the one with a question stays | the protocol does not touch the media |
| periodicity as accountability | yes | non-participation costs the budget; judgement in hindsight is stricter | — |
| freedom from instructions → betrayal | yes | one betrays the remembering; reputation becomes an asset checked at the second election (the architect's move) | — |

## Weak point

A reconstruction; Manin wrote about forms of representation, not turnout, and his book ends in 1997 — before the populists and before "audience democracy" became a diagnosis. The architect's move on memory is a thesis: that those who stay remember better than those who leave follows from the definition of horizon but has not been measured, and the first check is only the pilot's second election, that is, two cycles (048l). And the first argument remains the place where Manin would strike hardest: if the payment is taken out of poverty rather than horizon, the protocol is aristocracy in his exact sense.
