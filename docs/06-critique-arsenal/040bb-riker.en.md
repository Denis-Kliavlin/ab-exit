William Riker, *Liberalism against Populism: A Confrontation between the Theory of Democracy and the Theory of Social Choice* (1982); *The Art of Political Manipulation* (1986). The social-choice angle: by Arrow, elections do not express a "popular will," and the only defensible function of voting is to remove rulers. The protocol is a purely Rikerian instrument: it adds nothing to the "will" and sharpens removal. Written without a move by the architect.

---

*How to read. Riker separated two understandings of democracy: the populist — elections express the will of the people — and the liberal — elections allow the ruler to be removed. Arrow's theorem kills the first; the second survives. The chapter shows that the protocol stands wholly on the liberal side, and that the share of takers is not an aggregate of preferences but a count of individual acts with money on a binary choice, where there are no cycles.*

## 0. What he claims

Arrow's theorem: with three or more alternatives there is no aggregation rule that satisfies minimal requirements of reasonableness; the result depends on the rule and the agenda, not on the "will." Hence the populist reading — "the people have decided" — is empty; any decision could have been changed by reordering the questions. The liberal reading is more modest and more robust: elections are a way of removing those who rule from time to time; their value is in the threat of removal, not in the expression of will. Hence "heresthetics": the politician's art is the manipulation of the agenda and of the dimensions of choice.

## 1. There is no popular will; the protocol promises to "hear" it more exactly — it promises the impossible

**Argument.** Self-selection does not make the aggregate meaningful; "those who stayed have decided" is as empty as "the people have decided."

**Answer.** The protocol does not promise to hear the will. Its promise is a different, Rikerian one: a government that cannot be bought is easier to remove. Nowhere does the repository say "the people have decided"; it says "the government is the predictable answer to the composition of the hall" (1g "This is populism", 040ab), and it changes the composition, not the interpretation. The aggregate remains what it was; what changes is the price of buying it.

**Residual.** None.

## 2. The aggregate is meaningless with three or more alternatives; the choice "take or stay" is a third alternative, and cycles will begin

**Argument.** To two candidates the protocol adds "take"; three alternatives are Arrow's field.

**Answer.** "Take" is not a third alternative in the same vote; it is a binary choice before the vote, with a price, and a separate count (048m arts. 9, 10). A binary choice with a price does not cycle: it has no third option, and the preference "D or the vote" is transitive by construction. The vote among those who stay remains the same rule as before, with all its Arrovian properties — the protocol neither worsens them nor promises to improve them. Two counts, not one aggregate.

**Residual.** None.

## 3. The only function is removal; the protocol reduces the number of those who remove

**Argument.** Removal works when the removers are many; fewer voters — a weaker threat.

**Answer.** The threat of removal is strong not by number but by not being bought. Today the removers are many, but half were brought in (030b, 057d §3b), and the threat does not work: a government on bought turnout is not afraid (013g §3b). Under the protocol the removers are fewer, but not one is bought, each is ×2, and they remember a cycle (040at §2). And the protocol adds a second removal signal Riker did not have: the share of takers between elections — the government's quotation, removal without an election (013g §3b, the thermostat of 040aj item 4).

**Residual.** None.

## 4. Agenda manipulation: whoever sets the question wins; the percentage at the referendum is a field for heresthetics

**Argument.** Word the ballot differently and you get a different result; a referendum on the rule is as manipulable as any.

**Answer.** True of any referendum, and the protocol has one answer: the ballot line is fixed by the charter and changed neither by a commission nor by a council (048m art. 15; the decision not to change it after the Luntz audit, 040an §6). The question is single and binary — the percentage; there are no dimensions of choice to spin. Heresthetics works on a multidimensional field; the protocol reduces the field to one number. What remains manipulable is the time and place of the referendum, and that is closed by art. 14: the rule changes only by referendum, not by whoever sets the agenda.

**Residual.** None.

## 5. The government's quotation is a populist reading of an aggregate: the share of takers "means" something only to whoever wants to see it

**Argument.** You read a number as a will: "more take — the government is worse." That is populism with a calculator.

**Answer.** The share of takers is not an aggregate of preferences but a sum of individual acts with money. Each act means one thing, verifiably: to this person D is worth more than this vote. The sum of such acts is a price, not a will — as a share price is not "the market's opinion of the director" but a sum of trades. Riker rejected reading an aggregate as a will; a price is not a will, and he used prices himself. And the quotation decides nothing — it removes nobody, appoints nobody; it is only visible (013g §3b: an external indicator that cannot be drawn). The decision is still the vote of those who stay, by his own liberal rule.

**Residual.** None.

## 6. What he gives the protocol

A side: the protocol is a liberal instrument in his sense, and that lifts the charge of populism more precisely than Mudde (040ab). A distinction that was missing: two counts, not one aggregate, and a binary choice with a price does not cycle. And a name for the share of takers — a price, not a will.
