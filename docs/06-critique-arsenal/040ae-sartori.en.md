*How to read. Sartori is the twentieth century's chief opponent of referendum democracy, and the protocol is introduced, repealed and calibrated only by referendum. He is the only one recorded who strikes not at the payment but at the way it is adopted. The question to him: for which question is a referendum bad — any, or a complex one?*

## 1. Referendum democracy is zero-sum without deliberation

**The argument.** A referendum gives "yes/no" to a question someone wrote, without compromise; the majority wins completely, the minority loses completely; the same incompetent mass decides ("demo-ignorance"). Representation is needed as a filter of deliberation between question and answer; the referendum removes that filter. Switzerland is an exception resting on the rarity of questions and on culture.

**The strike.** The protocol stands on the referendum entirely (048m Arts. 3, 15): you took democracy's worst instrument and made it the only one, and the question will be written by the same government that brings in the apathetic.

**The answer by design.** The protocol's referendum is not an instrument of rule but a lock: it decides neither budget nor war — only the rule on the price of non-participation, and precisely because that is the one rule that cannot be entrusted to those elected under it (040u item 4; 029 §29.11, a judge in his own cause). Representation remains entire (040x item 2); one item is taken from it — the right to write the electoral law for itself. There is no zero sum: the losing minority loses nothing — if the rule is adopted, the dissenter may take the payment or vote; if rejected, everything stays as it was; it is a referendum after which both sides get what they chose. And the "incompetent mass" self-selects by the very question: whoever came to vote on a rule about the price of the vote showed that the vote is worth more to him than the payment.

**The architect's move — and it agrees with Sartori in order to turn him over.** "Sartori is right that complex questions with nuances make no sense at a referendum. But the protocol is very simple — and it is the last referendum on complex questions that need long discussion. After the protocol an intelligent government will discuss everything at elections and in parliament. Sartori saw the referendum as an alternative to stupid populist government. The referendum is needed as a lock and a bridge between the present situation and the protocol." Sartori is right about the complex, and the protocol does not contradict him but carries him out: the protocol's question is the simplest possible — "whether to pay for non-participation and how much", a yes/no and a number; it has no nuances, nothing to deliberate, something to measure (the counter). Sartori feared the referendum rightly but for the wrong case: he saw in it a replacement for representation under a government incapable of deliberation — and under such a government the referendum is indeed its instrument (040w item 4, Orbán). The protocol uses the referendum once and for one thing: to pass from a government incapable of deliberation, because elected by a short horizon (040w item 5), to a government capable of it — and after that the complex returns to where it belongs, elections and parliament. It is the last referendum on the complex because it is the only one after which the complex no longer needs a referendum. A lock — so that the government cannot repeal it; a bridge — to cross; after that the bridge is not needed.

**Residual.** Sartori is right that the government writes the question, and in an autocracy a referendum on the protocol will not take place (040w item 4) — an argument about the route, not about the referendum. And right that a percentage chosen by referendum is a number without deliberation: the charter (048m Art. 3) answers with revision by the same path and the counter until revision; deliberation is replaced by measurement, and for a simple question that is the architect's move — measure the simple, deliberate the complex.

## 1b. Addendum of 11 November 2026: Shulman on the referendum — a question can be zero-sum, a referendum cannot

The same blow thirty-nine years after Sartori, in seven arguments. E. Shulman, *Status* of 10 November 2026 (BILD in Russian, 55:14–57:55), answers a question about the Czech bill on referendums (initiative at 300,000 signatures, binding at a turnout of at least 35 %, EU and NATO membership, defence and the constitutional order excluded). Her arguments are paraphrased; the answers follow what the repository already records, except for two of the architect's moves.

| № | Shulman's argument | What is true | Answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A referendum is a zero-sum game: one position wins, the other loses; parliamentary elections are not, everyone shares the seats | For a question of policy — true | Seats are shared by parties, not voters: one whose candidate lost gets nothing, and in majoritarian systems elections are pure zero sum ([040i §1.1](040i-linz-shulman.md), Linz). The main point is the architect's move below: zero sum is a property of the question, not of the form |
| 2 | A turnout threshold is necessary, otherwise "the active majority decides for everyone else" | For free voting — true: the "active" there are the brought | A threshold gives those who did not come a free "no": a vote without price and without presence, the freest of all; by the [accounting standard](040br-ten-propaganda-devices.md) it is an off-balance item counted as a posted entry. The rule has no quorum ([048m art. 15](../08-implementation/048m-charter.md), [1g](../01-introduction/001g-objections-index.md)): one who did not come is not silent, he chose D, and it is recorded |
| 3 | "The autocrats' favourite toy": "yes" is the position of the question's author, "no" has no shades, a negative campaign is hard to run | True | The autocrat asks about himself; the rule asks about money for the voter, and that question the autocrat will never put ([non-political judo](040bs-paine.md)). "No" here has a shade — the number p. In an autocracy a referendum on the rule will not take place — an argument about the route (the remainder of §1) |
| 4 | Taxes, foreign policy and security are the competence of central government, not of a referendum | True, and the repository agrees | The rule takes one item from the elected — the right to write the electoral law for themselves ([029 §29.11](029-hypocrisy.md), a judge in his own case); the rest stays with parliament — the architect's move in §1: the last referendum on the complex |
| 5 | Democracy is the distribution of power and an obstacle to its concentration; a referendum gives an unambiguous answer and therefore concentrates | The definition is right | The unambiguous answer here is a lock on concentration: the elected lose the key to the electoral law, and every voter gets a choice every cycle. The distribution of power is literal, not a metaphor |
| 6 | Half disagrees — what will that half do? | True for a question of policy | The architect's move: today the half that did not come has every moral right to say "it was not we who chose them"; after the rule the taker can say he was forced, but this logic breaks almost completely, the energy is channelled, and the excuse remains only to the destitute ([1i](../01-introduction/001i-consequences.md), [040i §2](040i-linz-shulman.md)) |
| 7 | Switzerland is "one of a kind" | Yes | The repository does not argue from Switzerland; here the positions coincide |

**The architect's move — zero sum belongs to the question, not to the referendum.** "A question can be zero-sum, but a referendum cannot. So the AB-EXIT question is not a zero-sum question but a balance with mutual exclusion: either the money or the vote — not 'shall we hand out money' or 'shall we only increase the vote'."

This is more precise than the answer in §1 ("there is no zero sum"), because it names what can have one. The "yes/no" form has no sum; the question does. "Shall we hand out money" is a zero-sum question: payers against recipients, and the working vote it down ([021](../04-electoral-dynamics/021-centrist-void.md), UBI). "Shall we only increase the vote" of those who come is zero-sum too: the weight of some grows at the expense of others. The rule's question is neither: everyone is offered a choice, and the mutual exclusion — one cannot take both the money and the vote — makes it a balance in the accounting sense: the taker books the money and gives up the vote, the stayer books the weight and gives up the money, and each person's sum balances by itself. That is why the referendum on the rule has no loser under any outcome: "yes" gives everyone a choice, "no" leaves everything as it was. Shulman's zero-sum argument is right and strikes exactly the two questions the rule does not ask.

**The lot.** In the same broadcast (57:55–59:34) she asks not to despise the lot: chance among an already selected pool "removes the unhealthy passions inherent in elections"; elections are the best thing invented, but mechanical rotation is useful. The architect's observation is recorded in [040i §2](040i-linz-shulman.md): she is for elections but not for the referendum, and considers a lot among the already elected more honest than elections — in both cases she does not trust voters 100 % but cannot say so. The pool she trusts, the last step she does not, because the passions are exactly there. This is Landemore's position, and the architect's move on it is already recorded: "a steam engine for want of a combustion engine" ([040au §3](040au-landemore.md)). The fork: accept it whole — the voter cannot be trusted with "yes/no" or with the last step, but can be trusted with selection. Then the distrust attaches not to the voter but to the fact that in the decisive step the vote costs nothing: "passions" and "the active majority" are properties of the free vote. Two ways out: take the voter out of the decisive step (threshold, lot — her way, which cannot be named aloud, because "the voter cannot be trusted" is the autocrat's thesis) or take the free ride out of the decisive step. Both her devices are substitutes for the price she does not name.

**Remainder.** The honest limit is the same as with Sartori: a free hall will come to the referendum on the rule too, the price appears after adoption; the answer is a fork for the turnout machine ([1g](../01-introduction/001g-objections-index.md): "they will not come to the referendum"), not a denial. Arguments 2 and 5 are answered through the accounting standard — the assistant's construction, not Shulman's words.

## 2. Demo-ignorance

**The argument** (ch. 5). Democracy presupposes public opinion, and the majority has none — it has a mood, assembled from what it was shown; the "informed voter" is a fiction, and the more decisions are handed to the mass directly, the more ignorance decides. The mass may be admitted to choosing *who*, not *what*; democracy must be a "democracy of knowledge" at least at the point of selection.

**The strike.** The protocol hands the mass the question *what* (whether to pay and how much), and the same ignorant will come to decide it.

**The answer.** Sartori described the same figure as Dahl (040u item 3) and Lipset (040ac item 1): the voter without an opinion, with a mood. The apathetic has no opinion, but he has the price of his own vote, and that is the one thing he is exactly informed about (040q). The referendum on the protocol is not "what the country should do" but "what your vote is worth to you" — the one *what* question that ignorance answers competently, because it is about oneself. After it, *who* is decided by a composition from which ignorance has left for a payment: Sartori wanted to separate the knowing at selection — the protocol lets the unknowing separate themselves.

**The architect's move — why there will be more knowledge.** The first version of the answer ended in a concession: "Sartori wanted more knowledge, and that the protocol does not give." The architect: "re-read the repo — that is exactly what the protocol gives. The one who took the money but wants to change the government and the world will know that it is quite possible, and every time he will think and weigh — to take or not; and he may not take, and then all his wishes are expressed in the vote, he will propose innovations and his candidate will win. This becomes more real than now — so the wish to learn and find out will grow, and knowledge will grow. Because knowledge is now needed and can be used usefully and more effectively." Sartori's knowledge does not grow not because there is nowhere to get it but because nobody needs it: with two cancelling cores and the layer (040v item 1) the vote changes nothing, and learning for it is a waste; this is Downs's "rational ignorance" (013 §13.2), and Sartori described its consequence, not its cause. The protocol changes not access to knowledge but the demand for it: the taker sees on the counter that change is possible (040aa item 5), and at every election weighs anew (048m Art. 5 part 2) — to take, or to express everything in a vote with a share; and when the vote really chooses, knowledge for the first time has a use and pays off. Knowledge grows where it is needed. Sartori looked for a pedagogy of knowledge — the protocol gives its economics, and economics is more reliable: it needs no teacher, only a buyer.

**Residual.** Sartori would not accept "price instead of opinion" in the referendum on the rule itself — for him that is surrender to ignorance in bookkeeping form. But his main demand — that there be more knowledge — the protocol meets not by enlightenment but by demand; and it is tested by the pilot: does the share of those who return grow from the second cycle (048l).

## 3. Polarised pluralism

**The argument** (*Parties and Party Systems*, ch. 6). With five or more parties and anti-system flanks, competition becomes centrifugal: parties run from the centre to the edges, the centre empties, and the system works for an "irresponsible opposition" — the flanks promise everything because they will never govern. Weimar, the Fourth Republic, Italy in the 1970s.

**The strike.** The protocol removes from the polls those who are brought — and they are brought to the centre and to power (the core); the flanks rest on the committed, who will not take the payment. The protocol strips the centre and strengthens the flanks.

**The answer.** Sartori's centre empties not because centrists are few but because they are burned — nobody fights for them when the outcome is decided by two brought-in cores and the layer (040v item 1). The protocol brings them back with a vote that has a share (1f §6) — the only offer that fills the centre not with a party but with voters. The flanks: the committed fanatic will not take the payment — true, but fanatics are few; the flank's numbers come from the same brought-in and frightened as the core's (1f §2c: the core is the dependent, not the fanatics; 040w item 3: hysteria is for sale). The protocol takes the mass from the flank and leaves it the committed — and that is the size at which a flank is safe. Sartori's centrifugality is the product of an irresponsible opposition that can promise everything; under the protocol promises must be made to those who stayed and remember (040x item 5), and promising everything stops paying off.

**Residual.** Sartori measured polarisation by the number of parties and ideological distance; the protocol changes neither — it changes who stands behind them. That the flanks lose mass rather than the centre is the thesis of 1f §2c and 040w item 3; the first measurement will come from a pilot asking the takers "whom would you have voted for" (048l).

## 4. Demo-power and demo-protection

**The argument.** Two sides of democracy: "demo-protection" — that the people are not oppressed, and "demo-power" — that the people rule. The first is attainable and attained: rights, courts, rotation. The second is a myth: the people do not rule and cannot, the elected rule; to demand demo-power is to shake demo-protection for the unattainable. Hence Sartori's minimalism, close to Schumpeter (040q) and Przeworski (040v).

**The strike.** The protocol is a demo-power project: "the one with something to lose will decide" — that is, part of the people will rule better. You shake a working protection for a myth.

**The answer.** The protocol is not demo-power but demo-protection, and that must be said plainly. It does not let the people rule: representation remains entire (040x item 2), the elected rule. It protects the people from one oppression Sartori did not name in 1987: the free use of their vote by those who bring them (029 §29.11 — "convenient when everyone guards for free what only you can use"). Rights, courts and rotation protect from the state as force; the protocol — from the state as employer and transporter. It is an extension of demo-protection into the area where it did not work, and no existing protection is touched (048m Art. 16).

**Sartori's continuation and the architect's move.** Sartori would ask: if this is protection, why the amplified vote of those who stay — that is already power. The architect: "the growth of the vote with the number of takers and the reverse is a perfect balance: on any shift both arms move at once, and in that lies the mathematical exactness and balance of the protocol." It is a scale with one beam: one arm is the takers, the other those who stay, and they cannot move separately. Every taker at once reduces the number of voters and increases the share of every remaining vote; every returner does the reverse (1d, statement 12; 040aa item 5). There is no moment when one arm has moved and the other not yet, because these are not two rules but one quantity: the share of a vote equals one divided by the number of those who stay. So nobody assigns "power to those who stay" — it cannot be assigned: it equals exactly how many left, and returns exactly as they return. The protocol is not calibrated for power — it is self-balancing; the amplification is not a design but an identity. That is the answer to Sartori's suspicion: demo-protection from dilution, with no separate lever by which it could be made into demo-power.

**Residual.** None by mechanism. By terminology — Sartori would leave the word "protection" in question: a protection that changes the share of a vote did not occur in his vocabulary; but neither was the dilution of a vote by the brought-in called oppression in it.

## 5. Vertical democracy: selection, not participation

**The argument** (ch. 6). Democracy is not the horizontal equality of participants but a vertical: the people *select* the rulers, and the quality of democracy equals the quality of selection. "Selective polyarchy" — the best should rise; a democracy that selects the worst is democracy in form but degenerate. Equality of votes does not guarantee the quality of the selected: the lower the average voter, the lower the selected.

**The strike.** The protocol does nothing about selection: candidates are nominated by the same parties, there are no filters at the entrance (1f §4 — no exam for politicians), and those who stay choose from the same menu.

**The answer.** Sartori is right in the diagnosis, and the repository recorded it before him: 1f §4b — five steps to an unfit president, "fitness has no buyer". The protocol puts no filter on the candidate — it creates a buyer of fitness. Today two cores and the layer select: the core needs its own, the layer needs an image (040x item 3), and neither needs a fit one; the party nominates whoever passes with those two. Under the protocol nominations must be made for those who stay — those who remember (040x item 5) and count beyond the cycle (1f §7); the menu changes not because it was filtered but because the customer of the menu changed. Sartori's vertical works from the bottom up: the quality of the selected equals the quality of the selectors, and the protocol changes exactly the bottom end of the vertical — the only one that can be changed without an examiner (040aa item 3, against epistocracy).

**Residual.** The menu does not change in the first cycle: the party learns of the new customer from the result of the first election under the protocol and nominates differently at the second (040v item 1, weak point). And Sartori would ask who guarantees that those who stay select the *best* rather than *their own*; the answer is the same as today: nobody — with the difference that those who stay have something to lose from their own if he is bad.

## Weak point

A reconstruction; Sartori wrote against the referendum as a *regime*, and the transfer to a single lock-referendum is ours. "The last referendum on the complex" is a forecast about the behaviour of a long-horizon government, not a rule of the charter: that it will deliberate the complex in parliament follows from the composition but is not guaranteed by it; and the charter keeps the referendum for revising the percentage (Art. 3) — so not single but rare, and Sartori would ask where the boundary of rarity lies.
