# 40t. Arend Lijphart: *Unequal Participation* — Five Arguments on Low Turnout and Compulsory Voting Against a Price for the Vote

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File:** 06_040t · v1 · 11 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's assignment of 11 October 2026: "each — five arguments from his book, separately". The second is Lijphart: *Patterns of Democracy* (1999, 2nd ed. 2012) and the American Political Science Association presidential address "Unequal Participation: Democracy's Unresolved Dilemma" (1996, published 1997). A reconstruction from the texts; where he would object, the objection is recorded before the answer.

---

*How to read. Lijphart is the only one of the five who has his own remedy for the same disease the protocol treats: low and unequal turnout. His remedy is compulsory voting; ours is payment for non-turnout. The same disease, opposite instruments: oblige or pay. That makes him the protocol's mirror twin, and the comparison with him the cleanest possible.*

## 1. Low turnout is systematically unequal

**The argument.** The poor, the young and the less educated do not vote; the well-off, the old and the educated do. A turnout of 50 % is not half of society but its upper half. He gathered this across every democracy with data and called it "the unresolved dilemma".

**The strike.** None — this is the repository's diagnosis: 44 % of the votes with those who give 11 % of the labour (1f §3), a core of 55–75 with below-median income but from the budget (1f §2). His cut is by income and education, ours by age and stake; it is one gap photographed from two sides. Coincidence, not dispute.

## 2. Unequal turnout yields unequal policy

**The argument.** Governments answer to those who vote. Where the poor do not vote, policy tilts towards the rich — taxes, spending, social programmes; he showed it across countries and American states.

**The strike.** If the poor take the payment more often (036e §6, the price of the menu; 040p §1), the protocol will strengthen the very tilt he measured.

**The answer.** Today's tilt is not towards the rich but towards the old and the budget-dependent: policy answers to the core that comes (1f §5, the closed cycle). And under the protocol non-turnout for the first time acquires a price in the budget: policy still answers to the vote, but the budget now answers to non-turnout too — the payment stands as a line beside the contracts (040o). Lijphart measured turnout's effect on policy; the protocol adds non-turnout's effect on the budget.

**Residual.** Selection by income is the repository's main open question, and Lijphart posed it before us: if the payment is taken from poverty rather than indifference, his tilt is strengthened. It is the first refuter in 040p, with a threshold.

## 3. The remedy — compulsory voting

**The argument.** Australia since 1924, Belgium, Luxembourg: turnout above 90 %, a small fine for abstention, the inequality of participation disappears. He called it "a simple and cheap" solution of the dilemma and the one reform that really works.

**The strike — the main one.** He has a working remedy with a century of data; we have a pilot that has not been run.

**The answer.** Compulsory voting and the protocol treat one disease with opposite means: a fine for non-turnout — and a payment for it. The fine brings to the polls those who do not care, and they vote at random or as told: in Australia spoilt and blank ballots run at about 5–6 %, and the "donkey vote" down the first column is known well enough that ballot order is drawn by lot. The payment takes out those who do not care and leaves the decision to those who do. His model maximises turnout; ours maximises stake. Both remove the tilt by income: his at the price of a random vote, ours at the price of an exit. And decisively: the fine is impossible where it is needed most — in an autocracy compulsory turnout is the dream of authorities who bus people in anyway (1g: "non-turnout is a crisis, therefore compulsory voting" — the prohibitive model of turnout). Lijphart's remedy works in healthy democracies and strengthens sick ones; the protocol's does the opposite (040m §5d, "the sick first").

## 4. Consensus democracy is better than majoritarian

**The argument** (*Patterns of Democracy*). Thirty-six countries: coalitions, proportionality, multi-party systems, federalism — "kinder and gentler": less inequality, higher turnout, more trust, no worse economically.

**The strike.** The protocol is a setting for any system; it does not touch majoritarianism. Lijphart would say: treat the electoral system, not the voter.

**The answer.** We do not compete: the protocol is compatible with any of his models and in a consensus one probably works more gently — fewer are brought in, more have something to lose in coalition bargaining. But his reform requires rewriting the constitution and the electoral law; ours is one line, by referendum, without repealing anything (040o §3, minimal launch; 039b §4b, "freedom plus one").

## 5. Turnout as the measure of democratic health

**The argument.** High turnout is legitimacy; falling turnout is alarm. His whole argument on the dilemma rests on turnout as the measure.

**The strike.** Under the protocol turnout falls by construction — by Lijphart that is disease.

**The answer.** This is the first misreading in 1d: turnout under the protocol is not comparable with turnout without it. Those who left are counted and paid — they did not "fail to come", they chose; the measure of health is not the share who came but the share who *decided*, and under the protocol it tends to one hundred: everyone either voted, or took, or consciously kept the vote (048m Art. 16). Lijphart measured turnout because he could not measure stake; the counter measures it.

## Summary

| Lijphart's argument | What is true | Answer | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| turnout is unequal | yes — our diagnosis from the other side | coincidence | — |
| unequal turnout → unequal policy | yes | today's tilt is to the old and the budget-paid; under the protocol non-turnout gets a price in the budget | selection by income — 040p §1 |
| compulsory voting | works in healthy democracies | the fine maximises turnout, the payment maximises stake; the fine brings a random vote; in an autocracy compulsion is the authorities' dream | he has a century of data, we have a pilot |
| the consensus model | yes, kinder | no competition; the protocol is one line, his reform a constitution | — |
| turnout as the measure of health | a measure for want of a better | the measure is the share who decided; the counter measures stake | — |

## Weak point

Lijphart is the only one in the series whose remedy has been tested in practice for a century, and the dispute with him is a pilot's dispute with data. The point about the random vote under compulsion is right, but Australian democracy lives with it and no worse than others; so the "random vote" is a price that can be paid, and the choice between his price and ours (the exit) is not logical but empirical. And he is right that selection by income is our unresolved dilemma, as unequal turnout is his.
