# 40as. Walzer and Sandel: Blocked Exchanges and "What Money Can't Buy" — Five Arguments: the Vote Is Not for Sale, the Poor Sell Under Need, Payment Corrupts the Good, Money as a Solvent, the Debate About Meaning

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites

*How to read. This is the moral accusation of day one: "auctioning democracy" (059l, stage 2) is Walzer in his own words. The chapter shows that his prohibition is violated today and enforced by the protocol, and that Sandel's two objections — fairness and corruption — are answered by one move: the price stands on the exit, not on the act.*

## 0. What they claim

**Walzer.** Justice is "complex equality": each sphere has its own principle of distribution, and tyranny begins when the good of one sphere — money — dominates another. Hence the list of "blocked exchanges", fourteen things that cannot be bought: human beings, criminal justice, freedoms, offices, exemptions from military service and jury duty, prizes, love — and, second on the list: "political power and influence: citizens cannot sell their votes or officials their decisions".

**Sandel.** Two objections to markets in certain goods. *Fairness:* under inequality the poor sell under the compulsion of need, and the choice is not free. *Corruption:* market valuation changes the good itself — "markets leave their mark". Examples: an Israeli day-care centre introduced a fine for parents arriving late — lateness increased, the fine turned into a fee and removed the shame; Titmuss showed that paid blood donors give worse blood than unpaid ones, because payment crowds out the motive.

## 1. Walzer: the vote is a blocked exchange, money must not enter the political sphere — and the architect's move: he is for the protocol

**Argument.** The protocol admits money into a sphere where it is forbidden; that is the dominance of money, tyranny in Walzer's sense.

**The assistant's answer.** The protocol does not sell the vote — nobody buys it. The test by Walzer — does power pass for money: no. The taker gets money, not power; the stayer gets weight, not money; there is no reverse path — a second vote cannot be bought for cash. The exchange runs one way, and money does not convert into influence.

**The architect's move.** "Here it is in fact all the other way round. De facto there already are hidden purchases and bribery. The protocol, by payments from the budget — which is essentially just a tax reduction — removes the bribery. The protocol is for Walzer, not against him." This changes not the answer but the sign of the row: Walzer is not an opponent but a witness for the prosecution against the present system. His prohibition is violated today, every election: the vote is bought with a bus (030b), a package of benefits, an indexation before the election, and parties are kept from the budget (040y, legalised bribery). The protocol removes this hidden bribery in the only way that works: it takes away its buyer. A payment from the budget is not money entering the sphere from outside but a tax rebate to everyone: the citizen's money returns to the citizen, and nobody acquires anything in the sphere for it (048m art. 4). After the protocol there is nothing left to buy in the political sphere: the apathetic's vote is not for sale, because it is not cast; the stayer's vote cannot be bought, because it is the vote of one who has already refused money. The protocol is the first mechanism that enforces Walzer's blocked exchange rather than declaring it.

**Residual.** None.

## 2. Sandel, fairness: the poor sell under the compulsion of need, the choice is not free

**Argument.** Under inequality an offer of money for non-participation is not a choice but compulsion: the poor "choose" the money because they cannot do otherwise.

**Answer.** Compulsion exists today, and a vote under pressure is worth −1, not 0 (040ac, the architect's move). The protocol gives the compelled an exit with money, in secret (048m art. 9.3): one can be forced neither to take nor not to take. D is not a handout but a share of the median, desirable for 40–50 % of the population (1h, the architect's yardstick). The fairness argument presumes that the poor man's vote is worth something today and is being taken cheaply; it is worth nothing (039b §4b) — it had one buyer, the populist, and he paid with other people's money.

**Residual.** None.

## 3. Sandel, corruption: paying for abstention turns a civic act into a commodity; the shame disappears, as with the day-care fine; the motive is crowded out, as with Titmuss's donors

**Argument.** Their strongest: as soon as abstention has a price it stops being shameful — and even those who were not paid stop coming; and those who stay vote no longer from duty but "for ×2", and the good is corrupted.

**Answer.** The vote from shame is the vote of "the meek", from fear and habit (1f §2), and the protocol wants exactly that crowding out. For the stayer the price stands on the exit, not on the act: the act's value rises — ×2 — and that is crowding out in reverse. Titmuss inside out: the protocol pays not the donors but those who do not want to be donors, for not giving — and the blood in the bank gets cleaner; that is screening by a choice that cannot be faked (036e §6, Mirrlees). The civic virtue Sandel stands for becomes real for the first time: to stay when one could take the money is the most explicit civic act ever designed; choice B too is a decision, not inaction (040ag item 2).

**Residual.** The answer is logical, not a measurement: that paying for exit does not crowd out the stayers' motive but strengthens it is tested by the pilot (048l) — the same question as Dalton's (040aj §6): who returned and why.

## 4. Walzer: money as a universal solvent — the protocol makes it the "numeraire" of politics

**Argument.** Even if power is not bought, the protocol measures politics in money, and that is the dominance of money over the sphere.

**Answer.** The numeraire D is pegged to the median and set by referendum (048m arts. 2, 14): it is the polity's own measuring rod, not private money. Dominance of money in Walzer's sense is when the rich convert cash into power; the protocol makes that conversion harder: bringing-in loses its product (059g §4). And by his own list, exemption from military service cannot be bought. In the charter voting is not a duty but a right (art. 16), and a right can be declined. Walzer forbids buying exemption from a duty; the protocol declares that there is no duty.

**Residual.** None.

## 5. Sandel, the method: the limits of a market cannot be drawn without debating the meaning of the good; the debate must be moral, not monetary

**Argument.** Whether paying for non-participation is permissible should be decided by a moral debate about what a vote is, not by a referendum on a sum.

**The assistant's answer.** The referendum on the rule is that debate, and the four readings of the slogan (039b §4b) are four meanings of the vote at last said aloud. "Markets leave their mark": yes, and the mark here is the first measurement in history of how much citizens themselves value their vote (040aj §6). The moral debate has data for the first time.

**The architect's move — government as a plumber.** "Explain: is choosing a sausage a moral choice? A coffee maker? Choosing the plumber who will fix the tap — is that a moral choice? And how does government differ from a plumber? They too simply fix mechanisms, only within the state. The sacredness of power is exactly why the vote is sacred. We are not deciding between life and death here." This finishes Sandel's argument at its base. Choosing a plumber is not a moral choice, though whether the house has water depends on it; government differs from a plumber only in the size of the tap — roads, courts, pensions — and if it fixes badly it is replaced, like a plumber. The sacredness of the vote derives from the sacredness of power, and the sacredness of power benefits power itself: the sacred is neither valued nor paid, the sacred is served for free (059c §2, the death of the myth of the sacred vote; 039b §1, the idealists' three myths). Schumpeter said it a century ago (040q): democracy is a method of selecting managers through competition for votes, no more; Popper added the one condition — that the managers can be replaced without blood (a condition the protocol keeps: the thermostat with memory, 040aj item 4). Sandel demands a moral debate about the meaning of the vote; the meaning of the vote is to hire the one who will fix the tap, and hiring is paid for, not prayed over. There is only one moral question here: why paying for the plumber's work is allowed and paying for the decision of whom to hire is not.

*And the last — also the architect's move:* "By the way, elections under martial law are cancelled almost everywhere — and that is exactly where, in wartime, there would be a moral choice." The one moment when the vote really decides between life and death is war, and that is exactly when elections are cancelled: Britain postponed the election of 1940 to 1945, Ukraine has not voted since 2022, Israel postponed its election in 1973. The vote is declared sacred in peacetime, when it chooses a plumber, and taken away in wartime, when it could be moral. The sacredness of the vote is not about its object but about the convenience of power: where the decision is really hard, power decides by itself. The honest exception: the US cancelled no election in 1864 or 1944 — the one country where the vote in wartime remained a vote.

**Residual.** None.

## 6. What they give the protocol

The opponent's ready-made words of day one: "auctioning democracy" (059l, stage 2) is Walzer in his own words, and the answer of §1 — that the protocol enforces his prohibition. A structure for 1d: any moral objection reduces to the pair "fairness or corruption", and each has an answer. Titmuss inside out as an image for those who stay. And the architect's formula for day one: a payment from the budget is a tax rebate that removes bribery.

## Weak point

Walzer's list and Sandel's examples are given from memory; the item on votes and the wordings should be checked against the editions. The third argument is answered by logic, not by measurement. And in general: both philosophers wrote about buying a vote *for*, not about paying for *exit*; the transfer of their arguments onto the protocol is ours, and an honest statement of their position could be softer than the reconstructed one.
