# 40au. Hélène Landemore: Democratic Reason — Five Arguments: Diversity Trumps Ability, Condorcet, Sortition Instead of Money, Epistocracy with a Cheque, Deliberation

Hélène Landemore, *Democratic Reason: Politics, Collective Intelligence, and the Rule of the Many* (2013), *Open Democracy: Reinventing Popular Rule for the Twenty-First Century* (2020). The one author in the arsenal who strikes at the protocol neither with morality nor with data on the average voter but with a theorem: the more different heads, the better the decision. Three moves by the architect — §1, §3, §4.

---

*How to read. Landemore defends democracy epistemically: the crowd decides better than experts. The chapter shows that the conditions of her own theorems — independence, honesty and engagement of the votes — are not met in a hall that is bought, and that the protocol restores them. Her sortition is an honest steam engine for want of an honest qualification; the protocol is that qualification.*

## 0. What she claims

The main argument for democracy is epistemic: the crowd decides better than experts, because "diversity trumps ability" (the Hong–Page theorem), plus Condorcet's jury theorem: if each is right a little more often than wrong, a majority of many is almost never wrong. Hence the inclusion of all as a condition of decision quality. In *Open Democracy* she goes further: elections select an elite — "closed democracy" — and should be replaced by lot, mini-publics, rotation. The main enemy is Brennan with his epistocracy (040d): to exclude the "incompetent" is to lose diversity.

## 1. Diversity trumps ability: paying the apathetic to leave reduces cognitive diversity — and the architect's move: the bounty trap

**Argument.** The Hong–Page theorem: a large diverse group decides better than a small competent one; the protocol shrinks the group — so it worsens decisions.

**The assistant's answer.** The theorem requires different ways of solving the problem among people who solve it. The apathetic's vote is not a way of solving but a non-attitude (040ao §1, Converse; 040at): a random answer or a campaign's cue. Random votes add noise, not diversity; brought-in votes are correlated — one cue from one campaign — and correlation kills both theorems. What remains are the cross-pressured (040ao §1), the most diverse group of the four by position.

**The architect's move.** "This is a typical bounty trap. There is no phrase 'free and honest choice': for the apathetic and the meek, when the government has the resources and the will to bribe and to pressure, it no longer works, and the rest does not matter. The payment is exactly what removes the unfree, and those who stay are de facto freer and more honest than those who left. The protocol works for them, not against: it gives honest diversity the ×2 vote instead of drowning it in noise." The theorem compares the protocol with a hall where everyone has a free and honest choice, and there is no such hall (040p, compare with the status quo). In Hong–Page and Condorcet the independence and honesty of the vote are conditions, not results, and where the government has the resource and the will to buy and to pressure (030b, 039 §88.9), the apathetic and the meek do not have those conditions: their "diversity" is the diversity of whoever brought them. The payment removes the unfree; those who stay are de facto freer and more honest than those who left, because they have already refused money and pressure. The protocol does not reduce diversity — it makes it audible for the first time. Landemore is for the protocol exactly by her own theorem, once the real hall rather than the ideal one is put into it.

**Residual.** None.

## 2. Condorcet: the more voters the better; the protocol cuts the number — so it worsens

**Argument.** The jury theorem: the probability of a correct majority decision grows with the number of voters; the protocol cuts that number.

**Answer.** The theorem has two halves. If each voter's competence is below one half, then the more voters the *worse*, and the hall that judges by the face (059i §3) is below. The protocol raises the average competence by self-selection (036e §6, Mirrlees) and still leaves millions, not a hundred. And the theorem requires the independence of votes — bringing-in violates it more than any reduction (§1). Condorcet with real parameters is for the protocol.

**Residual.** None.

## 3. Elections are oligarchic; the cure is lot and mini-publics, not money — and the architect's move: a steam engine for want of the combustion engine

**Argument.** Elections select an elite; lot and assemblies cannot be bought and do not discriminate; paying people is the road in the other direction.

**The assistant's answer.** Lot draws a random sample, the apathetic included, who then have to be trained; a mini-public of a hundred does not replace elections — Ireland needed referendums after its assemblies anyway (023e, 006b). The protocol is self-selection by interest: those who want to decide decide, a sample of millions and no training. And open democracy has no answer to who watches between assemblies; under the protocol everyone watches, out of interest (040an §3).

**The architect's move.** "Here I understand them: not seeing how a qualification could be introduced honestly, they settled for the lesser evil. But that does not mean the protocol does the same job worse. They did not know the combustion engine — they rode the steam engine; they learned of the combustion engine — and switched fast." Landemore and the whole mini-publics school were looking for an honest qualification: how to select those who decide without appointing a judge over people. Not finding one, they took the lesser evil — chance: lot does not select, but at least it cannot be bought. That is an honest steam engine, and it ran. The protocol is the qualification they did not find: one that each passes himself, in secret, reversibly (048m arts. 5, 9.3, 16), and not by an exam but by refusing money. Lot selects random people and then trains them; the protocol selects the ready — and in millions. Having learned of such an engine, people switch from the steam engine fast, all the more since their aim was the same and only the means was missing.

**Residual.** None. Lot is compatible with the protocol: a mini-public of those who stay is better than a mini-public of everyone.

## 4. This is Brennan's epistocracy with a cheque: the exclusion of the incompetent under the guise of choice — and the architect's move: two errors in the argument

**Argument.** Brennan wants to filter voters by a test; the protocol filters by money — the same epistocracy, only the poor screen themselves out.

**The assistant's answer.** Nobody is excluded. Epistocracy filters from outside, by a test; the protocol lets each assess himself, in secret and reversibly: "I do not know, I will not decide" is an honest answer (040ag item 2). Landemore values inclusion as a right; the protocol keeps the right and prices the duty.

**The architect's move.** "Here again an error. The poor and the destitute among the hungry and destitute — no elections work at all, people have to survive. But the protocol does not select, it gives, and so the poor man who is not destitute can refuse, and there is no important reason to take the money except extra income. And the second error: is the destitute's vote really needed?" Both errors are pinned to the repository. *The first:* 19b.1 forbids gluing together income, cognition and information and separates the poor from the destitute; the poor man who counts every penny knows the mechanism better than a sociologist; the destitute man who is surviving does not vote under any system — 015b phase 1 ("the mathematics of survival") and Henry George in 033 (on top of destitution any democracy votes for the continuation of destitution). The protocol does not select, it gives: the poor man who is not destitute can refuse, and he has no reason to take except extra income. "Exclusion under the guise of choice" presumes there is no choice — and there is one for everyone except those who have none under any rule. *The second:* "the destitute's vote is certainly needed" is the premise of Brennan and Landemore alike, and 1g "a tax on the poor" already answers: selection runs by stake, not by income; the poor man who has something to lose stays; for him the payment is a share of the median, not pennies. And whether the vote of one who is surviving is needed nobody asks, because the answer is uncomfortable: that vote today belongs to whoever brought the package, and it is "needed" only by the buyer. The protocol gives this person for the first time what he really needs — money — instead of a vote that was being taken from him anyway. From here grew 057d §3b and 013g §3b: poverty as a vote-price policy and the cornerstone.

**Residual.** None.

## 5. The engine is deliberation, and money kills it

**Argument.** Collective intelligence works through deliberation; paying for exit replaces conversation with a transaction.

**Answer.** Deliberation requires deliberators. The protocol creates the demand for knowledge (040u item 3); the game "take or ×2" is mass deliberation on the one question everyone can discuss (040an §3); the argument about the percentage at the referendum is deliberation in Landemore's own sense (048n step 7). And the exit is reversible (048m art. 5): nobody leaves the deliberation for good.

**Residual.** None.

## 6. What she gives the protocol

The right yardstick: judge the protocol by the quality of decisions — ×2 or ×4 (040ar §4) — not by turnout. The conditions of her own theorems — diversity, independence, engagement — are exactly what the protocol restores: independence by removing bought blocs, engagement by keeping those who decide. Lot is compatible with it. And the honest question she poses more precisely than anyone — about the diversity of problems, not of heads (see the weak point).

## Weak point

A reconstruction from two books; the theorems are given in their standard formulations. The honest residual is Landemore's, but it is about problems, not heads: if those who stay skew upward in income, what falls is not cognitive diversity but the diversity of *problems* — those who stay may not know the problems of the poor. The repository's answer is partial: not all the poor are apathetic, the poor burned stay (19b.1); the takers can return at any election if their problems are ignored — the thermostat. But the income composition of those who stay is a pilot measurement (048l), and until then it is open. It is the same question as that of Gilens and Page, next on the list.
