# 40w. Ivan Krastev: *The Light That Failed* — Five Arguments on Imitation, Fear and the Threatened Majority

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File:** 06_040w · v1 · 12 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's assignment of 11 October 2026: "each — five arguments from his book, separately". The fifth is Krastev: *After Europe* (2017), *The Light That Failed* (2019, with S. Holmes), *Democracy Disrupted* (2014). A reconstruction from the texts; the architect's move on the third argument is recorded as a move.

---

*How to read. Krastev is the only one of the five who writes about Eastern Europe from inside and about populism after 2015 as an accomplished fact. His arguments are neither criteria nor a remedy but an explanation of why the East chose Orbán; for the protocol it is the freshest test and the one closest to the "sick jurisdictions".*

## 1. The age of imitation

**The argument.** After 1989 the East copied the West for thirty years without the right to its own version; a copy is humiliating by definition — the copier is always behind and always at fault. Populism is a revolt against imitation, not against liberalism as such.

**The strike.** The protocol is one more Western construction imposed on "sick" jurisdictions; imitation in its pure form.

**The answer.** The protocol copies nothing that exists: it is nowhere (040p, "what is not on the list"). "The sick first" (040m §5d) means the first version will be theirs, and the West will receive its data, not the other way round. Imitation is reversed: the copying will be from the one who took the risk. But Krastev is right about the route: if the pilot is American and the referendum Eastern European, his argument works against us exactly — see the weak point.

## 2. Demography and the panic of disappearance

**The argument.** Eastern Europe is a region people leave; in thirty years Bulgaria lost a fifth of its population. The fear here is not of poverty but of the nation's disappearance, and it makes the migrant an existential threat: not "he will take my place" but "none of us will be left".

**The strike.** Money cannot remove an existential fear.

**The answer.** Disappearance is the burned who left: exit through emigration, Estonia (039 §88.9). The protocol returns to those who left and those who stayed a vote with a share, not an immigrant in their place; and it does not argue with the fear, it changes who decides under fear — not the one being frightened but the one who stayed to pay. The emigrant with a ×3 vote at home has the first reason to return that Krastev lacks (040s, Putnam, the architect's move).

**The architect's move — on the object of the fear itself.** "Nations were invented only for the apathetic, to unite them. The clever have long known that the genes are all mixed; a hundred years ago it was already hard to say what nation you were, especially at the borders — and there were hardly any borders, people went where they wanted. This fear will leave together with the apathetic." This is Anderson (the imagined community) as mechanism rather than judgement: the nation as an image is held up by those who have nothing to imagine about themselves personally, and it is exactly they whom the authorities gather at the polls with that image (1f §5; 040r, Arendt — the mass with nothing to lose). The payment gives them something personal, and the image loses its holders — not because they were persuaded otherwise but because they now have something of their own. The link to argument 3 is obligatory, or the two moves collide: the nation as an image (who you are by blood) is hysteria with an imaginary object, and it is for sale for the payment; the nation as a real risk (war, an enemy at the border, children) is real fear with a long horizon, and it keeps the vote. The protocol removes the first and keeps the second; Krastev's panic of disappearance is the first.

## 3. The threatened majority

**The argument.** The populism of Orbán and Kaczyński is the democracy of a majority that fears becoming a minority in its own country; it votes not for a programme but for its own preservation. This is not a deviation from democracy but a form of it.

**The strike.** The protocol amplifies exactly those who stay at the polls — and what stays is a frightened majority.

**The answer — the architect's move.** Fear is not one-dimensional, and the payment tells its kinds apart. "The weakly frightened with a short horizon — they simply have hysteria, and money removes it fast. The strongly frightened with a long horizon fear real and important things, and the reverse — they take the vote even when they badly need the money." Hysteria is a short horizon with an imaginary or inflated object: the migrant nobody in the village has seen; the personal and the now are stronger than an image (040q, Schumpeter), and the payment extinguishes it at once. Real fear is a long horizon with a real object: war, the border, children; it cannot be bought, because the one who fears knows what he would pay for giving up the vote. The protocol does not "amplify the frightened" — it divides the frightened into those being frightened and those who fear, and keeps the latter. Krastev's threatened majority is a mixture of hysteria and fear that a vote count cannot tell apart; the counter can. What stays is not a frightened majority but a fearing minority with a long horizon — deciding for itself for the first time, without the hysteria brought in to join it.

## 4. Elections without uncertainty: the referendum as imitation of participation

**The argument.** Orbán replaced elections with series of "national consultations" and referendums with a predetermined answer; participation exists, uncertainty does not. The referendum is the populist's favourite instrument.

**The strike — the most dangerous.** The protocol itself stands on a referendum.

**The answer.** Orbán's referendum is about migrants, with a question written by the government and no consequence for it. The referendum on the protocol is about money to the voter, with a consequence for the government: the rule is introduced — the core loses the brought-in (048m Art. 15; 040u item 4). The difference is not in the form but in who pays for the result. But to admit: in an autocracy a referendum on the protocol will not take place — an objection not to the protocol but to the route (048n, signatures; 056f §13s).

## 5. Liberalism lost because it stopped promising a future

**The argument.** After 1989 liberalism promised that tomorrow would be like the West; when tomorrow came and turned out worse, the promise ended and there was no replacement. The populist promises not a future but protection of the present.

**The strike.** The protocol is also about money now, not about the future.

**The answer.** The protocol is the only offer in which the future has a price in the present: the long horizon stays to vote, the short one takes the payment, and both see it on the counter (1f §7). Krastev described how the promise ended; the protocol is a promise checked every cycle, not after thirty years.

**The architect's move — why the promise ended.** "Governments elected by the apathetic do not want to build a future, because the apathetic, out of fear and a short horizon, have no future: to live till tomorrow is good enough for them. The protocol changes the electorate, then the electorate changes the government to one with a long horizon, and there politicians appear who start building a future; the circle has gone the other way — liberalism starts promising a future again." Krastev's argument is about the seller: liberalism stopped promising. In fact it is about the customer: the promise ended because the demand for it ended — a government elected by a short horizon builds what its customer can see, that is, until tomorrow (1f §5, the closed cycle; 036e §2, Nordhaus — a cycle needs the payer to differ from the recipient). The protocol does not give liberalism back its faith; it gives it back its buyer: the one who stayed to vote thinks beyond the cycle, and the politician who wants his vote promises a future again, because that is now the only thing he is paid for. Krastev's circle runs open in one direction — imitation, disappointment, the populist; the protocol closes it in the other: a long horizon at the polls, a long horizon in government, the promise of a future has a price again.

| the threatened majority | yes, a form of democracy | hysteria is for sale, fear is not; what stays is a fearing minority with a long horizon (the architect's move) | — |
| the referendum as imitation | yes | the referendum on the protocol has a consequence for the government | will not take place in an autocracy — the route |

## Weak point

Krastev is the most recent and the most empirical on the East, and the protocol exists there in no form. His first argument strikes us exactly to the extent that the pilot turns out American and the referendum Eastern European: then the protocol is a copy, and the copier is at fault again. The architect's moves on the nation-as-image and on the two fears are theses, not measurements: that hysteria takes the payment and fear keeps the vote is tested only by a pilot with a question about the object of fear (048l, the fifth circle's requirements). And the fourth argument remains honest: where Krastev is right about the referendum, the protocol is not introduced at all.
