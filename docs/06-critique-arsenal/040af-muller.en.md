*How to read. Müller is stricter than Mudde (040ab): anti-elitism is not enough for populism, every opposition has it; populism begins where a politician says "only we represent the real people". One criterion, and it tests not the government but us: has the repository declared who the "real" voter is?*

## 1. Populism is a claim to exclusive representation

**The argument.** Populism is a moral monopoly on representation: "only we are the people", so the rest are not the people, and opposition is illegitimate by definition. Trump: "the only thing that matters is the unification of the people, because the other people don't mean anything". Everything else — anti-elitism, simple answers, a leader — occurs outside populism too.

**The strike.** The repository says: "the one with something to lose should decide", "'those who stay' are those who think beyond the cycle", "'the thinking middle class'", "the clever and the remembering". A Müller critic will say: you have declared who the real voter is, and named him morally — thinking, remembering, with a long horizon; while "the taker" is "apathetic", "meek", with a short one. That is exclusive representation, only the selection is made by money rather than by word.

**The answer.** Müller's test is who decides who the people are. With the populist, the populist decides: he names the real ones, and the list is closed. Under the protocol each decides for himself, at every election, and the list is open by construction: an "apathetic" who did not take the payment is among "those who stay" with the same rights as a "burned" one; a "thinking" voter who took the payment is among "the takers" (048m Arts. 4–5). The repository describes *states*, not types of people ([1h](../01-introduction/001h-glossary.md)): a state is chosen, not assigned. Exclusive representation requires that the represented be named by the representative; the protocol takes that from everyone — the party, the government, the repository itself — and gives it to the voter through a price. Second: Müller's claim is moral — "the real people" are *better*; the protocol does not say who is better — it says to whom what is worth more (040ab item 1, the test "punishment or price"). "Thinking" and "remembering" are not praise but a description of what having a stake does to a person: a "long horizon" is not a virtue but a position (1f §7).

**The architect's decision — a glossary and quotation marks.** The mechanism is not moral, but the words are, and Müller will quote them: "thinking", "the clever and the remembering". By the architect's decision of 12 October 2026 these words are entered in the glossary [1h](../01-introduction/001h-glossary.md) as terms — with a meaning ("one to whom the vote is worth more than the payment") and with what they are not ("not praise and not IQ") — and in new texts are put in quotation marks at their first use in a chapter, so that it is seen: this is a word from the glossary, not a characterisation of a person. A claim to exclusive representation needs a closed list of the "real"; a term in quotation marks is an open definition that anyone enters by his own choice.

**Residual.** Quotation marks and the glossary are for the reader who gets as far as "what is meant here"; a critic with three words will not be stopped by them (1h, weak point). And Müller would leave one question: the repository says "the people are broken into four states" — but who broke them? The answer: not the repository but the price — a state is defined by the choice A or B, not by the description; the description only names what the choice has already shown.

## 2. The populist in power

**The argument.** Three techniques by which a populist is recognised once he governs: occupation of the state (courts, media, commissions — "ours"), mass clientelism (benefits in exchange for loyalty, openly) and "discriminatory legalism" — the law is soft for one's own and hard for others. Orbán, Kaczyński, Chávez.

**The strike.** Clientelism is the payment: the protocol hands money to all voters from the budget; mass clientelism raised to a rule.

**The answer.** Müller's clientelism is benefits *in exchange for loyalty*: the recipient is known, his vote is checkable, and stopping the benefit is a lever. The protocol's payment goes to everyone who chose B, equally (048m Art. 4), names are not published (Art. 9 part 3), and "the taker" owes nothing: he cannot vote, and in the referendum on the rule he can — including against (Art. 5 part 5). No loyalty, no lever, no "our own" — clientelism with the client removed. More: Müller's three techniques are exactly what the protocol makes expensive: occupation of the state runs through "the core" and the clerks (039 §88.9), who under the protocol can take the payment in secret; clientelism gets competition from a budget that pays without loyalty; discriminatory legalism — the law on "bribery", written by its recipients (040y item 3) — becomes the first object of that legalism and the first thing a new government breaks.

**Residual.** Müller would say: a government introducing the protocol can also declare it "ours" and pay its own under its name. The answer is the charter: names are not published, the payment is equal for all, it is introduced by referendum, not by the government. But if the government has already occupied the commissions (his first technique), the precinct balance (Art. 10) is counted by the same commission — the honest limit, the same as Krastev's (040w item 4): under occupation the referendum will not take place.

## 3. "The silent majority"

**The argument.** The populist's favourite device is to invoke those who are not in the hall: "the real people are at home, they are for us, they just are not heard". Non-turnout becomes proof of support, and the lower the turnout, the louder the populist speaks for those who did not come. A claim to representation without a count: silence cannot be refuted, because the silent do not object.

**The strike.** The protocol pays exactly the silent majority — you have handed the populist his argument ready-made: "two-thirds took the money because the system is alien to them — they are for us".

**The answer.** A silent majority exists while silence is not counted: non-turnout has no number but the number of absentees, and anyone may speak in its name. The protocol gives silence a number and a signature: "the taker" is not silent — he made a visible choice, he was counted (048m Art. 10), and he said exactly one thing about himself: this time his vote was worth less than the payment. Nobody is at home any more — everyone either voted, or took, or consciously abstained, and the third group was counted too. Müller wanted the claim on the silent to become refutable; the counter makes it pointless.

**The populist's continuation and the architect's move.** The populist rephrases: "they took the money because your system disgusts them". The architect: "Then why did they not vote for the populist, if they like him so much? He could have offered them the system they like. Or do they not believe him — and money is better for them?" The populist was on the ballot. "The taker" had a choice: a vote for the populist, free, or the payment; he chose the payment — so for all the "disgust with the system" the populist was worth less to him than the sum for sitting out. "The system disgusts them" breaks on one question: *then why did they not vote for you?* You were on the list, you promised them the system they like — and they preferred the money. Either they do not believe you, or they do not need you; the counter leaves no third. Today the populist lives on those who did not come; under the protocol the one who did not come is the one who saw him on the ballot and took the money. This is not a refutation of the claim on the silent — it is its end: for the first time the silent have a price, and the populist lost to it.

**Residual.** None. All that remains to the populist is to claim the payment is too large and "outbid" his voter; but that is already an admission that his voter sells cheaper than he said.

## Weak point

A reconstruction; Müller wrote about populists in power, not about turnout rules, and the transfer of his test to the repository is ours. The glossary closes the register after the fact: chapters before 12 October 2026 use the terms without quotation marks, and Müller is entitled to quote them as they stand. And "a state is chosen, not assigned" holds for "those who stay" and "the takers", but "apathetic" and "burned" are descriptions of the path before the choice, which the repository does give itself; that they are not moral is the glossary's assertion, not a proof.
