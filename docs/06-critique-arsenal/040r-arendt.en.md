# 40r. Hannah Arendt: the Banality of Evil, the Atomised Mass and the Self-Selection of Those Who Act — What She Would Say of a Price for the Vote

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File:** 06_040r · v1 · 10 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's question of 10 October 2026: "Hannah Arendt — what would she say about the protocol?" She was not in the repository. A reconstruction from *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951), *The Human Condition* (1958), *On Revolution* (1963), *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963), *Truth and Politics* (1967), *On Violence* (1970). She said nothing on payment for non-turnout; quotations are given by sense, not verbatim; where she would object, the objection is recorded before the likeness — and hers is the strongest of all examined.

---

*How to read. Arendt is neither an economist nor a political scientist but a philosopher of action, and so she is the only one in this series for whom a price for the vote is not a question of incentives but of what politics is. Five of her things touch the protocol directly: the atomised mass as the raw material of totalitarianism; the banality of evil — evil without intent, from thoughtlessness; action as appearing before others and the self-selected "elite" of those who care; organised lying and factual truth; power as acting in concert against violence as its absence.*

## 1. What she would recognise as her own

**The atomised mass — and the first political offer made to it.** *Origins*: totalitarian movements are mass organisations of atomised, isolated individuals; the mass consists of people who belong to no organisation, and its chief trait is not cruelty but loneliness, "the ground prepared for totalitarianism". The repository described how such a mass is manufactured: the state drives the man into loss, becomes husband to the widow, hires the dependent for predictability (036e §10, 1f §2b). That is her "lonely man" with the mechanism of his making. And the protocol is the first political offer addressed to the atomised person as atomised: it demands of him no belonging, no organisation, no comrades, no assembly — none of what he lacks and what every party demands. It demands one choice, which he makes alone, and for the first time that choice counts. Arendt would say the protocol acknowledges the mass's loneliness as a fact, not a fault — and that until now the only one who addressed the lonely as lonely was the leader.

**The banality of evil — word for word.** Eichmann was not a monster; he did not think. "I only executed" is §13o (the policeman), §13r (the official in the sketch: "I was told and I did, I am a pawn"), §2b (the aunt who was told). Her thesis that the greatest evil is done by those who never decided to be either evil or good is the repository's "a mechanism, not villains" and "Frankenstein" (1f §2b): the result is real and evil, there is no author. Arendt would be the first to say that Shulman's infantility (056f §13t) is her thoughtlessness: neither stupidity nor malice but the refusal of judgement, and that one cannot argue with it because there is nobody to argue with. The protocol does not try: it asks neither thinking nor judgement — it asks a choice, and the choice between money and the vote is open to the one who does not think exactly as to the one who does.

**The self-selected elite of those who act — her own text.** *On Revolution*, on Jefferson's wards and the councils: such a system "would spell the end of general suffrage as we understand it today"; only those who cared would take part, and they would form a "political elite" — not of birth and not of money but self-chosen; while those who did not wish to take part would have "the right to be left alone". That is the protocol's filter by stake and horizon (1f §7) in her words, half a century earlier: those remain to whom the outcome is worth more than the payment, and it is not appointment but self-selection; the rest are left alone — and for the first time with money. She would say the protocol is the only design that makes her council workable without abolishing universal suffrage: the right for all, action for those who chose it.

**Factual truth and organised lying.** *Truth and Politics*: factual truth is defenceless before power and rests only on institutions — courts, the press, archives; and the result of consistent lying is not that the lie is believed but that nobody believes anything any more. The list of the instilled in §13t is her organised lying; "they are not bloodthirsty, they were told" is her result. The counter (048m Art. 9) is an institution of factual truth in her sense: a number published every day, which cannot be suspended and is reconciled by two records (Art. 10); it need not be believed — it can be checked. Arendt held the judge and the reporter as keepers of factual truth; the protocol adds the treasury and the commission checking each other.

**Power against violence.** *On Violence*: power is the ability to act in concert, and it belongs to a group as long as the group holds together; violence appears where power is lost, and can destroy power but never create it. The regime's contraction to its security service (056f §13t, item 2) is her diagnosis: power lost and replaced by violence, "two instruments — jail and kill". And the protocol is power in her sense without one act of violence: the counter makes concerted action possible without conspiracy (Aumann, 036e §11) — each sees that the others are enough, and acts alone knowing he acts together. The weapon that does not fire (029 §29.11) she would call power, not a weapon.

## 2. What she would object to — and it is the strongest objection in the repository

**Payment for leaving the public realm is the victory of "the social" over the political.** The whole of *The Human Condition* rests on the distinction of labour, work and action, and politics for her is action: to appear before others, to begin something new, to be seen. The chief misfortune of modernity, in her view, is the invasion of the public by "the social", the economic: politics becomes the housekeeping of the nation, the citizen a consumer, freedom welfare. The protocol read through her eyes is the summit of that invasion: it sets a price on non-participation in the one act in which a person was a citizen, and tells him his absence is worth so much. She would say: you have not restored politics — you have finally priced it in money, and now even the vote is a line of the household.

The answer is not denial but reversal, and it must be given with her own means. First: she herself wrote that the voting booth is "too small for the citizen" — that general elections are already the victory of the social, representation instead of action; the protocol spoils no action that was never in the booth. Second: the one the protocol pays does not act and does not appear today — he is brought in or sits at home; the payment does not take him out of the public realm, it acknowledges for the first time that he was not there, and pays for the honesty. Third — the main one: those who stay are her actors — self-selected, appearing before others, with a weight that is visible; the protocol does not displace the political by the social, it separates the two for the first time: whoever wants welfare receives it and is left alone; whoever wants action acts, and his action no longer drowns in the mass of the brought-in. She would not accept this at once; but her own *On Revolution* accepts exactly this.

**Loneliness cannot be paid for.** The other half of the same objection: the payment entrenches atomisation — the lonely man is paid to stay lonely. The answer: loneliness is entrenched not by the payment but by the silence it costs today for nothing; a paid exit is visible on the counter beside others like it — for the first time the lonely are counted together without being together. And the choice is made anew at every election: the door back is always open (048m Art. 5 part 2).

**The architect's reversal (10.10.2026): atomisation is a plus, and it works both ways.** "You forgot that she wrote very long ago, and many of her suppositions have since acquired facts. Totalitarianism does not come at once, and it needs a strong ideology — of race or of communism. The protocol precisely breaks up the mass of united fanatics and atomises it — that is a plus, not a minus. But totalitarianism often passes through a personal autocracy, and the autocrat wants to atomise society, as Putin does — and there the protocol works against him too: it gathers people into a group, ‘those who took the money’." Arendt wrote in 1951 with two specimens before her eyes, and atomisation was for her only an evil — the raw material from which a leader assembles a movement. Linz two decades later separated what she had merged (040i): totalitarianism needs a mobilised mass and an ideology, authoritarianism lives by demobilisation and does without ideology ("a flickering set of phrases", Shulman on Russia, 056f §13t); and totalitarianism more often comes through a personalist autocracy that first takes society apart. Hence the protocol has two actions, both against. Against the totalitarian raw material it atomises the united: a fanatical mass is held by each deciding together — in the square, in the column, under the neighbour’s eye; the protocol lets each decide alone, in secret, for money, and a mass in which anyone can quietly take and leave stops being a mass — the very loneliness Arendt feared, turned against the movement rather than the person: take the united apart before unity has become ideology. Against the authoritarian raw material it gathers the atomised: the autocrat wants society taken apart — "don’t meddle", each on his own (the jellyfish, 040i §2, thesis 1); the protocol gathers the taken-apart into a group they never had — "those who took the money": not an organisation, not a party, not one name (048m Art. 9 part 3), but a number on the counter that everyone sees and in which everyone learns he is not alone; an Aumann group (036e §11), coordinated without conspiracy — it can be neither banned, nor dispersed, nor led, it has no headquarters and no square. The protocol does to the mass the opposite of what every regime does: totalitarianism unites — it takes apart; authoritarianism takes apart — it gathers. Arendt could not see this, because she had one enemy; here is her own material plus seventy years of facts.

**"You leave no room for judgement."** Arendt placed judgement above will and interest: politics begins where a person judges rather than counts. The protocol counts. The answer: it counts for those who do not judge, and clears room for those who do; and the decision on the rule itself — the referendum — is an act of judgement about one's own price, the most accessible of all.

## 3. What the repository takes

- **The banality of evil** — as the name for "I only executed" and for "a mechanism, not villains": evil without intent, from thoughtlessness — not to be defeated by argument, only by design.
- **The atomised mass** — as the exact name of the addressee: the protocol is the first offer to the lonely that demands no belonging.
- **The self-selected elite of those who act** from *On Revolution* — as her own formulation of the filter by stake.
- **The counter as an institution of factual truth** — beside the court and the press.
- **Power against violence** — as the formula for "the weapon that does not fire": it is not a weapon, it is power.
- **Two actions on the mass** — it takes the united apart (against the totalitarian raw material) and gathers the taken-apart into a counted group "those who took the money" (against the authoritarian): the opposite of what every regime does.

## 4. Weak point

A reconstruction of a philosopher who despised the economic explanation of politics; her main objection — that the protocol prices in money what should have no price — is not fully removed by argument and stands as her position: for her a citizen who takes money for not turning out has not become more honest, he has ceased to be a citizen. The repository's answer — that he was not one in the booth either — is true by the data and disputable by philosophy. The quotations from *On Revolution* on the self-selected elite are given by sense; the exact wording must be checked against the text of chapter six. And her distinction of totalitarian and authoritarian (by mobilisation) coincides with Linz (040i), which strengthens both but adds nothing new to the protocol.
