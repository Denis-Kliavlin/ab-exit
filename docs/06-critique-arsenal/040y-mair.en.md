# 40y. Peter Mair: *Ruling the Void* — Five Arguments on Parties That Moved Into the State

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File:** 06_040y · v1 · 12 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's assignment of 11 October 2026: "each — five arguments from his book, separately". The seventh is Mair: *Ruling the Void: The Hollowing of Western Democracy* (2013, posthumous), and with R. Katz "Changing Models of Party Organization and Party Democracy: The Emergence of the Cartel Party" (1995). A reconstruction from the texts; the architect's move on the third argument is recorded as a move.

---

*How to read. Mair is about parties, not voters: he showed that Western parties moved out of society into the state and live on its money, and that elections became rotation inside a cartel. For the protocol this is a test from the side the others lack: not who votes, but whom there is left to vote for and on whose money.*

## 1. Parties moved out of society into the state

**The argument.** Party membership in Western Europe fell several-fold in thirty years; parties live on state funding and offices, not on dues and activists; from representatives of society they became agencies of the state.

**The strike.** The protocol deepens dependence on the state — the payment too comes from the budget.

**The answer.** The payment goes to the voter, not to the party: it is the first budget flow that bypasses both the party and the official (040o §1; 048m Art. 1 part 4). Mair's party feeds from the state behind the voter's back; the protocol turns the flow around — it pays the one the party stopped asking.

## 2. Mutual withdrawal: citizens from parties, parties from citizens

**The argument.** This is not one side's apathy but a divorce from both: citizens stopped joining and voting, parties stopped calling them, because they no longer need them.

**The strike.** The protocol pays for withdrawal — it seals the divorce.

**The answer.** The protocol pays for *visible* withdrawal instead of invisible (040s item 1), and for the first time gives the party a reason to return to the citizen: there is nobody left to bring in, and those who stay will not come for an image (040x item 3). Mair's divorce has no document; the protocol issues one and sets a price for both sides.

## 3. The cartel party

**The argument** (with Katz). Parties stopped competing and share state resources, smoothing out differences; state funding is a barrier to newcomers, elections are rotation inside the cartel. This is Przeworski item 1 (040v) from the other side: two equally alien parties.

**The strike.** In the West the cartel rests not on the brought-in but on state money, and the protocol strikes it only through the voter — it does not touch the funding.

**The answer — the architect's move.** "New governments after the protocol will break this scheme first: it was de facto legalised bribery." State funding of parties is the cartel in its pure form: the state pays, that is, the parties pay themselves, from the budget, behind the voter's back, and they pay among other things so that there are no newcomers. Why the new ones will break it first: they have no such feeder, and renouncing it is the cheapest signal "we are not from the cartel" to a voter who remembers (040x item 5). The protocol does not abolish the funding — it changes to whom one answers for it: while the brought-in and the layer vote, the question "who pays you" is not asked; when those with something to lose stay, that question is asked first. **The caveat Mair would demand:** on taking power the new ones get the funding too, and the temptation to keep it — the cartel reproduces itself with new members. Here too the protocol does not forbid but makes it expensive: those who stayed at the polls will ask at the second election, and the answer "we took it too" will cost them what they came with.

**The architect's second move — and the measurement to it.** "It is the simplest way to save money fast right after the election and to fund the protocol." The first part is right: abolishing state funding of parties is a saving from day one, with no tax law and no resistance from anyone but the parties themselves. The second part was checked against the numbers and does not match in scale — stated honestly so that a critic does not take this line first:

| Country | State funding of parties per year | Payment per cycle (estimate: half take it) | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | ≈ €225 m (absolute ceiling 2024) | €400 × 30 m ≈ €12 bn | ≈ 2 % |
| Russia | ≈ 7 bn ₽ (152 ₽ per vote per year) | 40,000 ₽ × 50 m ≈ 2 trn ₽ | ≈ 0.3 % |

The party purse covers a few per cent of the payment, not the payment; the source is the one in 048n (audit and return of what was stolen, "I return, I do not hand out") and in 048k (the budget, Art. 1 part 4). But as a new government's first step it is the exact move: the most symbolic budget line that can be closed in the first week — and the first by which the voter understands that the government has changed.

## 4. Responsibility versus responsiveness

**The argument.** Governments must answer to the EU, the markets, treaties — and to the voter at the same time; the gap grows, and parties choose responsibility to outsiders, handing responsiveness to the populists.

**The strike.** The protocol will strengthen responsiveness at the expense of responsibility.

**The answer.** Responsiveness to the short horizon is exactly what Mair handed to the populists; responsiveness to the long horizon coincides with responsibility — the one who stayed thinks beyond the cycle (1f §7; 040w item 5). Mair's gap is between external obligations and a voter for whom "living till tomorrow" is enough; the protocol changes the voter, and the obligations stop being external.

## 5. Democracy without a demos

**The argument.** The procedures are intact, but the people to whom they belong is no longer there — neither in the parties nor at the polls; what remains is a void that is ruled.

**The strike.** The protocol does not fill the void — it pays for it.

**The answer.** The protocol fills it not with a people but with a counter: for the first time it is visible who is where — at the polls, at home with the payment, at home without (048m Art. 10). Mair's void is unmeasured; a measured void is already a composition one can work with, and the first thing visible in it is that the demos did not vanish but left, and will return where its vote is a share (1f §6; 040s, the architect's move).

## Summary

| Mair's argument | What is true | Answer | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| parties moved into the state | yes | the payment bypasses party and official; the flow turns around | — |
| mutual withdrawal | yes | withdrawal becomes visible and paid; the party has a reason to return | — |
| the cartel party | yes | state funding is legalised bribery; the new will break it first because they have none (the architect's move) | the cartel reproduces itself if those who stay do not ask at the second election |
| responsibility vs responsiveness | yes | responsiveness to the long horizon = responsibility | — |
| democracy without a demos | yes | the void is measured by the counter; the demos left, it did not vanish | — |

## Weak point

A reconstruction; Mair wrote about Western Europe with proportional systems and state funding, and there the cartel really rests on state money, not on the brought-in. The protocol does not touch that funding, and the architect's move is a forecast about the behaviour of new governments, not a rule of the charter: it rests on those who stay remembering and asking (040x item 5), and that is tested only by the second election. The honest residual: if the new governments keep the funding and the voter forgives it, Mair's cartel survives the protocol — with different members.
