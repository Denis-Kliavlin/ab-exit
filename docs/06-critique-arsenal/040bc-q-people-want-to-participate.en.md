# 40bc. Political Scientists' Question 1: "People Want to Participate but Are Not Allowed"

*How to read. The series gathers not authors but the premises on which modern political science stands, and for each names who holds it. The first premise: non-participation is a shortage of access; people want to vote and take part in the life of the state, but barriers stop them. The protocol does not argue with the barriers — they can be real — but with the claim that a willing majority stands behind them.*

**The series frame — the architect's move.** Political science wants the same thing the protocol delivers: a change of power by ordinary procedure, without bloodshed, through a law that cannot be repealed. It understands that the apathetic and the impulsive must be removed from the decision, but it knows no honest franchise qualification. And within the magnitudes and parameters that qualifications have had for two thousand years, it is right: every known qualification — property, education, a test — took the vote away from outside and hit the weak, and non-participation was a loss of the vote, that is, an illness ([006](../02-history/006-2500-years.md)). The architect: "AB-EXIT somehow went around all of this. I am in shock myself." The protocol is not a subtype of the known qualifications but another dimension: a qualification each person passes himself, in secret, by refusing money, and a balance in which nobody loses — the one who leaves gets the payment and the right to return, the one who stays gets double weight. Political science does not see this balance because it counts votes one to one and never puts a price on exit.

## 1. Who says so

- **E. Shulman** (2026, theses 1 and 3). In Russia barriers to participation are built deliberately; an autocracy's population must be passive; elections are a show of loyalty, "no wonder people are not interested." And more broadly: the new masses are politicised, but representative institutions do not satisfy them.
- **E. E. Schattschneider** (040av §1): non-voting is the system's fault; abstention reflects the suppression of alternatives people need.
- **F. F. Piven and R. Cloward** (040az): apathy is a consequence of exclusion, not its cause; remove the registration barriers and people will come.
- **S. Verba, K. Schlozman, H. Brady** (040ax): participation is decided by resources and recruitment; whoever is not asked does not take part.

## 2. The architect's move: they do not want to, until it hurts

"She almost always claims that there is a people who wants to vote and take part in the life of the state, but is not allowed. I am categorically against. There are people who do not want any of this, ever, until things are really bad and uncomfortable for them."

The premise glues two different groups together. Those who are kept out — the burned (1f §6b): they know the price and do not go while the count is foregone. And those who do not want to — the apathetic (1h: a state, not a type). The first are a minority, the second a majority.

**What confirms it.**

- Removing barriers does not wake people. The 1993 motor-voter registration law Piven and Cloward themselves fought for: more were registered, turnout barely moved, and they admitted it in 2000 (040az).
- Direct voting without barriers does not wake them either. Italy, the June 2025 referendums on citizenship and labour: turnout about 30 %, the 50 % quorum not reached.
- Shulman's own example works against the premise. "Yabloko," as soon as it appeared, polled up to 20 % in some surveys and was immediately removed. These are not the apathetic who were not allowed to vote but the burned, who came back as soon as there was someone to vote for.

**"Until it is really bad" is the thermostat.** Participation grows from pain, not from access. The apathetic wakes when it hurts and votes against: hence Le Pen's 30 % and Pew's 44 % "to punish the president" in 2026 (059n).

**Where the premise is right.** In Russia barriers are indeed built on purpose. But that explains the absence of the burned, not of the apathetic: remove the barriers — the burned return, the apathetic stay home.

## 3. The architect's move: they have nowhere to go reasonably and safely

"And Shulman gives them nowhere they can go reasonably and safely."

In modern political science a person has two positions: participate or be suppressed. Within the parameters of two thousand years of qualifications it is right that non-participation is an illness: exit was always a loss of the vote (the series frame). So for one who does not want to take part there is no lawful exit. The exits named or implied — emigrate, change profession or be marginalised, keep silent and be filed as "suppressed" or as the "deep people" — either cost the life one has built or are not recognised at all.

In an autocracy one cannot leave even silently. Shulman herself says (thesis 10): public-sector workers are driven to register for electronic voting, told to "bring three friends," the proper public employee votes on Friday morning online. And she herself says people want a way to express themselves "for which they will not be beaten." That way is absent from her scheme.

**The protocol is that exit.** Reasonable — lawful and paid (048m art. 4). Safe — secret (art. 9.3), the boss will not know. Reversible — one can return after a cycle (art. 5), when things get really bad. Without leaving the country. This is Hirschman's exit (013 §13.9) carried inside the ballot box (040ay).

## 4. The architect's move: in an autocracy the protocol matters most

"Here the protocol matters in an autocracy. It gives the oppressed an honest, non-political move, and the autocrat loses what matters most — the apathetic. Making them becomes unprofitable."

An autocracy needs passivity in substance and activity in form: turnout as a ritual the government reports on. The brought-in public-sector worker is the cheapest turnout, paid for by fear for one's job (013g §3b, the cornerstone). Taking the payment is not a protest and not a petition but a lawful secret choice that cannot be prosecuted as opposition. The apathetic is the autocracy's main resource: he is brought in, frightened, bought with a package (057d §3b). Under the protocol he takes the money and leaves.

The sign flips. Today keeping people poor, dependent and depoliticised pays: such a voter is cheap. Under the protocol each such person costs the budget a payment and does not come. The more apathetic citizens a regime has grown, the more it pays and the less ritual turnout it has. Loyalty becomes visible: the share of takers among those who used to be driven to vote shows real loyalty, not performed loyalty.

## 5. What follows

| Premise | What is true | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| people want to participate | for the burned, a minority | the majority does not want to until it hurts; removing barriers and direct voting do not wake them (the architect's move) |
| non-participation is an illness cured by access | no | non-participation needs a reasonable and safe exit; the protocol provides it (the architect's move) |
| in an autocracy participation is suppressed | yes, the barriers are real | the protocol gives the oppressed an honest non-political move; producing the apathetic becomes unprofitable (the architect's move) |

**Boundary.** Nobody promises that an autocracy will adopt the protocol; the route is a municipal pilot (056f).

## Weak point

Shulman's quotations follow the episode's automatic subtitles. Italy 2025 is one country and one occasion; the 1993 law is one country. The shares of the apathetic and the burned among non-voters are a pilot measurement (048l §4).
