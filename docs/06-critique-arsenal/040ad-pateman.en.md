# 40ad. Carole Pateman: *Participation and Democratic Theory* — Five Arguments on the School of Participation and the Vote That Equals Minus One

*How to read. Pateman is the protocol's strongest moral opponent: for her democracy is neither the selection of elites by vote (against Schumpeter, 040q) nor stability through apathy (against Lipset, 040ac), but a practice in which a person becomes a citizen. The protocol pays for non-participation — and in her terms that is the worst thing one can do. One question to her: what did the school of participation teach the one who is brought to it?*

## 1. Participation educates

**The argument.** Through Rousseau and Mill: by taking part in decisions — at work, in the neighbourhood, at elections — a person learns to see beyond his own interest, gains a sense of efficacy and responsibility. Participation is not a means but a school; low turnout is not a fuse but the school's failure.

**The strike.** The protocol pays for non-participation — it pays so that a person *does not grow*. The apathetic is not merely left apathetic, he is rewarded for it, and the school closes with compensation. Lipset wanted the apathetic not to go; Pateman wanted him to go and change; the protocol hands him money and shuts the door.

**The answer.** Three steps. First — data against theory: Pateman's school has been running for half a century, and in a hundred years of participation the apathetic has learned nothing — 1f §5, the closed cycle: he is brought, he votes "so that it does not get worse", and the next election starts from the same place. The educative effect belongs to participation *by one's own decision*; the brought-in has none of it, and Pateman knew it — her examples come from workers' self-management, where a person decides himself, not from turnout under the boss. Second — the protocol does not shut the door: for the first time it puts before the apathetic a decision he takes *himself*, at home, with a price — to take or to vote. That is the first act of participation in Pateman's sense in his life: meaningful, personal, with a consequence. For a hundred years he was led; now he is asked. Third — for those who stay, Pateman's school opens properly for the first time: the share of the vote is visible, the decision has weight, and the sense of efficacy she looked for is backed by a number on the counter (1d, statement 12), not by words about duty.

**The architect's move — and it changes the argument's arithmetic.** The first version of the answer counted the brought-in as zero: "today too he does not decide about the common — the one who brought him decides for him." The architect: "no, here it matters more: he harms everyone, and himself too, if he votes under pressure as he was told. That is not 0, it is −1." A vote under pressure is not the absence of participation but participation with a minus sign: it is given to the one who presses, not to the one who is right, and so it runs against the voter's own interest and against the interest of everyone who cares — their vote is cancelled by his (040v item 1, two cores cancel each other). Pateman's school teaches that participation makes a person better; participation under pressure makes him worse — himself, because with each such vote he confirms his own dependence (1f §5), and the others. The protocol moves him from −1 to 0: he took, he did no harm. That is not the absence of a school, it is the end of harm; and for Pateman, who wanted participation to improve, zero is more honest than minus one dressed as civic duty.

**Residual.** Pateman would not accept "the choice to take is also participation": for her participation is in deciding *about the common*, not about oneself; and if the apathetic takes the payment every time, the school is closed to him for good — the protocol allows it (048m Art. 5 part 2) and promises nothing else. But her scale ran from zero upwards; the architect's move shows that today's point is below zero, and the first thing the protocol does is raise it to zero. Beyond that — her school, for those who stay.

## 2. Participation at work is the main school

**The argument** (chs. 3–4). The centre of democracy is not elections but the enterprise: that is where a person spends his life and where he decides least. Democracy at work — self-management, cooperatives, the Yugoslav experience — is the only school that works daily rather than once in four years; elections are an exam, not instruction.

**The strike.** The protocol is about the exam and does nothing about work; worse, it pays from the budget those who depend on an employer and so entrenches the dependence: the apathetic takes the money and stays under the boss.

**The answer.** Pateman found where dependence is created; the repository found where it is *sold* — at the polls (1f §5; 039 §88.9, clerks and budget employees as the core). It is one dependence, and she is right that elections do not cure it. But the protocol strikes the point where dependence at work becomes a political resource: the boss brings the subordinate to vote because it is free; under the protocol the subordinate can take the payment in secret (048m Art. 9 part 3), and for the first time the boss does not know whether he brought him or not (040s §4). Pateman's democracy at work takes from the boss his power over the decision; the protocol takes from him his power over the vote — less, but something nobody took away in a hundred years. And the payment from the budget does not entrench the dependence — for the first time it gives the dependent an income the employer does not control: the first break in the chain "wage — loyalty — turnout" (036e, Goldin, node 1).

**The architect's move — on the school itself.** "A company is a strange school: everyone there is selected for the same priorities and the same parameters." The enterprise is not a school of democracy but its opposite: people are *selected* into it for one customer, the dissenter is not persuaded but dismissed, and "participation" inside is the participation of those who have already passed the loyalty filter (040x item 5, "they hire their own kind"; 1f §2b). Self-management teaches one to agree with people like oneself; democracy is needed precisely to decide with those who are not. The Yugoslav council voted unanimously not because it had learned but because it was selected. Pateman's school trains agreement inside a filter — the very stuff the core is made of; an election under the protocol is the only place where the filter does not operate, because nobody selected the composition of the deciders: each set his own price.

**Residual.** Pateman is right that the protocol is about the exam and opens no school at work; the repository does not promise one. But the chain "employer — turnout" has outlived every self-management, and a selected collective does not teach democracy by construction; the protocol removes the one thing that made her school dangerous for elections — a price of the vote equal to zero.

## 3. The sense of efficacy

**The argument.** Resting on Almond and Verba (*The Civic Culture*, 1963): a person takes part when he believes that taking part changes something, and the belief grows with participation — a positive loop. Efficacy is low among the poor and the dependent, which is why they do not go; democracy's task is to raise their belief, not to accept its absence.

**The strike.** The protocol makes the absence of efficacy profitable: the one who does not believe his vote changes anything is paid to be confirmed in it. The loop turns downwards: did not believe — took — believes even less.

**The answer.** Pateman's efficacy is a *belief*, measured by survey; the protocol replaces belief with a number. Today the apathetic is right that his vote changes nothing: one in millions with two cancelling cores (040v item 1) is not low self-esteem but an accurate estimate. Pateman proposed to raise the belief without changing the fact; the protocol changes the fact: the share of every remaining vote grows with every taker (1d, statement 12), and the efficacy of those who stay becomes true for the first time rather than felt. The taker's efficacy is not zero either, and that is new: his decision is visible on the counter, it costs the budget, and the government reads that line (040t item 2) — the first time his non-participation has affected anything. The loop does not turn downwards: for the first time it has a real basis in both directions.

**Residual.** Pateman would say efficacy through money is not that efficacy: "my vote matters" and "my non-participation costs a lot" are different feelings, and the second does not make a citizen. True; but the first was a lie for the apathetic for a hundred years, and the second is true from day one, and the protocol promised no feelings — it promised numbers.

## 4. Against Schumpeter: democracy is not a method of selecting elites

**The argument.** Pateman is written as an answer to Schumpeter (040q): if democracy is merely competition of elites for votes, then the voter's apathy is not a problem but the norm; she rejects this as substituting democracy's procedure for democracy.

**The strike.** The protocol is Schumpeterian to the end: it accepts apathy, pays for it and leaves the competition of elites for those who stay.

**The answer.** The protocol stands between them. With Schumpeter — in that apathy is not cured by words and the sense of reality is strongest in the personal and the now (040q); with Pateman — in that apathy is not the norm but a result, and it has a price nobody pays today. Schumpeter accepted apathy for free; Pateman refused to accept it; the protocol accepts it for money — and thereby makes it visible, finite and reversible (040aa item 5: the more have left, the higher the share and the more have returned). Neither of the two offered a mechanism of return; the protocol has one.

## 5. The sexual contract

**The argument** (*The Sexual Contract*, 1988). The social contract hides another — the subordination of women, framed as private; men's "participation" is paid for by women's non-participation at home.

**The strike.** The protocol pays the median to the dependent — and the core, by the repository, is mostly female (039 §88.9; 036e, Goldin); so the protocol pays for the same contract: money to the woman for not deciding.

**The answer.** Goldin is examined by six nodes (036e §10): dependence is the incentive, and the core is female not by nature but by dependence on the budget that replaced the husband. The protocol pays neither for sex nor for women's non-participation — it pays everyone the same (048m Art. 4), and for the first time in the core's history gives the dependent woman an income controlled by neither husband, nor boss, nor the state-as-husband (036e §10, node 2). Pateman's contract rested on the woman having no money of her own; the protocol is the first social contract under which she has it. The horizon filter, by the architect's decision, is pro-base, not pro-female (036e §10): the one who stays is the one with a long horizon, and among women with a reliable support there are more of them.

## Weak point

A reconstruction; Pateman wrote about workers' self-management and normative theory, not about turnout, and her "participation" is wider than voting — the protocol answers only the electoral part. "−1" is an estimate of sign, not a measurement: that a vote under pressure harms the voter follows from its being cast against his interest, but the harm of one vote cannot be measured; only the result is measurable — the composition of deciders before and after (048l). And Pateman would hold her ground on one thing: a school opened for money is not the school she wrote about.
