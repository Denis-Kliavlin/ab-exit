# 40ba. Turnout Theory: Blais, Ferejohn and Fiorina, Franklin, Wolfinger and Rosenstone — Five Arguments: Duty Holds Turnout, Minimax Regret, the Habit of First Elections, Education Decides, the Price of Registration

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File:** 06_040ba · v1 · 16 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's assignment of 16 October 2026 ("Put them all in the repo"). André Blais, *To Vote or Not to Vote? The Merits and Limits of Rational Choice Theory* (2000); John Ferejohn and Morris Fiorina, "The Paradox of Not Voting: A Decision Theoretic Analysis" (1974); Mark Franklin, *Voter Turnout and the Dynamics of Electoral Competition in Established Democracies since 1945* (2004); Raymond Wolfinger and Steven Rosenstone, *Who Votes?* (1980). One chapter for four works, because their blow is one: turnout is held not by calculation but by duty and habit, and the protocol is the first to put a price on duty. Written without a move by the architect.

*How to read. Downs's calculus (013 §13.2) explains turnout poorly: by Blais about half of voters go out of a sense of duty, by Franklin turnout is a habit of the first three elections, by Wolfinger and Rosenstone the strongest predictor is education. The chapter shows that the protocol does not abolish duty but gives it a price for the first time, and that the habit of first elections becomes the habit of the first choice "take or stay".*

## 0. What they claim

Blais: the rational model explains little; duty is the main motive of about half of those who vote; those who do calculate the probability of deciding the outcome vote no less than the rest. Ferejohn and Fiorina: the voter does not compute probabilities but minimises maximum regret — "what if my vote would have decided, and I did not come." Franklin: turnout is a habit formed in a young voter's first three elections and depends on how competitive and consequential those elections were; generations that entered politics under uncompetitive elections do not vote all their lives. Wolfinger and Rosenstone: education is the strongest predictor of turnout, stronger than income; the registration closing date is a measurable cost that lowers turnout.

## 1. Duty holds turnout, not calculation; the payment strikes at duty, and everything follows

**Argument.** Half go out of duty; put a price on duty and duty becomes a commodity, and commodities get bought.

**Answer.** This is 1g "It will destroy civic duty" with numbers, and the answer is the same: the protocol does not abolish duty, it places the price of the alternative beside it. The one for whom duty is worth less than D leaves; the one for whom it is worth more stays, and his duty for the first time has a measured price — the refusal of the payment. A duty that survives a price is real; a duty that does not was holding turnout by bringing-in and packages (057d §3b). By Blais half vote out of duty — so half will stay under any D, and that is the lower bound of those who stay, matching the estimate of 040al §4.

**Residual.** None.

## 2. Minimax regret: people go so as not to regret; the payment removes regret by replacing it with money

**Argument.** If I took the payment and my vote would have decided — there is no regret, there is money; the protocol kills the one motive that works.

**Answer.** The protocol moves regret, it does not remove it. Today there is one regret: "I did not come, and I would have decided." Under the protocol there are two, both with a price: "I took, and I would have decided" and "I stayed, and everyone took, and my vote changed nothing, and I lost D." The second is the daily game of 040an §3: everyone recomputes his stake until election day. Minimax with two priced regrets is exactly the decision by substance that Ferejohn and Fiorina did not have: theirs was about probability, under the protocol it is about the stake.

**Residual.** None.

## 3. The habit of first elections: those who entered under uncompetitive elections never vote; the protocol will produce a generation that began with a cheque

**Argument.** A young voter whose first election is "take $800" will never come.

**Answer.** Franklin is right about habit and has described today's hall: the generations that entered under a foregone count do not vote (040av §2). Under the protocol a young person's first election is the first choice "take or stay," a choice with a price rather than a default absence. The habit forms not on "did not come" but on "decided"; and every election it is formed anew (048m art. 5). Franklin himself showed that the competitiveness of the first elections decides: under the protocol elections are more competitive for those who stay, because the votes are fewer and each is ×2. The young person who took at his first election is not lost; for the first time he knows what his absence costs.

**Residual.** Yes: the habit of "taking" among the young is a pilot measurement in the second and third cycle (048l), and it is the chapter's one residual not closed by the construction.

## 4. Education decides: those who stay are the educated — Brennan without the test

**Argument.** The strongest predictor of turnout is education; it will also predict who stays.

**Answer.** Education predicts turnout where turnout costs skills (040ax §2); the protocol's one question costs none. And 19b.1: income, education and being informed are three different scales, and about a mechanism that pays everyone is informed. Brennan filters from outside by a test; the protocol leaves the assessment to the person (040au §4). Their own data: education is stronger than income — so the poor educated stay too, and that breaks the argument "those who stay are the rich" from inside.

**Residual.** None.

## 5. The price of registration is measurable and lowers turnout; the protocol is the largest price possible

**Argument.** A registration closing date costs several points of turnout; D costs tens.

**Answer.** Their scale is the price of coming. The protocol leaves the price of coming unchanged and sets a price on not coming — paid by the state (040az §4). These are different quantities: registration takes from the person, the payment gives. And turnout is not the protocol's aim (040ar §4: the yardstick is the quality of decisions, ×2–×4); their own work shows that turnout responds to price, so the share of takers will respond to D as they would predict — which makes it a measurable quotation (013g §3b).

**Residual.** None.

## 6. What they give the protocol

A lower bound of those who stay — half, by duty (Blais). The form of the decision — minimax with two priced regrets. A forecast about habit: the first choice forms a generation, and that is a pilot measurement. And proof that turnout responds to price — so the share of takers will be an honest number.

## Summary

| Argument | What is true | Answer | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| duty holds turnout | yes, half of it | duty gets a price, not abolition; half is the lower bound of those who stay | — |
| minimax regret | yes | two priced regrets instead of one unpriced; a decision about the stake, not the probability | — |
| the habit of first elections | yes | the first election is the first priced choice; formed anew every cycle | the habit of "taking" among the young — a second- and third-cycle measurement |
| education decides | yes, where turnout costs skills | the one question costs no skills; the poor educated stay | — |
| the price of registration | yes | the price of coming is unchanged; a price is set on not coming, paid by the state | — |

## Weak point

Four works in one chapter; "half out of duty" is Blais's estimate, not a cross-country measurement. One residual — the habit of the young, the pilot's second and third cycle. Written without a move by the architect.
