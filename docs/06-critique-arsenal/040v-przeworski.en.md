# 40v. Adam Przeworski: *Why Bother With Elections?* — Five Arguments on Minimal Democracy and What a Price for the Vote Changes

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites
**File:** 06_040v · v1 · 12 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's assignment of 11 October 2026: "each — five arguments from his book, separately". The fourth is Przeworski: *Democracy and the Market* (1991), *Democracy and Development* (2000, with co-authors), *Why Bother With Elections?* (2018), *Crises of Democracy* (2019). A reconstruction from the texts; the architect's move on the first argument is recorded as a move.

---

*How to read. Przeworski is a minimalist: democracy for him is neither the will of the people nor justice but a procedure by which rulers can be peacefully removed. He is the most sober of the five, and so his arguments are not about an ideal but about what elections actually do. The question to each: does the protocol do it better, or break it.*

## 1. Democracy is a system in which parties lose elections

**The argument.** The minimal definition: not "the will of the people" but a regular, peaceful possibility of removing the rulers. If parties lose, there is democracy; if not, there is none, whatever the constitution says.

**The strike.** None — this is Shenderovich's criterion "can he be re-elected" (039b §4b) in academic form, and the protocol meets it more exactly: the price of not leaving is visible before the election, and losing becomes cheaper for the loser (the thermostat, 001b; 040i, Linz 1.3).

**The architect's move — and it turns the criterion into a diagnosis.** "Parties lose not because the voter clearly supported someone, but only because of a wavering, uncertain, thin layer; the parties' arguments for that layer have become extremely shallow and primitive; in essence these are two parties equally alien to the thinking middle class. But the parties cannot change — they would at once lose their frightened apathetics and meek. So alternation either gives nothing — they went the wrong way and go on going — or cancels the long projects of the previous government, again for populism's sake." Przeworski says "lose" and does not say *to whom*. To the layer: two cores — the brought-in and the habitual — cancel each other out, and the decision is left to those who make up their minds last and know least. Hence three mechanisms. The arguments are for the layer, hence primitive: Downs (013 §13.2), both parties converge on the median voter, and with two cancelling cores the median is the waverer with a short attention span; the thinking middle class recognises neither as its own, because neither is addressed to it. The parties cannot change: the core holds the party hostage, any shift frightens those who vote "so that it does not get worse" — the closed cycle of 1f §5 from the party's side. Alternation without consequences, or with negative ones: the long horizon loses to every cycle.

What the protocol does — to all three at once. *It frees the party from its core:* when the apathetic and the meek take the payment, the party has nobody left to lose by shifting — for the first time it can change, because those whom change frightened are at home with the money; the protocol is not only a filter of voters but the lifting of a hostage situation from parties. *It moves the decision from the layer to those who stay:* the decider is not the one who made up his mind last but the one with something to lose; arguments will have to be made for the thinking, because fewer others are at the polls. *It protects long projects:* the horizon filter (1f §7) keeps those who think beyond the cycle, and cancelling the long for populism's sake stops paying — the one who stayed to vote pays for it. Przeworski's minimal democracy under the protocol stays minimal — parties still lose — but they lose *to someone*, not to a layer.

## 2. Elections as "paper stones": the peaceful processing of conflict

**The argument.** The value of elections is not that they find the truth but that the sides learn each other's numbers and therefore do not fight: the loser sees he is fewer and waits for the next time.

**The strike.** If part of the electorate leaves for the payment, the sides' numbers are distorted — the stones do not count everyone.

**The answer.** Today the stones do not count everyone either: two-thirds are at home and their number is known to nobody — which is exactly what makes a street reckoning of forces possible: "we are more, we just did not come". The counter counts everyone — those who came, those who took, those who abstained (048m Art. 10 part 3, the precinct balance). Przeworski wanted the sides to know their numbers; the protocol shows the full ones for the first time, including those who left and those who stayed home without the payment.

## 3. People accept losing if they believe they can win next time

**The argument.** Democracy rests on the expectation of alternation, not on alternation itself; the loser endures while he has a future.

**The strike.** The one who took the payment has dropped out of the game — he has no "next time".

**The answer.** He has — anew at every election (048m Art. 5 part 2). More: the protocol returns a "next time" to those who had none — the burned, who knew the core could not be outvoted and stopped waiting (1f §6). The expectation of alternation returns with the amplified vote; and the taker keeps his vote in referendums on the rule itself (Art. 5 part 5).

**The second layer — the architect's move, and the stronger one.** "An autocrat like Orbán or Putin simply changes the electoral law and intimidates the meek and the apathetic — and never loses again. The protocol is only by referendum, and as an institution it breaks extremely hard and visibly." Przeworski's argument rests on the "next time" but does not say what the "next time" itself rests on: the electoral law, written by the sitting government (029 §29.11, row 10). Orbán rewrote the law in 2011, Putin in 2020; after that the expectation of alternation became a fiction which the frightened meek and apathetic confirm with every turnout (1f §5), and minimal democracy ended without breaking a single procedure (item 4). The protocol is fastened differently: introduced by referendum, repealed only by referendum (048m Art. 15); a parliamentary majority cannot amend it away, and cancelling the payment is not a line in a law nobody reads but the disappearance of money from everyone who took it, under a counter everyone sees. Przeworski's "next time" is protected by a rule the government itself changes; the protocol's — by a rule changed only by those who pay.

## 4. Crises of democracy: institutions intact, belief gone

**The argument** (*Crises of Democracy*). Polarisation, falling turnout, flash parties, distrust of institutions — with formally working elections. A democracy can die without breaking a single rule; the symptoms are there, the mechanism he does not name.

**The strike.** The protocol is one more rule in a system where rules do not save.

**The answer.** The protocol is not a rule about procedure but a rule about price, and the only one that changes not the form but the composition of the deciders (1f §7). Przeworski listed the symptoms and did not name the mechanism; the repository has one — the core, the brought-in, the burned — and each symptom has a move: polarisation — the layer and the cancelling cores (item 1); falling turnout — exit for free and unseen (040s); flash parties — parties unable to change because of the core (item 1); distrust — organised lying without institutions of factual truth (040r). Rules do not save when they are about procedure; the protocol is about who uses the procedure.

## 5. Development does not create democracy but makes it durable

**The argument** (*Democracy and Development*). The most cited result: above a certain income per head (about 6,000 dollars at 1985 prices) no democracy has fallen; poor ones fall, rich ones do not.

**The strike.** The protocol is for poor and sick jurisdictions, where by his data democracies die anyway; the rich do not need it.

**The answer.** Przeworski's threshold is about a country's income, not about who decides; and after 2016 his rule cracked where income exceeds any threshold: the United States and Hungary are rich on his scale, and the slide went on with full wallets (039b, 1f §2c). That is exactly the occasion for the protocol: when income stopped protecting, what remains is to change not income but the composition of the deciders. And "the sick first" (040m §5d) is not because the rich do not need it but because the poor have nothing to lose from a trial, and the result for the rich will already be data.

## Summary

| Przeworski's argument | What is true | Answer | What remains |
|---|---|---|---|
| parties lose elections | yes — the criterion | they lose to the layer; the protocol frees parties from the core, moves the decision to those who stay, protects long projects (the architect's move) | — |
| paper stones | yes | today not everyone is counted; the counter counts everyone | — |
| the expectation of next time | yes | it exists at every election; it returns to the burned; an autocrat removes it by rewriting the law, the protocol only by referendum (the architect's move) | — |
| crises with intact institutions | yes, the symptoms | the protocol is a rule about price, not procedure; each symptom has a mechanism | — |
| income protects democracy | it did until 2016 | cracks with full wallets; change the composition of deciders | the rich — after data from the poor |

## Weak point

A reconstruction; Przeworski did not write on payment for non-turnout and, as a minimalist, would view it with suspicion: anything that adds meaning to the procedure he held superfluous. The architect's move on the layer is a thesis resting on Downs and 1f §5, not on Przeworski's data; empirically it is supported only by the fact that outcomes in two-party systems are decided by fractions of a percent with the cores unchanged. And "freeing parties from the core" is a consequence that does not arrive in the first cycle: a party learns that the hostages are gone only after they have taken the payment.
