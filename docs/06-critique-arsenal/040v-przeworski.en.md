# 40v. Adam Przeworski: *Why Bother With Elections?* — Five Arguments on Minimal Democracy and What a Price for the Vote Changes

**Chapter:** 06 — The Structural Hypocrisy of Criticism and the Arsenal of the Elites

**Source:** the architect's assignment of 11 October 2026: "each — five arguments from his book, separately". The fourth is Przeworski: *Democracy and the Market* (1991), *Democracy and Development* (2000, with co-authors), *Why Bother With Elections?* (2018), *Crises of Democracy* (2019). A reconstruction from the texts; the architect's move on the first argument is recorded as a move.

*How to read. Przeworski is a minimalist: democracy for him is neither the will of the people nor justice but a procedure by which rulers can be peacefully removed. He is the most sober of the five, and so his arguments are not about an ideal but about what elections actually do. The question to each: does the protocol do it better, or break it.*

## 1. Democracy is a system in which parties lose elections

**The argument.** The minimal definition: not "the will of the people" but a regular, peaceful possibility of removing the rulers. If parties lose, there is democracy; if not, there is none, whatever the constitution says.

**The strike.** None — this is Shenderovich's criterion "can he be re-elected" (039b §4b) in academic form, and the protocol meets it more exactly: the price of not leaving is visible before the election, and losing becomes cheaper for the loser (the thermostat, 001b; 040i, Linz 1.3).

**The architect's move — and it turns the criterion into a diagnosis.** "Parties lose not because the voter clearly supported someone, but only because of a wavering, uncertain, thin layer; the parties' arguments for that layer have become extremely shallow and primitive; in essence these are two parties equally alien to the thinking middle class. But the parties cannot change — they would at once lose their frightened apathetics and meek. So alternation either gives nothing — they went the wrong way and go on going — or cancels the long projects of the previous government, again for populism's sake." Przeworski says "lose" and does not say *to whom*. To the layer: two cores — the brought-in and the habitual — cancel each other out, and the decision is left to those who make up their minds last and know least. Hence three mechanisms. The arguments are for the layer, hence primitive: Downs (013 §13.2), both parties converge on the median voter, and with two cancelling cores the median is the waverer with a short attention span; the thinking middle class recognises neither as its own, because neither is addressed to it. The parties cannot change: the core holds the party hostage, any shift frightens those who vote "so that it does not get worse" — the closed cycle of 1f §5 from the party's side. Alternation without consequences, or with negative ones: the long horizon loses to every cycle.

What the protocol does — to all three at once. *It frees the party from its core:* when the apathetic and the meek take the payment, the party has nobody left to lose by shifting — for the first time it can change, because those whom change frightened are at home with the money; the protocol is not only a filter of voters but the lifting of a hostage situation from parties. *It moves the decision from the layer to those who stay:* the decider is not the one who made up his mind last but the one with something to lose; arguments will have to be made for the thinking, because fewer others are at the polls. *It protects long projects:* the horizon filter (1f §7) keeps those who think beyond the cycle, and cancelling the long for populism's sake stops paying — the one who stayed to vote pays for it. Przeworski's minimal democracy under the protocol stays minimal — parties still lose — but they lose *to someone*, not to a layer.

## 2. Elections as "paper stones": the peaceful processing of conflict

**The argument.** The value of elections is not that they find the truth but that the sides learn each other's numbers and therefore do not fight: the loser sees he is fewer and waits for the next time.

**The strike.** If part of the electorate leaves for the payment, the sides' numbers are distorted — the stones do not count everyone.

**The answer.** Today the stones do not count everyone either: two-thirds are at home and their number is known to nobody — which is exactly what makes a street reckoning of forces possible: "we are more, we just did not come". The counter counts everyone — those who came, those who took, those who abstained (048m Art. 10 part 3, the precinct balance). Przeworski wanted the sides to know their numbers; the protocol shows the full ones for the first time, including those who left and those who stayed home without the payment.

## 3. People accept losing if they believe they can win next time

**The argument.** Democracy rests on the expectation of alternation, not on alternation itself; the loser endures while he has a future.

**The strike.** The one who took the payment has dropped out of the game — he has no "next time".

**The answer.** He has — anew at every election (048m Art. 5 part 2). More: the protocol returns a "next time" to those who had none — the burned, who knew the core could not be outvoted and stopped waiting (1f §6). The expectation of alternation returns with the amplified vote; and the taker keeps his vote in referendums on the rule itself (Art. 5 part 5).

**The second layer — the architect's move, and the stronger one.** "An autocrat like Orbán or Putin simply changes the electoral law and intimidates the meek and the apathetic — and never loses again. The protocol is only by referendum, and as an institution it breaks extremely hard and visibly." Przeworski's argument rests on the "next time" but does not say what the "next time" itself rests on: the electoral law, written by the sitting government (029 §29.11, row 10). Orbán rewrote the law in 2011, Putin in 2020; after that the expectation of alternation became a fiction which the frightened meek and apathetic confirm with every turnout (1f §5), and minimal democracy ended without breaking a single procedure (item 4). The protocol is fastened differently: introduced by referendum, repealed only by referendum (048m Art. 15); a parliamentary majority cannot amend it away, and cancelling the payment is not a line in a law nobody reads but the disappearance of money from everyone who took it, under a counter everyone sees. Przeworski's "next time" is protected by a rule the government itself changes; the protocol's — by a rule changed only by those who pay.

## 4. Crises of democracy: institutions intact, belief gone

**The argument** (*Crises of Democracy*). Polarisation, falling turnout, flash parties, distrust of institutions — with formally working elections. A democracy can die without breaking a single rule; the symptoms are there, the mechanism he does not name.

**The strike.** The protocol is one more rule in a system where rules do not save.

**The answer.** The protocol is not a rule about procedure but a rule about price, and the only one that changes not the form but the composition of the deciders (1f §7). Przeworski listed the symptoms and did not name the mechanism; the repository has one — the core, the brought-in, the burned — and each symptom has a move: polarisation — the layer and the cancelling cores (item 1); falling turnout — exit for free and unseen (040s); flash parties — parties unable to change because of the core (item 1); distrust — organised lying without institutions of factual truth (040r). Rules do not save when they are about procedure; the protocol is about who uses the procedure.

## 5. Development does not create democracy but makes it durable

**The argument** (*Democracy and Development*). The most cited result: above a certain income per head (about 6,000 dollars at 1985 prices) no democracy has fallen; poor ones fall, rich ones do not.

**The strike.** The protocol is for poor and sick jurisdictions, where by his data democracies die anyway; the rich do not need it.

**The answer.** Przeworski's threshold is about a country's income, not about who decides; and after 2016 his rule cracked where income exceeds any threshold: the United States and Hungary are rich on his scale, and the slide went on with full wallets (039b, 1f §2c). That is exactly the occasion for the protocol: when income stopped protecting, what remains is to change not income but the composition of the deciders. And "the sick first" (040m §5d) is not because the rich do not need it but because the poor have nothing to lose from a trial, and the result for the rich will already be data.

## 6. The self-enforcing equilibrium: why it pays the government to keep the protocol

*Added 12 October 2026 after the architect's move in 040ag item 5 and his request "to check what the repo has on this rule and examine it in more detail".*

**Przeworski's concept.** *Democracy and the Market*, ch. 1: democracy is consolidated when compliance with the rules is the equilibrium of the decentralised strategies of all relevant forces; when it is "the only game in town" and nobody gains more by acting outside the institutions. A rule holds not because it is guarded but because it pays everyone to keep it. Beside him, Weingast (1997): a sovereign transgresses while citizens cannot coordinate on punishment, and coordination breaks on citizens disagreeing about what counts as a transgression; a constitution serves as a focal point.

**The architect's move.** "The rule is protected not by a lock but by the fact that breaking it gathers everyone against the breaker. If the government cuts the payments or tampers with the rules, both those who are for the money and those who are for the vote will come out, led by an opposition with a motive; there will be no small protests here." In the repository this was scattered and unnamed: the forks of 1c ("cut the payments — an influx of the enraged with a tenfold vote", 056e §3; "the dictator and the army", 048d §3), the Nash equilibrium for three players (006 §15.13, 013 §13.5), Arts. 14–15 of the charter. Here it is gathered.

**Four properties by which the protocol is a self-enforcing rule more exactly than a constitution.** *A bright line:* a transgression is unambiguous — the payment equals p × M, the share equals one over the number who stay, the counter is public; there is no dispute "was that a transgression", there is a number that changed. *Unsplit loss:* a cut strikes both sets at once — "the takers" lose money, some of them return to the polls, and "those who stay" lose their share (a scale with one beam, 040ae item 4); a constitutional transgression strikes some and benefits others, and Weingast's coordination collapses. *A ready coordinator with a motive:* an opposition with a slogan (040ag item 4: "since you pay illegally — we raise it legally"). *Force inside the losers:* an army of "takers" (040ag item 5). Consequence: the government's best reply to the protocol is to keep it. Consolidation in Przeworski's sense achieved by construction, not by decades of habit.

**Comparison with other repeals.** Consolidation comes not from the importance of what is repealed but from its concreteness and simultaneity.

| Repealed | Bright line | Who loses | Force | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| elections | yes | the loss is abstract and split: "the core" loses nothing, "the burned" are long out | untouched | Russia 2004 — silence; Peru 1992 — 80 % for dissolving congress; what consolidates is not repeal but theft of the result (Ukraine 2004, Belarus 2020) |
| money (demonetisation) | yes | everyone, but with exits and a justification | untouched | India 2016 — no revolt, Modi re-elected; USSR 1991 — anger without uprising |
| pensions | yes | one category → split | untouched | Russia 2018 — rating −20, reform passed; Nicaragua 2018 — struck workers and employers at once → uprising |
| a subsidy on a universal good | yes, a price overnight | everyone, at once | an army from the people, called in | Egypt 1977 — reversed in two days; Sudan 2018 — fall of Bashir, the army crossed over; Kazakhstan 2022 — uprising, army; Iran 2019 — crushed by the IRGC |
| the Alaska dividend | yes, a number | everyone | not needed | 2016 — the governor halved it and did not run again; the dividend's size is the main issue of every election |

The closest relative of repealing the protocol is not repealing elections but repealing a universal subsidy: a number, everyone, overnight, an army from the people. The protocol is stronger than a subsidy in one thing: a subsidy has one arm — money — and its repeal does not raise those who do not need money; the protocol has a second arm — the share of the vote — and a cut for the first time brings out "the thinking" with a long horizon (Nicaragua 2018 shows what happens when both sets are struck). Alaska is the protocol without the second arm and without an army: with a bright line and universality one coordinator-candidate suffices, self-enforcement works electorally. And the protocol has no justification: subsidies are repealed "at the IMF's demand", money "against corruption", elections "for stability"; the payment under Art. 8 is an unconditional obligation, and there is no version in which a cut looks forced.

**The lock is weak — and that is fine.** Art. 15 says "forever", while repeal is by simple majority at a referendum (Arts. 2, 14); a remark to the charter held this as a contradiction. Self-enforcement removes it: the lock is not what protects, and a simple majority suffices; a strong lock without self-enforcement is broken anyway (Orbán, 2011), a weak one with self-enforcement is left alone.

**Two poles — two different breakings of the lock.** *A healthy rich democracy* (an American city after three cycles): the lock is broken legally. At a high stake a minority takes — 30–35 %; the referendum on the rule is for everyone (Art. 14), and "the takers" have no majority. Those voting for a cut are not only the ideologues of "the sanctity of the vote" but "those who stay" themselves — they are the taxpayers and see the payment as a budget line (040t item 2); the one who stays has two pans — the share (for) and the tax (against), and at 30 % takers the share is only ×1.4. Forecast: no repeal (Alaska 1999 — 83 % against), but the percentage will creep down by referendums once a cycle, "for fiscal responsibility", and stop where the taking coalition is blocking again: **the percentage self-calibrates to the point where about half take**. In a healthy setting the protocol is not broken but whittled by those who stay, and survives weakened — a second ground for "the sick first" (040m §5d). *An autocracy after transition:* "the takers" are 60–70 %, the lock cannot be opened legally, and the government goes through the count — it initiates a referendum itself with a loaded question ("abolish payments to non-voters in favour of pensions and the army", 040w item 4) and paints the result. Here is a gap in the charter: **a referendum on the rule has no choice B** (Art. 14.1 — everyone votes), hence no double spend and no precinct balance — the counter's protection (Art. 10) does not extend to a repeal referendum; it is protected only by ordinary anti-fraud safeguards. Fraud is technically possible and maximally visible: 70 % know they voted against — an unsplit protest, and then not the charter decides but who the army is made of (040ag item 5). A candidate for the charter decisions — apply the precinct balance to the referendum on the rule as well (048k §3).

**Three weak points of self-enforcement.** *A government that cannot count:* a short horizon in government (040w item 5) — it may touch the rule without calculating; the mechanism then works through protest, not restraint. Self-enforcement guarantees not that the government will not touch, but that the one that touches will lose. *Legal whittling* at the healthy pole — against it self-enforcement does not protect at all, because that too is the majority's will. *Invisible encroachment* — but here the architect's move closes what looked like a vulnerability: "the median grows a little more slowly, but a cycle of two to four years is enough, and it does not matter what the inflation is". A subsidy is a number in currency, and inflation eats it silently; the payment is a percentage of the median over the last 12 months (048m Art. 2), tied to income, which carries inflation within it; the median lags by months, the cycle is years, and by the next election it has caught up. The creeping path is closed by the formula itself; the fifteenth decision is needed only against understating the median by whoever computes it — and that is not inflation but fraud, that is, a visible transgression.

**A consequence that did not exist before: the whole country watches the median.** The architect: "the whole country will watch the median household income and worry that it grows." Today it is a line in the statistics nobody knows; under the protocol it is the number on which the money of half the country depends, and it becomes the most discussed indicator — more than the exchange rate and more than GDP, because GDP grows for the oligarch and the median for the neighbour. Three consequences. For the first time the government is interested in the growth of the median, not the mean: the mean is raised by ten rich men, the median only by the income of half the population; the government's target indicator changes from what is convenient to show to what is felt. Understating the median does not pay the government either: a low median is a low payment, but also an admission "the country is getting poorer", with which one goes to an election before "those who stay", who remember (040x item 5); the fifteenth decision acquires a second guard — reputation. And the country watches one number honest by construction, which cannot be inflated without raising the income of half — the first counter that works between elections, not on polling day; Goldscheid's "budget as skeleton" (040o) made public and measured by one number.

## Weak point

A reconstruction; Przeworski did not write on payment for non-turnout and, as a minimalist, would view it with suspicion: anything that adds meaning to the procedure he held superfluous. The architect's move on the layer is a thesis resting on Downs and 1f §5, not on Przeworski's data; empirically it is supported only by the fact that outcomes in two-party systems are decided by fractions of a percent with the cores unchanged. And "freeing parties from the core" is a consequence that does not arrive in the first cycle: a party learns that the hostages are gone only after they have taken the payment.
