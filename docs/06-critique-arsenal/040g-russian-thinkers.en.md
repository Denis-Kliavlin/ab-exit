## 0. What the six have in common

All six — from Radishchev to Pivovarov — wrote about one thing: power-as-property above, a silent majority below, and a thin layer in between crushed time after time. Each proposed his own way out: enlightenment (Radishchev), rational egoism (Chernyshevsky), bitter honesty (Herzen), revolution from above (Eidelman), satire (Shchedrin), diagnosis (Pivovarov). AB-EXIT reads to them as a *mechanism* they never had: it does not require the peasant to read Kant.

## 1. Yuri Pivovarov: dissolving the feudal marriage

**Power-as-property.** The key concept of the "Russian System" — the inseparability of power and property: the state owns everything, including a person's political subjectivity; the citizen is a petitioner. AB-EXIT "dissolves the feudal marriage": "subject — master" relations become "shareholder — manager"; for the first time the Leviathan pays for what it took for free for centuries — the political silence of the masses.

**Population vs citizens.** A Res Publica never arose because the mass dissolves the active minority. The protocol is "a cruel but necessary scalpel": it stops pretending that 100 % want to be citizens, honestly admits that for the majority paternalism is more comfortable than freedom, gives them their share and lets them go into private life without shame — and thereby creates an incubator for a political nation: those who refused the money are the founding fathers of the new Res Publica.

**The end of fake unanimity.** Hybrid regimes rest on an imitation of nationwide support (mobilised state employees, "99 % for"). The thermostat takes away the state's **monopoly on interpreting silence**: when people return their votes en masse because the dividend fell, there is no hiding behind a painted rating.

**Verdict.** "A brilliant attempt to outwit history: instead of raising a free man — institutional protection built around his mercantilism and laziness. One problem: any bureaucratic Leviathan will sense its death in this transparency and resist to the last cartridge." The protocol's answer to the "last cartridge" is the zugzwang of the core electorate, see 056d.3: **institutional judo** — the weight of the Russian System (cynicism, corruption, contempt for the person) is used to throw the Leviathan over the hip. The state employee is the modern state serf ("I give you rations, you give me the ritual of obedience"); forbidding him to take the dividend turns the "strict but caring Father" into a robber, and the serf into an avenger.

## 2. Nikolai Chernyshevsky: the machine of rational egoism

The whole philosophy of "What Is to Be Done?" — man is by nature an egoist; calling for virtue is pointless; create conditions in which personal gain coincides with the common good. AB-EXIT is "a machine of rational egoism": "for 2,500 years idealists demanded that the dark, tired peasant think selflessly of the nation, while he thought of bread — and was bought with promises. Take the money and do not hinder the repair of the state."

**Ordinary people and new people.** Chernyshevsky would not condemn the dividend-takers — the mass is crushed by need; for them it is liberation, not sale. Those who refuse for x3 are pure Rakhmetovs: the system "automatically, without revolutions and terror, hands power to the new people, identifying them by a financial test".

**The artel.** Tying the dividend to the median is "Vera Pavlovna's dreams" at national scale: the managers work — the members receive; they steal — the member sees the fall, revokes the mandate, dismisses them. Resolution: "Stop calling Russia to the axe. Deploy the algorithm. Democracy without economic incentives is chatter for the well-fed."

## 3. Alexander Herzen: an obituary for ideals — and "cut"

**The victory of philistinism.** Herzen's chief enemy is the vulgarity of satiety; after 1848 ("From the Other Shore") he admitted with horror: the crowd does not want freedom, it wants peace. AB-EXIT is "the perfect machine of philistinism, bourgeois pragmatism carried to its chilling end: freedom is expensive, peace costs a dividend — take it and disperse. And most bitter: the majority will gladly queue."

**The death of the commune.** A cold smart contract instead of warm solidarity; "there is no collective wisdom of the masses — there is a sum of egoisms".

**Legalising the thinking minority.** But in those who refuse the money Herzen would see his heroes — people voluntarily choosing the burden of responsibility; the system "frees the thinking man from the need to humiliate himself before the crowd and prove the obvious to it".

**The honesty of disillusion.** "You tore off the masks: democracy pretended the vote was priceless — you hung a price tag, and the illusion turned out to be cheap." A verdict from "The Bell": "a great and sad obituary for humanity's youthful ideals — but as a doctor seeing gangrene, I say: cut."

## 4. Natan Eidelman: a bloodless Senate Square

**The Decembrists' dream.** Their tragedy: they wanted to give the people freedom but feared Pugachev's revolt — and came to the square without the people. AB-EXIT is "the algorithmised dream of Pestel and Muravyov: an institutional Senate Square without gallows and Siberia. You pay the people not to rob them but so that they do not burn the estates while the intelligentsia rebuilds the economy."

**Revolution from above.** All modernisations (Peter, Alexander II) were made by the elite with the masses passive; as soon as the masses joined — blood. The thermostat lifts the reformer's noose between conservative bureaucracy and a crowd demanding miracles: the manager gets historical time for unpopular saving reforms.

**The unflogged generation.** Refusing money for a vote is a Deed, a test of civic dignity: the system "by itself, without secret societies, identifies people for whom subjectivity is dearer than satiety — and hands them the country".

**The tragedy of the split.** The humanist's one regret: the algorithm records the defeat of a great illusion — the intelligentsia wanted to merge with the people, and the protocol "sends the two nations, thinking and surviving, to separate rooms of the smart contract". Verdict: "the victory of Decembrism translated into economics".

## 5. Alexander Radishchev: "with tears in his eyes — deploy"

Two men would collide in Radishchev. **The idealist**: "political castration of the people; I wrote to wake the peasant, and you pay him to sleep; a new, algorithmic serfdom with the technocrat as master". **The economist** (Radishchev was a brilliant customs official): "free speech and the vote mean nothing to a father with nothing to feed his children; the hungry always sell the vote to a tyrant for meat. Your system is more honest than my verses: the dividend is not a hand-out but a lawful share; taking it, a man ceases to be a slave of need." The thermostat is a safeguard against Pugachev's revolt: "no pitchforks needed — the people return to the polls and lawfully sweep the elite away".

**The architect's clarification that changes the optics:** Radishchev did not yet have the data that America too would come to populism and shouters. For eighteenth-century romantics early America was the City on the Hill. Show him 2026 — shows instead of debates, troll factories, crowds at the Capitol — and his shock turns to acknowledgement: "the vice was not in kings — in the architecture of universal voting itself; in classical democracy freedom was sold long ago — not for an honest dividend but for empty promises and hatred". From critic to evangelist: "a journey from the era of lies to the era of pragmatism; not a Republic of Angels but the only Republic of Pragmatists able to survive without sliding into dictatorship or chaos".

## 6. Saltykov-Shchedrin: revolt without leaving the hut — and a confrontation

**Monetising Glupov.** The Glupovites want to govern no one — they want not to be flogged and to chew their pie in peace. "For centuries our pompadours herded them to squares to shout 'hurrah' while the Glupovite picked his nose and thought how to slip off to the tavern. You say: here is money, go to the tavern legally — they will build you a monument. You turned our native indifference into hard currency."

**Algorithmic revolt.** "How is it done here? The authorities steal until the last cow is gone; then the peasant takes the pitchfork — long, bloody, unaesthetic. And with you: the treasury is empty, the dividend is gone, the Glupovite, without leaving the stove, sees in his smartphone that there is no money, and presses 'return the vote'. You replaced pitchforks with a smart contract — a revolt without leaving the hut."

**The satirist's trap.** Shchedrin's first feuilleton ended in "perfect autocracy with a human face: pay the severance — and you can saw the budget in absolute silence". The architect catches him: "not everyone left — the Decembrists stayed, and Shchedrin himself can vote x3". The corrected feuilleton: "You led a million shouters off the square to let ten thousand prosecutors into the office. The governor thought silence would come — and got a board of directors where everyone reads the budget with a magnifier and weighs three times. That is not silence, sir, that is a confrontation." The mayor can no longer hide behind free porridge — the porridge lovers pressed Exit; he is left one on one with a conditional Shchedrin. Resolution: "Approve immediately! For nature has produced nothing more fitting to our national character."

## 7. Weak point of the gallery

All six accept the mechanism for one reason — it is more honest than hypocrisy; and all six (except Chernyshevsky) mourn the same thing — the legally formalised divorce of two nations. The protocol answers with reversibility (a divorce for one cycle) and gamification (the vote as a goal of growth, 040b.5), but does not remove the cultural remainder that 006 §15.26 calls "Fromm's honest remainder". 🟡
