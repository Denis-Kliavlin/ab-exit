# 40bh. Political Scientists' Question 6: "Change in an Autocracy Will Come from Above, from the Elite; Nothing Can Be Born from Below"

*How to read. The sixth premise: an autocracy is a dead space of necro-institutions and imitation; nothing will be born from below, and the only hope is that in a crisis some elite body wakes up and remembers its powers. The protocol disputes both halves: from below there is an honest move, and from above it is easier for a weak elite to introduce the protocol than to replace the person at the top.*

**The series frame — the architect's move.** Political science wants the same thing the protocol delivers: a change of power by ordinary procedure, without bloodshed, through a law that cannot be repealed. It understands that the apathetic and the impulsive must be removed from the decision, but it knows no honest franchise qualification. And within the magnitudes and parameters that qualifications have had for two thousand years, it is right: every known qualification took the vote away from outside and hit the weak, and non-participation was a loss of the vote, that is, an illness ([006](../02-history/006-2500-years.md)). The architect: "AB-EXIT somehow went around all of this. I am in shock myself." The protocol is not a subtype of the known qualifications but another dimension: a qualification each person passes himself, in secret, by refusing money, and a balance in which nobody loses — the one who leaves gets the payment and the right to return, the one who stays gets double weight.

## 1. Who says so

- **E. Shulman** (2026, theses 8 and 9). The crisis of institutions in democracies is living life, the birth of the new; an autocracy that wants only to hold on creates a dead space of necro-institutions and imitation. But imitation can come alive: "the better you imitate something, the more you turn into it." The State Duma does not look like a parliament, but if this very convocation is present at "the great state funeral," it has large powers under the Constitution; the deputies gave them to the bosses for a full stomach and look too cowed to see the opportunity — yet it would be rash to forget it.
- **J. Linz** (040i): authoritarianism rests on demobilisation.
- **Guriev and Treisman** (040n): spin dictators rest on imitation.

## 2. The protocol cannot be imitated

Shulman divides institutions into living and dead. An autocracy lives on imitation: elections, parliament and turnout are performed. The protocol is the one institution that cannot be imitated. Turnout can be produced administratively. A payment to millions cannot be faked: if you "imitate" the payment, you pay it — it is money on cards and bank records (048m art. 10). Her own formula "the better you imitate, the more you turn into it" is taken to its limit here: to imitate the protocol is to carry it out.

## 3. The architect's move: the autocrat cannot cancel the referendum

On thesis 8: "It is also important that the autocrat cannot cancel the referendum. He would lose legitimacy himself — he pretends to be lawfully in power."

An autocracy lives on an imitation of legality, and performs it in earnest, because its claim to lawful power rests on it. A rule adopted by referendum and changed only by referendum (048m art. 14) is a piece of that very legality. Cancelling it by decree means saying openly that the people decide nothing and destroying one's own imitation. The losses come in two at once: legitimacy and the money millions of families are already receiving; to say by name "I am taking your payment away" is a kamikaze move that was not found among ten American politicians (059l §8b), nor among those holding a ballot slot against money for families (059m §5).

## 4. The architect's move: it is easier for a weak elite to offer the protocol

On thesis 9 ("the hope for an elite that dares"): "Here it is easier for a weak elite to offer AB-EXIT to the people — a no-lose, non-political topic with political consequences. Here is my insight: the autocrat calls political actions non-political — he closed a TV channel through law and taxes, banned a book over 'propaganda'. He constantly replaces politics with non-politics. AB-EXIT does the same — it beats him with his own weapon."

- An autocracy rests on substitution: a political decision is presented as an economic, tax or moral one, and one cannot argue with it politically.
- The protocol is the same substitution in reverse: in form a payment to families and a budget line, in consequence a change in the composition of the hall.
- One can fight it only by calling it political — that is, by exposing one's own method.
- So a weak elite finds it the easiest to take up: a successor or a group without its own base needs a popular measure without the stigma of opposition.

## 5. The architect's move: easier to introduce the protocol than to replace the person at the top

The second part of the move on thesis 9: "Shulman is right that the imitation parliament may wake. But what is easier for it — to propose dismissing the person at the top, or to introduce AB-EXIT? And then he will be dismissed by itself."

Removing the person at the top is a kamikaze move by name, and the deputies, per Shulman, chose a full stomach over power. Introducing the protocol is a non-political measure that can be adopted without declaring war on anyone. And the consequences do the rest: the apathetic take the payment and leave, ritual turnout disappears, and a government that rested on it is left without its base at the next election. The person at the top changes by ordinary lawful procedure, not by a decision of parliament. The protocol overthrows nobody and calls for nothing (1g) — the change of power here is a consequence, not an aim.

And this is exactly what political science itself dreams of (the series frame): a change of power by ordinary procedure, without bloodshed. Shulman hopes an imitation parliament will wake in a crisis, though she herself describes it as unable to wake; the protocol does not wait for the elite — citizens wake it from below, and to a weak elite it offers a move within its strength.

## 6. What follows

| Premise | What is true | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| an autocracy is dead institutions and imitation | yes | the protocol cannot be imitated: imitating the payment is the payment |
| nothing can be born from below | no | the oppressed have an honest non-political move (40bc §4) |
| the hope is an elite that dares | the elite is weak and cowed | a weak elite finds it easier to introduce the protocol — a non-political topic that beats the autocrat with his own weapon (the architect's move) |
| the imitation parliament may wake | possibly | it is easier for it to introduce the protocol than to remove the person at the top; ordinary procedure does the rest (the architect's move) |
| what is adopted can be cancelled | formally | the autocrat cannot cancel a referendum without losing legitimacy (the architect's move) |

**Boundary.** The protocol does not promise that an autocracy will adopt it; the route is a municipal pilot (056f) or a weak elite in transition (048d).

## Weak point

Shulman's quotations follow automatic subtitles. The scenario "a weak elite introduces the protocol" is a construction; there are no historical precedents for this particular measure.
