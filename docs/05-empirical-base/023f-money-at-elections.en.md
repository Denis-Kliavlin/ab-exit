# 23f. Money at Elections: From Arizona to Trump in Twenty Years

**Chapter:** 05 — Empirical Base
**File:** 05_023f · v1 · 30 September 2026 (session 30.09.26)
**Source:** two open items from the comparison with the closest projects (006b): the Arizona referendum of 2006 and the field experiments paying for turnout; the architect's conclusion "people are not against money at elections; while we spend half a year discussing whether it is legal and whether people are ready, Trump simply went and promised to hand out money". Figures checked on 30.09.2026. The legal side is not considered here: the protocol pays for non-participation, not for a vote (045, 029.4).

## 1. Why this section exists

The only referendum on money at elections lost. The only cash draw among voters passed without consequences. The only promise to hand money to everyone was made by a sitting president. Three events in twenty years show one thing: the ban on the words "money" and "elections" in one sentence was lifted not by arguments but by practice.

## 2. Three steps

**Arizona, 2006.** The citizens' initiative "Voter Reward" of the physician Mark Osterloh: after every election, draw a million dollars among those who voted, the money from unclaimed lottery prizes. Against it — both parties and every major newspaper in the state, with the words "bribery", "cheapens the vote", "lottery". Result: 33.4 % for, 66.6 % against.

**Musk, 2024.** Two weeks before the presidential election the America PAC committee announced that every day it would give a million dollars to a registered voter in a swing state who signed a petition. The Philadelphia district attorney sued, calling it an "illegal lottery" and a "scam". The judge refused an injunction, and the draws continued until election day. No political price followed: the candidate the committee supported won.

**Trump, 2026.** At the first Republican convention in history held before the midterms a "Trump dividend" was announced: 5,000 dollars to every adult citizen if the majority is kept. Analysed in 059c; the polls there: there is demand, there is no trust.

| | 2006 | 2024 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who proposes | a private person through an initiative | a candidate's committee | the sitting president |
| Who is paid | one random person among those who voted | one random person a day among those who signed | every adult |
| For what | for turnout | for a signature, that is, for registration and data | for the desired election outcome |
| What the elites said | "bribery", in a united front | "an illegal lottery", one prosecutor | analysed in 059c |
| How it ended | rejected two to one | the court did not stop it, the candidate won | the promise stands |

From "a taboo everyone strikes at" to "the winner's main promise" — twenty years. The word "bribery" was uttered every time, and every time it cost less.

## 3. How all three differ from the protocol

| | Arizona, Musk, Trump | The protocol |
|---|---|---|
| What is paid for | for coming, signing or voting the right way | for a declared exit from voting |
| To whom | to a random person or to one's own | to everyone who chose, by a formula in the law |
| Who pays | a private person, a committee, a promising power | the budget, regardless of who is in power |
| Whom it brings in | the apathetic to the polling station and onto the rolls | nobody; it leads the apathetic out |
| What the opponent is left to say | "you are buying turnout and votes" | "both rights are kept; the payment is for refusal, not for a vote" |

All three are purchases of participation: they pull to the polls those who do not care, and do so with a random prize or a promise. The protocol does the reverse. Arizona rejected the purchase of turnout by lottery; there has not yet been a referendum on payment for exit by formula.

## 4. Field experiments: the threshold of sensitivity to the sum

Panagopoulos (Journal of Politics, 2013) ran the first randomised field experiments with a monetary reward for turnout: Gilroy, California, November 2007, and a replication in Lancaster, California, April 2010. Voters were offered by mail from 1–2 to 10 and 25 dollars for taking part. Small sums did not move turnout; a noticeable sum did.

What follows for the protocol. First: people have a threshold below which money does not act on behaviour, and it is above a token sum — an argument against "a small payment to start with" and for the yardstick "desirable to 40–50 % of the population" (048k §3). Second: the experiments measured payment for turnout, the protocol pays for exit; the threshold does not carry over as a number, what carries over is the fact of its existence. Third: this is a calibration point for the simulator's formula (055c §6.3b) — its response to the sum has the same shape as the experiments: zero up to a threshold, growth after it.

## 4b. A fourth step: the half-billion of 2026 and the thesis "money does not win elections"

*Added 5 November 2026 after the US midterm elections of 3 November 2026 ([059o](../10-usa/059o-ship-turning-every-700-days.md)).*

**The thesis.** A commentator summed up Musk's spending on the 2026 campaign: he "managed to burn half a billion dollars in plain view without it once showing in the voting figures. Money does not win elections… there is no exchange rate of dollars for votes. In a competitive system you cannot buy yourself a senator or a governor."

**What is true.** A convinced voter cannot be bought, and this has long been known: a candidate's spending barely moves the result (Levitt, 1994); Bloomberg in 2020 spent more than half a billion and won American Samoa; Musk in spring 2025 put more than $20 million into a judicial election in Wisconsin and lost by ten points.

**Where the error is.** The word "vote" mixes two goods. The vote of a person with a stake is not for sale and has no price. The vote of the brought-in is for sale, and the price has been measured: Moldova 2024 — $39 million for 138,000 people, about $280 a vote ([019d §2](../04-electoral-dynamics/019d-market-price-of-vote.md)); the field experiments of §4 — $25 for turnout already moves it; the targeted package in Serbia ([057h §2.6](../09-other-countries/057h-serbia-2026.md)). The rate exists; it is hidden, in places criminal, and works only on the one who does not count. Money does not buy elections when the hall has a stake, and buys the margin when the hall is apathetic — and most elections are decided at the margin. In 2026 the bought margin drowned in the wave, as did the redistricting designed for a shift of a few points.

**The architect's move (5 November 2026).** "The author thinks Musk's money did not help to win, but it did affect the result, and what matters is that it affected it two ways: it brought some people in, and the personality and publicity of Musk's spending pushed some away. So the idea that one cannot buy is wrong. De facto votes are already bought in different ways, and today we cannot say exactly how many came because of Musk's money. AB-EXIT is about something else altogether: there everything is counted, exact and visible, there are no personalities, and the money is more honest."

What follows. The author of the thesis measures the outcome ("they did not win") and infers from it the absence of influence; these are different quantities. Half a billion worked both ways: it brought some in and mobilised others against itself, and the resulting zero in the figures is not "no effect" but the sum of two effects of opposite sign. Hence the main fact of the present system: the influence of money on turnout is unmeasurable. Nobody — neither the campaign, nor the donor, nor the opponent — can say how many people came because of that money and how many came in spite of it; every estimate is made after the fact from polls. This is a fictitious entry in pure form: the expense exists, the line in the accounts does not.

| | Money in a campaign today | The protocol's payment |
|---|---|---|
| Whose money | a private person's or a committee's | the voter's own, from his own taxes |
| For what | for coming, signing, voting "the right way" — never said aloud | for a declared exit; both rights are kept |
| Who receives it | those chosen by the campaign | everyone who chose, by one formula |
| The payer's personality | present — and it becomes an argument for or against in itself | absent: the treasury pays by law, whoever holds power |
| What is known afterwards | nothing: how many came and how many were repelled cannot be counted | everything: how many took, how many stayed, by district, every day (048m arts. 9, 10) |
| Side effect | repulsion from the payer | none: there is nobody to be repelled by |

And a fork for the thesis. If there is no exchange rate of dollars for votes, then the protocol's payment cannot "buy" anyone either — the objection "this is bribery" ([1g](../01-introduction/001g-objections-index.md)) is removed in the words of the critic of money himself. If the rate exists, the protocol makes it open, equal and bounded. Under the protocol the thesis "money does not win elections" becomes true always, not only in a wave year: the good that money used to buy is no longer in the hall — the brought-in voter has taken his own from the treasury, and the one who stayed is by definition worth more than D.

**The architect's second move (5 November 2026): the return exists; the public does not see it.** To the fork "if there is no rate, why were about $16 billion spent on the 2024 cycle and why is it spent again — either every campaign and donor errs every cycle, or the money buys something" the architect: "Yes, it is strange that such pragmatic people, used to counting KPIs and efficiency in money, so often and so heavily repeat their mistake. Perhaps they simply see the efficiency, and publicly it is not visible." This removes the first branch of the fork: people who count the return on every dollar do not repeat a loss-making operation for twenty years. So the return exists, and the public looks for it in the wrong place. The public measures money in votes on election day; the donor measures it in what he receives between elections. Three lines of his accounts that are absent from the public ones:

- **The margin.** The share of money predicts the share of votes in House races almost linearly (Ferguson, Jorgensen and Chen, 2016–2019): the rate exists in an ordinary year and vanishes only in a wave year.
- **Access and policy.** Decisions follow the preferences of the affluent and of organised groups, not of the median voter ([040aw](../06-critique-arsenal/040aw-gilens-page.md), Gilens and Page). A contribution buys not a senator but a call that gets answered.
- **His own balance sheet.** In the 2024 cycle Musk gave about $290 million; within weeks of the election his fortune rose by more than $100 billion, and his companies hold government contracts worth tens of billions. On that line the "burned" money is the most profitable operation of the cycle, and the half-billion of 2026 is the price of keeping it.

The public is shown the first line and told "it did not work"; the second and third never reach a campaign's reporting. It is the same accounting defect as with the vote: an off-balance-sheet item. Under the protocol the donor loses both hidden lines by design, without a ban: the margin of the brought-in has left the market by itself, and a politician whose survival is tied to a hall with a stake ([036d §1](../06-critique-arsenal/036d-nobel-fourth-circle.md)) answers first to those who count departments and contracts ([019c §6k](../04-electoral-dynamics/019c-reform-by-subtraction.md)), and only then the call.

**The architect's third move (5 November 2026): to deny the rate is not to see mines where they are.** "This is an important trap: with voters being bought in various ways, to say that there is no exchange rate. It is like not seeing the illegal drug trade and saying it does not exist, while the addicts die of a cold and the police do not catch the dealers. That is the problem: the rate of the vote has long existed, lives and develops, and around it Musk does not spend half a billion for nothing, while politicians and political scientists still say, on the basis of a beautiful but wrong hypothesis, that there is no rate. It is a very dangerous delusion to think there are no mines where there are."

What follows. Denying the rate is not an observer's neutral mistake but the condition under which the market works. A market that "does not exist" is not measured, not capped and not taxed: it has no ceiling, no receipt and no reporting, and death from it is recorded as a cold — the defeat of a "weak candidate", "voter fatigue", "a wave". The hypothesis is beautiful because it suits every side at once: the donor (he bought nothing), the politician (nobody bought him), the voter (he did not sell himself) and a science that measured the wrong good — the effect of a candidate's spending on a convinced voter — and from zero there inferred zero everywhere. To admit the rate is not to permit the buying of votes; it is to stop pretending it does not happen. Mine clearance begins with a map: the protocol is that map — one open price on the exit, adopted by referendum, and a counter showing where and how many took it. As long as the rate is denied, every next buyer walks onto a field that is deemed clean and wins the margin on it.

## 5. The main conclusion

The architect: "people are not against money at elections. While we spend half a year discussing whether it is legal or not and how ready people are, Trump simply went and promised to hand out money — as a bribe."

The question "is society ready for money at elections" was closed not by us. Society accepted a million-dollar draw and a promise of five thousand from winners; it rejected only a lottery from a private person twenty years ago. The protocol does not need to prove that money is admissible — that was done for it. It needs to prove one thing: that its money goes for refusal, not for a vote; to everyone, not to one's own; by a formula, not by a promise. Everything else from the dispute over Arizona is words that have already lost their value.

## 6. Weak point

The three events come from one country, and the first two from a presidential campaign with a record budget; the conclusion "society accepted the money" rests on the winner having paid no price, not on a measurement of voters' attitude to a payment for exit. Panagopoulos's experiments give a threshold for payment for participation in two Californian towns; for payment for exit and for other countries there is no threshold, and the yardstick "40–50 %" remains an estimate. 🟡
