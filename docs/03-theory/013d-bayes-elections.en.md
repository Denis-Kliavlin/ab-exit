# Elections as a Measuring Instrument: Comparing "Before" and "After" by Bayes

**Chapter:** 03 — Theoretical Foundations (paired with §13)
**File:** 03_013d · v1 · 19 September 2026
**Source:** the architect's question "can Bayesian mathematics be applied to AB-EXIT as an analysis or a proof, if one compares current elections with those after AB-EXIT"; his demand to name percentages and to find experiments in which one and the same action is compared free and at a price; two natural experiments (Peru, Switzerland).

## 1. An analysis, not a proof

Bayes' formula describes one thing: how to recompute confidence when new evidence arrives. It does not produce truth out of nothing: with arbitrary starting numbers the conclusion is just as arbitrary, only it looks scientific. So there is no "Bayesian proof of AB-EXIT", and announcing one would be exposing oneself. As an analysis, however, it gives what an argument in words does not: it reduces the whole question to one quantity and shows exactly what must be measured.

## 2. One quantity

An election result is evidence about the quality of the winner. The strength of the evidence is measured by the likelihood ratio:

LR = the probability of winning if the candidate is a good manager, divided by the probability of winning if he is a bad one.

At LR = 1 the result says nothing about quality: it is a lottery. At LR above one, winning is evidence of quality. At LR below one the system selects the worst.

**Today.** Winning depends on mobilising the indifferent, on name recognition, flattery and handouts (040c.3b) — on things barely connected with the ability to govern. There is independent confirmation that LR today is close to one: serious scholars propose replacing elections with a lottery and expect an improvement (015b.6b). A lottery is LR of exactly 1; if an improvement is expected from it, the present selection is no higher. The reason is that votes are not independent. Condorcet's theorem — "many voters yield a wise decision" — holds for independent judgments; a million and a half people led by one television and one handout are not a million and a half measurements but a few.

**After the protocol.** Those leave for whom the vote is worth less than the sum. Two things change at once: the average accuracy of the signal among those who stay, and the dependence between votes. That there are fewer voters matters little for Condorcet: six hundred thousand independent judgments with a weak signal tell candidates apart better than a million and a half dependent ones with none.

## 3. Accumulation over cycles

Posterior odds equal prior odds multiplied by LR for each cycle, and this compounds.

| LR per cycle | Shift in odds after 1 cycle | after 3 | after 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 (a lottery) | ×1 | ×1 | ×1 |
| 1.3 | ×1.3 | ×2.2 | ×3.7 |
| 1.5 | ×1.5 | ×3.4 | ×7.6 |
| 2.0 | ×2 | ×8 | ×32 |

The LR values in the table are an illustration, not a measurement. The table shows the mechanics: even a weak signal gives a manifold shift in the composition of government over a few cycles. This is another reading of "x10": not a leap within a year but the accumulation of selection. The fast effect comes from subtraction (019c.6b), the slow one from the change of selection.

## 4. The assumption and its test: the same action free and at a price

Everything rests on one assumption: those who stay choose at least a little more accurately than those who leave. The weak spot is named in 015c: refusing the money speaks of motivation, not competence. The architect proposed a way to test it: find cases where *one and the same action* — voting — is performed free and at a price, and see who is screened out. Such cases exist, and they have been measured.

| | Peru, the 2010 election | Switzerland, 26 cantons |
|---|---|---|
| What was changed | A random part of voters were told of a reduction in the fine for not voting — skipping became "cheaper" | Postal voting was introduced — taking part became cheaper |
| What happened to turnout | A 50 % cut in the fine — turnout −2.6 pp; 75 % — −5.3 pp | Turnout +5 pp |
| Who was screened out or came in | Those who left were in the centre of the political spectrum, less informed, and themselves valued their vote less | Those who came were less educated and knew the ballot propositions less well: the average education and knowledge of those voting fell |
| What else | Aggregate preferences for specific policies did not change; vote buying became rarer and the price of a vote higher | Welfare spending and business taxes fell |

The two experiments look at the same thing from two sides and give a consistent answer: when participation becomes dearer, the less informed leave first; when it becomes cheaper, it is they who come. The price of participation works as a filter on informedness, and this is not theory but measurement. The Peruvian experiment incidentally confirms 019d: as participation becomes dearer, vote buying thins out and the vote becomes dearer.

Outside elections the picture is less clear-cut, and this is worth saying. In an experiment in Zambia a higher price for a water purifier screened out those who used it less; in an experiment in Kenya with anti-malarial bed nets those who paid used them no more often than those who got them free, and demand fell sharply with price (both results from the assistant's memory, unchecked). A price does not always select "the best users". For voting, however, both direct experiments give the same sign.

## 5. Percentages

The architect rightly remarked that the assistant "as always slipped away from percentages". The assistant's estimates, subjective, with what shifted them:

| Statement | Before the two experiments | After |
|---|---|---|
| Those who stay choose more accurately than those who leave (LR after the protocol is above one) | about 60 % | 80–85 % |
| LR per cycle is at least 1.3 | about 35 % | about 50 % |
| Those who stay choose *worse* (LR below one) | about 20 % | about 10 % |

Why not higher than 85 %. First: the experiments changed the price of participation by a small amount, whereas D is half a minimum pension; at such a price the screening runs not only on informedness but on income, and an informed poor person leaves too. Second: "informed" does not mean "chooses the best manager" — an informed voter may choose in the interests of his own group. Third: a motivated voter may be ideologically blinded. These three objections make up the remaining 15–20 %, and each can be tested: by a survey with a real stake (who takes the money and how accurately he judges the government's results) and then by a pilot.

## 6. The base rate: why a person must not be checked

The same formula closes the question analysed in 019d.4b. Suppose one in fifty of those who refused the sum is a recruit, and the marker "poor and refused" correctly points to a recruit in 90 % of cases and falsely fires on 10 % of non-recruits. Then of a hundred people with the marker about fifteen are recruits and eighty-five are not: checking by the marker hits mainly the innocent. This is the base-rate fallacy, and it makes the rule "an anomaly at a polling station, not suspicion of a person" not only a principle of the protocol but arithmetic. At the level of a polling station the same formula works in favour of a check: a hundred and twenty refusals where twenty were expected leave almost no room for chance.

## 7. Weak point of the section

LR values have not been measured for any country; the table in §3 is mechanics, not a forecast. The estimates in §5 are the assistant's subjective probabilities: they honestly show the direction and size of the shift from evidence, but another person with other starting numbers will get others. Both experiments in §4 concern small changes in the price of participation in working democracies; carrying them over to a sum of half a pension and to a poor country is extrapolation. The main thing the section gives is not numbers but the question in measurable form: is the accuracy of a person's choice connected with his decision to stay. 🟡
