## 1. The old model

The value of one vote V = 1/N, where N is the number who turned up. The utility of participation U = p·B − C: p — the probability that the vote decides the outcome, ≈ 0; B — the benefit of "your" result; C — the cost of going. For the mass of people U < 0, and they do not vote for free. Any political-technology trick — advertising, GOTV, fear, charisma — moves C or briefly raises the emotional B, that is, moves the person *inside the same function*. The playing field remains V = 1/N. That is a tool, not a new system.

## 2. What the protocol does

An explicit choice appears. Option A, stay: V = 1/(N − M), where M is the number who exited for money; if half exit, V doubles. Option B, exit: U = D. The person now compares not U_vote with zero but the boosted U_vote with D: max(B/(N − M) − C, D). That is a different optimisation problem.

| | Old system | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Object of choice | Candidate or party | Money or an amplified vote |
| Value of a vote | 1/N | 1/(N − M) |
| Status of non-attendance | Noise, shame | Paid exit |
| Main resource | Mobilising the apathetic | An explicit choice of incentives |

A trick changes parameters inside the old function; a paradigm changes the choice function itself. Once the citizen's objective function changes from "go or not go" to "take D or get a x2–x3 vote", this is not a campaign technique but a change of the rule by which the rationality of participation is computed. Old politics optimises turnout inside V = 1/N; the protocol introduces the variable M and the new payoff D, which changes the structure of the equilibrium (013b.1).

## 3. The change of sign

The architect's correction: the dispute about "+2 % or +15 % efficiency" is the wrong scale. The state's utility for the citizen U = B − C: B — order, service, growth, predictability; C — taxes, barriers, corruption, noise, losses. In the current model, for many, U < 0: power wins with noise and promises, the costs of bad governance are smeared, the benefit of the carve-up and populism is concentrated, the citizen gets a state that hinders more often than it helps. After the change in the composition of those deciding, U > 0 becomes possible. That is not +5 % to the bad but a crossing of zero: from state-as-obstacle to state-as-instrument. The condition for the change of sign: those who remain must actually demand cheaper, clearer, a longer horizon, fewer parasites; if only the more ideologically charged remain, the sign may not change — the minus changes its form (§7, §8).

## 4. For whom the payoff changes

| Group | Share | How the protocol changes it |
|---|---|---|
| The apathetic, non-voters | 40–50 % | Instead of "I just don't go" — D |
| Ordinary voters | 25–35 % | Instead of equal noise — a chance of a x2–x3 vote |
| The conscious, motivated | 15–25 % | Influence becomes dearer and weightier |
| Machines, beneficiaries of noise | 3–7 % | The business model breaks |

For whom the picture changes — 93–97 %; for whom it directly gets better — 85–90 %; worse — 3–7 %; almost indifferent — 5–10 %. Usually it is not so: a typical political reform divides society 40–60 against 40–60, a party reform — "our base against theirs", a moral reform — supporters of the norm against opponents, a procedural reform — activists against machines and the habituated. "Better for almost everyone" happens only with direct personal benefit to a large mass: Social Security, deposit insurance, mass healthcare, the Alaska PFD — and precisely for that reason such institutions later live so stubbornly (048g). The protocol claims two mass benefits at once: money for those who exit and weight for those who stay.

## 5. Seven "magical" changes in a hundred years

Not slogans but shifts after which a system suddenly began to work noticeably better: **the secret ballot** (removed open pressure from employers and machines; the vote became more personal); **independent central banks** (politicians cannot print for elections; lower chronic inflation where it held); **budget rules and transparent procurement** (where the rule worked, the cost of the state fell and predictability rose); **targeted privatisation of loss-making state enterprises** (the asset stops living on an endless subsidy); **the Alaska PFD** (resource → direct money; it is harder for politicians to quietly carve up a fund when residents are waiting for a dividend); **containerisation** (one technical standard changed the scale of world trade — not +5 %); **vaccination and sanitation as a norm** (mortality fell by an order of magnitude). The common feature: it was not the "mood" that changed but **the rule by which the payoff of the key players is computed**; after that, behaviour adjusted by itself. The protocol claims the same: not another beautification programme but a change of the rule of who decides and with what weight. None of the seven was accepted easily (013c.7).

## 6. Park benches: thirty steps

A test of the scale "improvement or change of sign" — the most down-to-earth public good.

1. Benches are a local public good; their quality depends on who decides and who pays, not on slogans.
2. Today the decision is taken by politicians elected by a mixed mass: the interested + the random + the apathetic.
3. The apathetic barely feel the difference between a good and a bad bench in their choice at the election.
4. Their vote on this question is informational noise.
5. The politician wins not with ideal benches but with mobilisation, emotion, brand and general promises.
6. The park budget is an object of bargaining, PR and carve-up, not engineering optimisation.
7. The contractor works for whoever signs the contract, not for whoever sits on the bench.
8. The resident to whom the bench matters has a vote weighing 1/N.
9. With a large N his influence is negligible.
10. So he has a weak incentive to look into the material, the service life, the cost of repair.
11. The system produces average, expensive and often uncomfortable benches.
12. The current model optimises not "comfort per rouble" but "comfort for winning the election".
13. Now the protocol: some people officially exit and take the money.
14. Those for whom participation matters more than the payment remain in governance.
15. Their vote is heavier: 1/(N − M).
16. Those who care about parks, order and long-term benefit are more likely to stay.
17. Those who don't care are more likely to exit.
18. The bench voter becomes dearer and more influential.
19. It is harder for the politician to win with empty noise.
20. It pays him to show a measurable result: comfortable, clean, cheap to run.
21. The contractor begins to depend on a more demanding principal.
22. A demanding principal looks not at the press release but at life-cycle cost.
23. It pays to buy slightly dearer but for 15 years than cheap and repair every year.
24. Simple metrics appear: complaints per 100 benches, time to repair, price per year of service.
25. The budget starts working as engineering, not as a poster.
26. The benches become more comfortable because they are judged by those who care.
27. The benches become cheaper in the long run because the incentive for showy and rotten solutions disappears.
28. This is not "the protocol magically fixes parks".
29. It is a change in the composition of those whose vote determines economic decisions.
30. Result: the current system makes benches for mobilisation; the protocol — for people who bear responsibility for the choice.

The same principle in small as in big politics: quality grows not from moral appeals but from a construction in which a bad state is unprofitable for someone.

## 7. Who refuses the money and why that is not stupidity

The one who refuses D does not "fail to count" — he counts other quantities: not "$1,000 > one vote" but "$1,000 < the damage from bad government, the loss of the norm, the loss of influence, the loss of the status of principle". Three types of refusal: rational pragmatic ("if noise elects a bad government, I lose more than $1,000"), value-based but not stupid ("I don't sell participation, even for money"), status-based ("it matters more to me to be one of those who decide"). This is not stupidity, it is a different utility function; to refuse a concrete thousand for the sake of influence you need either calculation or a strong internal norm.

| Type of refuser | Estimate | Stability across cycles |
|---|---|---|
| Rational pragmatists | 8–12 % | Medium–high: stay while they judge influence worth more than money |
| Principled, value-based | 5–8 % | High: principle rarely changes because of $1,000 |
| Mixed, status-based | 3–5 % | Mobile: take the money one cycle, return the next |
| **Total refusing** | **~16–25 %** | The tired and burned-out go into money for a cycle and return |

Can they be trusted with the country? More than the current mix — not as saints but as a dearer principal: today the deciders are a few interested, many random, many mobilised, many almost indifferent, and the vote is cheap, so power is taken with noise. After the protocol the deciders are those who refused a concrete sum and have a higher stake in the outcome. Pragmatists can count the losses from bad government, ideologues hold norms and do not let everything reduce to short gain. They can still err, conflict, retreat into ideology — this is not a guarantee of an ideal country but a guarantee of a different quality of error: less power for those who don't care. And it is not an eternal caste: the right returns every cycle, the composition of those remaining is not cemented — a repeatable filter, not a lifelong status (015b.2, 040b.3).

## 8. Why the politician of the new world will not become an "ideologue of evolution"

From the structure of the new world, not from history. After the exit of the apathetic the electorate is dual: pragmatists (count the damage from bad governance) and ideologues (vote by principle). A politician who becomes a pure ideologue gets one part and loses the other — in the new world that is expensive. The pragmatist stays to vote precisely because he judges influence worth more than money if government wrecks the economy; he needs not slogans of evolution but figures, feasibility, reduction of losses, a long but verifiable effect. The fuel of pure ideological mobilisation disappears: before, the ideological politician topped up with the apathetic through emotion; now that mass is smaller, and what remains is an audience that asks: what exactly will change, when, at whose expense, how measured. The horizon lengthens, but not into myth: the ideologue of evolution promises transfiguration, the politician of the new world — a trajectory with intermediate checkpoints; the second survives, the first loses beautifully to the pragmatists. The main formula: the politician no longer optimises "how to bring in as many people as possible" but "how to convince those who consciously refused money for influence" — and those people refused not for a myth but for control over consequences. The winner is not a saint of evolution but a manager who can be trusted with a long cycle.

## 9. Weak point of the section

The weakest link is §3 and §7 together: refusing money proves not competence but that the person has enough money, or principle matters more, or political identity is high; that is not the same as the ability to choose the best manager of parks, budget and transport. Both very rational managers and very hard ideologues to whom efficiency is secondary may remain. The state is not a firm: coercion, courts, security, work with dependent groups are not optimised "like a pizzeria". And if the formula, the fund and the median become critical, the struggle moves from mobilising the apathetic to controlling parameters — the old noise leaves, a new front appears (048g.3). All percentages are projections. 🟡
