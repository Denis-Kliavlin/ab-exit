# 13g. The Wear of Democracy: Why Elections Do Not Improve Like Cars, and Why the Protocol Is Needed More with Every Cycle

**Chapter:** 03 — Theory
**File:** 03_013g · v1 · 15 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's thesis of 15 October 2026 and the assistant's elaboration. First recorded in a separate repository, "The Wear of Democracy"; moved here by the architect's decision the same day ("everything is to be written into the project's common repo").

---

*How to read. This is not a chapter about Trump and not a defence of democracy. It answers one question: why a system that has a buyer improves by itself while a system without one wears out by itself — and why it follows that the need for the protocol grows with acceleration rather than declining.*

## 1. The architect's thesis

"I ask my questions about Trump in order to understand that historically, as it seems to me, democracies — even ones as bright and high-quality as the American one — under open populism have begun to fail visibly after decades of accumulated errors and a change in the quality of the electorate. And the need for the protocol only increases rather than decreases, as it would if, by analogy with cars, everything were becoming better, faster and more reliable in elections and government."

## 2. A car has a buyer; elections do not

A car improves because it has a buyer who pays with his own money and checks the result with his own life. Every dollar of the buyer is a signal to the maker; the maker whose car is worse loses the buyer, and selection runs towards the better.

Elections have no buyer. The vote is free; the result is checked by someone who does not pay; the signal from the voter goes not to the producer of government but to the producer of the campaign. So the engineering of elections advanced no less than that of cars — only elsewhere: micro-targeting, big data, districting, the technologists' ten tricks (030b) became faster, more precise and more reliable. The product that is paid for improved — victory at the polls. The product nobody buys did not — government. Cars were made better because the driver paid; elections were made better for whoever paid — the campaign.

## 3. Three self-reinforcing processes

From the absence of a buyer follow three processes, each with positive feedback. That is why the need for a correction grows not linearly but with acceleration.

**The selection of the electorate runs towards the worse by itself.** Every cycle burns part of the thinking, and they leave (1f §6). The remaining hall becomes purer in composition: the share of the apathetic and the brought-in grows not because people get dumber but because the clever exit. This is Akerlof's market for lemons (1970): when quality is not paid for, the quality ones leave first, and the average falls with acceleration. Dalton recorded the growth of "apartisans" alongside the growth of education (040aj): the electorate "got worse" exactly to the degree that the best stopped taking part in it.

**Errors accumulate because there is nobody to write them off.** Every populist cycle (039b) leaves a debt, an obligation, a precedent, a broken norm. Only the one who pays can remove it, and the payer does not decide. Debt, tariffs, the unwritten guardrails, central-bank independence (059i §5b) — none is rolled back by the apathetic's vote, because the rollback hurts now and the horizon is three days. Fifty years of such cycles produce a system in which each next error is cheaper than the last for the one who makes it and dearer for the one who pays.

**The quality of the candidate follows the quality of the hall.** The candidate does not get worse by himself — he is selected to fit those who remain (059i §3, the mirror). As the hall's selection runs towards the worse, so does the candidate's. In two or three steps you get a president without an internal limiter (059i §5b) — not an accident but the limit of the sequence.

## 4. Old knowledge and what it lacked

Polybius described the cycle of forms in which democracy degenerates into ochlocracy (*Histories* VI, 3–9) — a separate chapter by the architect's decision. The US Founders built the republic knowing it would spoil and tried to slow the spoilage with checks. What nobody found: a feedback that reverses the selection — so that the quality ones return rather than leave. The car got it from the buyer. The protocol does the same with the vote: it puts a price on it (001b), and government gets, for the first time, a buyer who pays and remembers (040x item 3).

## 5. Caveat

In other dimensions democracy improved like a car: the franchise, civil rights, transparency, courts. One thing did not improve — the selection of the deciders, and that is what determines everything else over the long horizon.

## 6. What is tested

The three processes are mechanisms; that they accelerate rather than run at constant speed is a deduction from positive feedback, not from time series. The series that would test it — the share of the thinking who have left, by cycle — has been collected by nobody (1f §6b); the instrument for it exists (040aj §6). Until the measurement, the thesis "the need grows with acceleration" is a forecast, and the only thing that would refute it is a country where the share of the thinking who have left falls without a change of rule.

## Weak point

The car analogy is a model, not a measurement: government has a buyer in a weak form (the taxpayer, the investor in sovereign debt), and the question is why his signal does not reach the selection, not whether he exists at all. The US case is taken as the best documented, not the only one; for other democracies the three processes are untested.
