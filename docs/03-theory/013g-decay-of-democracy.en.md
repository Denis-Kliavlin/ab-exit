# 13g. The Wear of Democracy: Why Elections Do Not Improve Like Cars, and Why the Protocol Is Needed More with Every Cycle

**Chapter:** 03 — Theory
**File:** 03_013g · v1 · 15 October 2026
**Source:** the architect's thesis of 15 October 2026 and the assistant's elaboration. First recorded in a separate repository, "The Wear of Democracy"; moved here by the architect's decision the same day ("everything is to be written into the project's common repo").

---

*How to read. This is not a chapter about Trump and not a defence of democracy. It answers one question: why a system that has a buyer improves by itself while a system without one wears out by itself — and why it follows that the need for the protocol grows with acceleration rather than declining.*

## 1. The architect's thesis

"I ask my questions about Trump in order to understand that historically, as it seems to me, democracies — even ones as bright and high-quality as the American one — under open populism have begun to fail visibly after decades of accumulated errors and a change in the quality of the electorate. And the need for the protocol only increases rather than decreases, as it would if, by analogy with cars, everything were becoming better, faster and more reliable in elections and government."

## 2. A car has a buyer; elections do not

A car improves because it has a buyer who pays with his own money and checks the result with his own life. Every dollar of the buyer is a signal to the maker; the maker whose car is worse loses the buyer, and selection runs towards the better.

Elections have no buyer. The vote is free; the result is checked by someone who does not pay; the signal from the voter goes not to the producer of government but to the producer of the campaign. So the engineering of elections advanced no less than that of cars — only elsewhere: micro-targeting, big data, districting, the technologists' ten tricks (030b) became faster, more precise and more reliable. The product that is paid for improved — victory at the polls. The product nobody buys did not — government. Cars were made better because the driver paid; elections were made better for whoever paid — the campaign.

## 3. Three self-reinforcing processes

From the absence of a buyer follow three processes, each with positive feedback. That is why the need for a correction grows not linearly but with acceleration.

**The selection of the electorate runs towards the worse by itself.** Every cycle burns part of the thinking, and they leave (1f §6). The remaining hall becomes purer in composition: the share of the apathetic and the brought-in grows not because people get dumber but because the clever exit. This is Akerlof's market for lemons (1970): when quality is not paid for, the quality ones leave first, and the average falls with acceleration. Dalton recorded the growth of "apartisans" alongside the growth of education (040aj): the electorate "got worse" exactly to the degree that the best stopped taking part in it.

**Errors accumulate because there is nobody to write them off.** Every populist cycle (039b) leaves a debt, an obligation, a precedent, a broken norm. Only the one who pays can remove it, and the payer does not decide. Debt, tariffs, the unwritten guardrails, central-bank independence (059i §5b) — none is rolled back by the apathetic's vote, because the rollback hurts now and the horizon is three days. Fifty years of such cycles produce a system in which each next error is cheaper than the last for the one who makes it and dearer for the one who pays.

**The quality of the candidate follows the quality of the hall.** The candidate does not get worse by himself — he is selected to fit those who remain (059i §3, the mirror). As the hall's selection runs towards the worse, so does the candidate's. In two or three steps you get a president without an internal limiter (059i §5b) — not an accident but the limit of the sequence.

*Serbia 2026 as a case of the three processes and the blind zone of the short horizon — [057h](../09-other-countries/057h-serbia-2026.md): decay is invisible not in general but to the apathetic, and the autocrat builds the regime in that zone (the architect's move, 28.10.2026).*

## 3b. The cornerstone: a government living on bought turnout benefits from a poor voter

*The architect's move of 16 October 2026, from the Landemore reading (040au §4) and the rentier-state hypothesis (057d §3b): "You have just described the cornerstone of all non-democratic regimes and even of part of the democratic ones — and the protocol knocks it out from under the government completely. Translated into a corporation, it is a director on a big salary who does not want the company to bring profit to the shareholders and deliberately makes bad decisions." Recorded on his own condition: "find confirmation from clever people that it is so, and not only my guesses."*

**The rule.** Any government that lives on bought turnout has an interest in a cheap voter, and a cheap voter is poor, dependent and not reading. That interest directly contradicts the task of governing well: good government makes people richer, and a rich voter gets dearer and stops being buyable. So such a government governs badly not from incompetence but by calculation — and this explains why countries that have everything stand still for decades. It is the third process of §3 (the candidate fitted to the hall) seen from the government's side: it not only fits itself to the hall, it fits the hall to itself. The rentier state (057d §3b) is the limiting case; the payroll of votes (039 §88.9) is the general mechanism.

**Confirmation from clever people.** This is not the repository's guess but an established result of three literatures that never joined it.
- *Acemoglu and Robinson, "Economic Backwardness in Political Perspective" (APSR, 2006):* the "political replacement effect" — an elite blocks development when development threatens its power, and blocks it the more readily the weaker the competition; on this they explain why nineteenth-century Russia and Austria-Hungary held back industrialisation while Britain and Germany did not. Poverty as policy is their central thesis, only about industry, not about the vote.
- *Magaloni, "Voting for Autocracy" (2006):* the PRI held Mexico for seventy years by "making millions of ordinary citizens vest their interests in the survival of the regime" — through discretionary transfers the regime could withdraw; citizens could not "throw the rascals out" because they voted not to lose the ration. *Díaz-Cayeros, Estévez and Magaloni, "The Political Logic of Poverty Relief" (2016):* clientelism distorts poverty relief itself, because the regime needs not the end of poverty but dependence on its hands.
- *Robinson and Verdier, "The Political Economy of Clientelism" (2013):* public employment is the most reliable purchase of loyalty because it is revocable (already in 039 §88.9).
- *Stokes, Dunning, Nazareno and Brusco, "Brokers, Voters, and Clientelism" (2013)* and *Bueno de Mesquita and Smith, "The Dictator's Handbook" (2011)* — by the standard accounts: machines buy the poor because a dollar is worth more to the poor; a ruler with a narrow coalition benefits from a poor and uneducated population, because a rich and educated one is expensive and dangerous.

Joining the three literatures gives the rule: the elite blocks growth (Acemoglu–Robinson) because growth makes the voter expensive (Stokes), and an expensive voter cannot be kept on a revocable ration (Magaloni, Robinson–Verdier).

**The corporate analogy — entrenched management.** A director on a big salary who does not want profit for the shareholders, because rich shareholders will hire an auditor and fire him, while small holders vote at the meeting for whoever hands out gift baskets; he deliberately makes bad decisions and keeps the shareholders small and dependent on handouts. That is the payroll of votes in a corporation. The cure in the corporate world is known: the activist investor who counts (the counter-audit, 059l §4) and the shareholder who refuses the gift and votes his stake. The protocol is that cure for the state: the one who refuses the gift gets ×2, and management is left without a cheap meeting.

*The architect's addition: three conditions without which the cure does not work.* "A corporation must have a transparent and exact budget and profit calculation — otherwise nobody can prove anything; access to information; and an external verified indicator, like the share price on the market. The protocol gives exactly this, and without it the boss is always right." A shareholder can fire the director only because profit is counted the same way for everyone, information is open, and the director does not draw the share price himself; without these three, the profit "is there", the report "is in order", the dissenter "does not understand". The protocol gives all three. *A transparent calculation:* the median is computed from the same tax base by which taxes are collected (the fifteenth decision, 048m art. 2 part 2), the budget and the formula are published on day one, and ten competing teams count them (059l §4). *Access to information:* the open code of the registry and the counter (art. 15 part 4), the charter, 1d and llms-full. *An external indicator* the state never had: the share of takers — a quotation of the government that the government cannot draw: when more take, its "shares" fall, and that is visible every cycle, without polls and without trusting the counter (art. 10, the reconciliation of two records). The thermostat (040aj item 4) is the state's share price, traded on an open market for the first time.

**What the protocol knocks out.** The voter on whom the government depends becomes the one who cannot be bought; for the first time the government has no benefit in keeping him poor and has a benefit in his staying — that is, in his having something to vote for. The government's interest and the growth of prosperity point the same way for the first time. This is the "×10 efficiency" (059c §3) and "the best managers" (040ah §4) derived not from good intentions but from the stone being removed.

## 4. Old knowledge and what it lacked

Polybius described the cycle of forms in which democracy degenerates into ochlocracy (*Histories* VI, 3–9) — examined in §4b. The US Founders built the republic knowing it would spoil and tried to slow the spoilage with checks. What nobody found: a feedback that reverses the selection — so that the quality ones return rather than leave. The car got it from the buyer. The protocol does the same with the vote: it puts a price on it (001b), and government gets, for the first time, a buyer who pays and remembers (040x item 3).

## 4b. Polybius and payment for attendance: the engine of wear without an exit

*The architect's decision of 15 October 2026: Polybius is examined not as an arsenal chapter ("rather weak arguments") but as a page here — for describing the engine of wear 2,200 years before us, and for a historical fact of the opposite sign.*

**The engine.** *Histories* VI, 4–9 and 57: monarchy → tyranny → aristocracy → oligarchy → democracy → ochlocracy → monarchy again. He describes the spoiling of democracy in three parts, and all three are our processes of §3, without feedback. *First:* the spoiling begins when the generation that remembered why the form was introduced has gone, and the children raised under it take it to be free (VI, 9) — the horizon (013 §13.2) and "the vote is worth nothing today" (039b §4b). *Second:* the ambitious, unable to win office by merit, spend their fortunes "tempting and corrupting the people" (VI, 9) — the populist cycle (039b) and the purchase of turnout (030b). *Third:* the mob, "accustomed to feed on the property of others", having found a leader, installs the rule of force, and ochlocracy ends in a master, because the exhausted look for one themselves — the core on salary (039 §88.9) and the infantile ruler without a limiter as the limit of selection (059i §5b). His remedy is the mixed constitution, Lycurgus and Rome (VI, 10–18): the forms hold one another. His own verdict (VI, 57): everything decays, "as iron rusts", Rome too.

**What he lacks.** One thing: where those who remembered go. In Polybius they die; in ours they leave the polls without dying (1f §6). In the polis exit meant ostracism or banishment, and "stopped coming to the assembly" was no category for a Greek: the producer who stayed in the city and left the forum he did not see, because the forum was a duty, not a choice. Hence the rest: for him the vote is either free or bought by the ambitious — a price set by a rule does not exist; the mixed constitution slows the cycle but does not bring back those who left, because for him those who left are dead. That is the difference between a filter and a mirror (059i §5): checks hold the forms, the protocol reverses the selection.

**The fact of the opposite sign.** Antiquity knew payment for the vote — payment for attendance. Athens from the 390s BC paid for presence in the assembly (the misthos, from one obol to three under Agyrrhius), and Aristophanes in *Ecclesiazusae* (392 BC) mocks whom it brought to the Pnyx. Rome paid in grain: Gaius Gracchus's law of 123 BC on cheap grain, free distribution under Clodius in 58 BC, and half a century later — Caesar. Polybius saw not payment in general but payment for attendance, which brings to the forum exactly those he called the mob and hands them to the ambitious. Both times the mechanism ran his course to the end. The protocol pays with the opposite sign — for leaving — and the rule pays, not the candidate: nobody gains office from the payment (048m arts. 4, 15). It is the best historical proof that the sign of the payment matters more than the payment: the same obol, pointed the other way, dismantles the engine instead of starting it. The corruption Polybius described is the present system with its benefits and indexations, only without a ceiling or a receipt (040ab, 059j).

**Weak point.** The translation of Polybius's "mob" into "the brought-in" is ours. Rome refuted his forecast in form (empire instead of ochlocracy) but confirmed it in mechanism (from the dole to Caesar). The quotations from VI, 9 and 57 are paraphrased from the standard translations; the exact passages should be checked against an edition. The size of the misthos and the dates of the doles are from standard references, not from the sources.

## 5. Caveat

In other dimensions democracy improved like a car: the franchise, civil rights, transparency, courts. One thing did not improve — the selection of the deciders, and that is what determines everything else over the long horizon.

## 6. What is tested

The three processes are mechanisms; that they accelerate rather than run at constant speed is a deduction from positive feedback, not from time series. The series that would test it — the share of the thinking who have left, by cycle — has been collected by nobody (1f §6b); the instrument for it exists (040aj §6). Until the measurement, the thesis "the need grows with acceleration" is a forecast, and the only thing that would refute it is a country where the share of the thinking who have left falls without a change of rule.

## Weak point

The car analogy is a model, not a measurement: government has a buyer in a weak form (the taxpayer, the investor in sovereign debt), and the question is why his signal does not reach the selection, not whether he exists at all. The US case is taken as the best documented, not the only one; for other democracies the three processes are untested.
