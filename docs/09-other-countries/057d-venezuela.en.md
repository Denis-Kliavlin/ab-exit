## 1. Why without the protocol there will be a Maduro-2

Colossal resources and completely destroyed institutions are a textbook trap: classical "by the book" democracy almost certainly slides into a new cycle of populism: a charismatic arrives, promises to hand out the oil money to the people, the people vote, everything repeats. The clientelism of the barrios — cheap food and promises in exchange for votes — does not disappear with a change of flag.

## 2. How the protocol reassembles the country

**The oil dividend as the killing of clientelism.** PDVSA revenue goes into a Norwegian-type sovereign fund; part of the fund forms the dividend (the protocol ties D to the median wage, §1, but for a resource economy the fund is the natural source of funding the payment). Millions of the poor from the slums receive real money directly, not promises; the populists have nothing to trade — the voter has already taken the money and left the market.

**The oil technocracy.** Who stays at the urns in Caracas and Maracaibo: the returning diaspora from the US and Europe, surviving entrepreneurs, oil engineers, doctors, students — those who need not $1,000 but the restoration of property rights, the return of Chevron and ExxonMobil, the restoration of production. A parliament of businessmen and technocrats with free hands: not flirting with the poor but pumping oil, rebuilding infrastructure and making sure the fund pays the dividend.

**The thermostat against the resource curse.** The trouble with all oil countries is that sooner or later the elite steals from the fund because society does not control it. Here, as soon as the technocrats keep too much for themselves and the dividend falls (or is eaten by inflation from bad macroeconomics), the slum dwellers see it in their wallets and, instead of guarimbas (the bloody street protests of the Maduro era), press the button, refuse the fallen dividend and sweep the elite away at the next election.

## 3. The sine wave

1. *Shock recovery:* the technocrats open the markets, the oil flows, the nation grows richer — the poor are fed, the elite rebuilds its capital.
2. *Overheating and corruption:* some ten years later the new elite bronzes over and carves up contracts; the fund's revenue falls.
3. *The thermostat's blow:* the dividend declines, millions return to the polling stations, fire the government, hire hungrier managers.
4. *Diversification:* the new managers understand that depending only on oil is dangerous (prices fall — the dividend collapses — they are fired) and invest in tourism, IT and agriculture to protect their seats from the thermostat.

Summary: Venezuela as a corporation where oil is the asset, the technocrats are the board of directors, the people are holders of preferred shares who can always fire the board if the dividends stop coming. The fastest road to a Latin American economic miracle — provided the regime has already fallen (see 048d on how it falls).

## 3b. Poverty as a vote-price policy: why the oil does not reach the people

*The architect's hypothesis of 16 October 2026 (from the Landemore reading, 040au §4): "the destitute do not vote under any rule, and the vote of one who is surviving is needed only by whoever buys it — perhaps that is why even oil-rich countries like Iran or Venezuela still keep the people in poverty?"*

The repository already holds this mechanism under another name — the payroll of votes (039 §88.9): dependence on the budget is the most reliable purchase of loyalty, because a salary and a ration can be taken away tomorrow (Robinson and Verdier, 2013). The oil state is the limiting case: it does not depend on taxing the producers and can put the whole population on the payroll. But the payroll rests on two conditions. The vote must be cheap — and it is cheap only for the poor. The ration must be irreplaceable — and it is irreplaceable only where there is nowhere else to get anything. A people grown rich makes both conditions impossible: the price of its vote rises above what the regime can pay, and the ration becomes small change. So the rent goes not into the population's incomes but into distribution through the regime's hands — petrol for pennies, food boxes, subsidies, all stamped with who gave them.

Venezuela showed this in pure form: the "Fatherland Card" (Carnet de la Patria), scanned at "red points" beside polling stations in exchange for CLAP food boxes, is bringing-in (1f §5, 030b) raised to a state programme. Iran in 2010 even took a step towards D — it paid cash to every citizen instead of fuel subsidies — but tied the payment to the state rather than to a rule, and got loyalty rather than weight (057c §6b). Both countries already pay their people now, only with the opposite sign: for presence and for dependence, like Athens and Rome (013g §4b).

Hence the hypothesis — not the only cause of poverty (sanctions and misgovernment remain), but an explanation of why the oil reaches the regime and not the people wherever the regime lives on bought turnout: **poverty is a vote-price policy.** The regime rations prosperity so that turnout stays affordable. This is Lipset's thesis confirmed in the mirror (040ac): wealth leads to democracy because it makes the people too expensive to buy. And in such a country the protocol is not a reform but the most expensive thing the regime can imagine: it makes the vote dear with one number, without waiting for the people to grow rich — which is why the regime and the protocol are incompatible by arithmetic, not by ideology (057c §2: the theological attack is only the dressing of that arithmetic). The general rule and its confirmation in the literature — 013g §3b. A row in 1i.

## 4. Weak point of the case

The scenario substitutes a resource fund for the protocol's formula (median wage); in an economy where the median wage has collapsed and the rent is enormous these two sources diverge radically — and precisely this divergence must be fixed in the country statute (§14 Alaska is the precedent of a fund, but without the electoral option). A thermostat through the fund is sensitive to the world oil price, not to the quality of governance — step 4 of the sine wave (diversification) treats this, but only from the second cycle. 🟡
