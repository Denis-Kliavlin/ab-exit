# France: Le Pen, the Yellow Vests, the Unions, the Farmer and a "De Gaulle of the 21st Century"

## 1. Why France is the proving ground

If the protocol works in France — a country with one of the most bloated and reform-resistant bureaucracies, with a culture of the grève and a "peripheral France" — it will work anywhere. Plus a unique legal instrument: **Article 11** lets the president put a bill on the organisation of public authority to a referendum bypassing parliament; that is how de Gaulle introduced direct presidential elections in 1962 amid howls from parliament and the lawyers. A promise of a referendum on AB-EXIT is deliverable without the consent of the National Assembly and the Senate, which would block it.

## 2. Le Pen: the public façade and the mathematics of collapse

**The façade** (three pillars): "technocratic fascism and an elite conspiracy — Macron, Brussels and Wall Street have decided to buy France"; "an insult to the Republic — our ancestors shed blood for universal suffrage"; "protection of the poor — a caste society where engineers rule over slaves".

**The mathematics.** Her feeding base is the yellow vests, depressed regions, farmers, a working class tired of inflation; precisely they will be the first to take the dividend and voluntarily leave the polling stations. Those who refuse the money are the cadres, entrepreneurs, IT, medium and large business — the class that historically forms the "republican front" against her; the protocol leaves only her enemies at the urns and multiplies their weight. And she loses her monopoly on "salvation": she sells the hope of care — the protocol delivers care in advance, without intermediaries; a calm person who has left the cognitive tunnel does not need hysterical slogans. She would fight to the last drop of blood — defending the right to "feed the majority badly" with fairy tales.

An analysis of her own electorate: 34–36 % in the first round is not 35 % of convinced nationalists; at most 10–15 % are ideological, the other 20 % are tired people with falling purchasing power voting for "protection and money".

## 3. The yellow vests: a crash test

**Portrait** (Institut Montaigne et al.): not marginals — the precariat and the lower middle class: ~33 % clerical employees, 14–26 % workers, 17–21 % pensioners; peripheral France (villages, suburbs, small towns); the trigger was a fuel tax with the car as the only way to get to work; political newcomers, many abstainers or Le Pen/Mélenchon voters.

**If the protocol had been introduced a month before the tax.** (a) Removal of acute pain: people went to the roundabouts not out of love of freedom but because there was nothing to pay for petrol with; with the hole in the budget closed, cortisol falls — a well-fed person cannot be driven under the water cannons. (b) Monetisation of escapism: they already despised the establishment and did not vote — selling a vote you do not use for money for the family is an honest deal. (c) Destruction of victim status: protest feeds on resentment of the "president of the rich"; but you pressed the button yourself, took the money yourself, the state did its part — the object of hatred disappears; "it is psychologically impossible to hate the ATM that saved you from bankruptcy". Result: 300,000 furious vests → 300,000 consumers in the supermarket; and crucially, what united them was common poverty and common grievance, whereas relations with the state become individual and market-based: the one who took the cheque feels not solidarity with the crowd but the relief of a private owner. The clarification on payment timing (once per cycle, not monthly) does not change the conclusion: "a hefty one-off cheque — the equivalent of a new plough or a closed predatory loan — is a harder trigger than smeared-out pennies".

## 4. The unions against Macron

**CGT:** "individual bribery instead of solidarity — turning the proletariat into a herd of egoists chewing a cheque in front of the TV"; "the capitalists' trap — you take the money today, and tomorrow the engineers abolish the 35-hour week and raise the retirement age to 70, and you cannot vote against"; "privatisation of democracy, a digital guillotine — general strike".

**Macron (reconstruction):** "the gentlemen of the CGT shout about solidarity but fear losing their monopoly — for decades they have held France hostage by blocking the railways; AB-EXIT gives power directly, without intermediaries"; "the unions propose striking for weeks, losing wages, for a 50-euro rise; the protocol gives a dividend at the press of a button — that is pouvoir d'achat"; "we trust the intelligence of the French citizen: if you think the unions are right, refuse the cheque and vote with amplified weight; why do the unions consider the French children?".

**Who wins.** The strike collapses through sabotage by its own members: a worker with unpaid bills looks at the leader with the megaphone, then at the button "receive payment" — and quietly presses it; with 60–70 % taking the money the picket physically does not assemble. The union movement is not suppressed — it is elegantly dissolved by a market mechanism. (Weak point: this is a model of one strike, not of the institution of collective bargaining, which the protocol does not abolish.)

## 5. Who stays at the urns and who is the real underdog

The refusers are three groups: **cadres** and entrepreneurs (top managers, engineers, IT, SMEs), **bobos** (the urban educated intelligentsia, professors, the creative class, affluent eco-activists), **the traditional bourgeoisie** and affluent pensioners. Le Pen and Mélenchon vanish into oblivion (their bases take the dividend); Macron's centre and the Republicans keep the cadres and the bourgeoisie with amplified weight.

The first version of the model declared the moderate left and the greens (Glucksmann, Faure) the underdog — their "bobo" base would refuse the money for the sake of values. The architect: "the greens — do they tell the truth or exploit fear of progress? human rights and institutions — or bureaucracy?" The reassembly: the greens are populists for the rich, trading in fear of apocalypse instead of fear of poverty (closing nuclear plants — the cleanest and most efficient source — on a wave of irrational fear, leading to an energy crisis and dependence on foreign gas; anti-engineering, Luddism in a pretty wrapper); "human rights and ecology" in practice are ESG rent, ethics committees, an administrative market à la Gaaze that protects itself. The real battle after the vests and the marginals are filtered out is not right against left but **creators against regulators**: techno-optimists (entrepreneurs, scientists, factories, nuclear physics, the new agrarians, venture capital — those who generate the taxes from which the dividend is paid) against the new nomenklatura (Eurocrats, ESG consultants, the grant-funded urban elite, the left professoriate — whose aim is to keep the status of priests when the flock has left). The engineers will discover a paradox: "we pay the cheques to calm the mass — why do we also pay an army of eco-officials forbidding us reactors and data centres?" — and with amplified votes push that bureaucracy to the roadside.

## 6. The debate for the farmer

**The populist:** "a metropolitan technocrat has brought a contract from Paris lawyers; your grandfathers ploughed this land, and he offers you to hand over your vote for a hand-out; press the button and you become their pet; they'll strangle you with a diesel tax and import Ukrainian grain, and you won't be able to vote against; don't sell your birthright for a mess of pottage". Strong: it strikes at peasant pride.

**The pragmatist:** "Jean, what greatness are they singing to you about? A 14-hour working day, the tractor mortgaged to Crédit Agricole, 50 pages of reports for a diesel subsidy to an official who has never seen a cow; a politician buys your vote every five years with promises he forgets the next day. Your poverty is his job: while you are stressed, you vote for him. This is not a hand-out — it is your share in the business 'France'; not forever — throw off the yoke now: close the loan, buy fertiliser at a normal price, get out of the survival tunnel. And when the farm is back on its feet — refuse the cheque, take back your vote with amplified weight and dictate the grain tariffs to the Paris idlers yourself."

Why the pragmatist wins: the debt card beats the flag card (pride does not pay for a seeder); hatred of bureaucracy is stronger than hatred of globalists ("no papers, the money comes automatically" is a religious liberation; the populist offers "new support programmes" — new circles of hell); the psychological fuse "I will come back" — the money is taken not as capitulation but as a tactical manoeuvre; the ego is not saved but motivated.

**Second round: "not everyone has to know how to drive the bus".** "When a cow falls ill, do you call the vet or convene a village meeting to vote on the medicine? When the gearbox goes, do you trust the engineer or demand that every resident of the commune have an equal right to turn the nuts? You are a professional in farming. Why should the financial system and import tariffs be tuned by people who understand them less than a city hipster understands goat milking? We propose hiring a professional driver for the bus called 'the State'." Within 1–2 cycles the farmer sees the result on his account (simplified rules, predictable fuel, halted inflation) — and society moves from "sacred right" to "quality of service": as with the internet — nobody configures the provider's backbone routers, everyone wants the Wi-Fi to work. Awareness of incompetence as a good; a cult of professionalism instead of "the cook running the state".

## 7. The underdog and a "de Gaulle of the 21st century"

A technocrat candidate of the David Lisnard type (mayor of Cannes, president of the AMF, leader of Nouvelle Énergie, chief critic of the "technostructure" and impuissance publique) polls at 5–8 %: too dry for the TikTok era. But if he comes out not with a 500-page programme but with one proposal — a referendum on AB-EXIT as his first decree — the game breaks:

- **minus 15–20 % for Le Pen**: half her electorate goes to whoever offers a guaranteed cheque instead of a mythical cut in petrol prices; she falls back to her historical minimum of ~15 %;
- **plus 10–15 % of pragmatists**: IT, engineers, business, centrists tired of Macron;
- **plus ~10 % of the "silent"**: the 25–30 % who do not vote realise their lawful income is at stake and come for the sake of the referendum itself.

First-round result: from 5 % to 35–40 %; second round — 70 %+, because there is nobody left to vote against. The populists' zugzwang: criticise ("don't sell your votes") — look like an elite taking 1,500 euros from the poor; support ("I'll give it too") — confirm someone else's idea and lose the status of opposition.

**Referendum turnout** — 85–90 % (procedural referendums ~30 %, the EU Constitution in 2005 — 69 %): for the first time non-participation costs money; FOMO; the rich understand they will lose the right to govern, the poor that they will lose the money; principled abstainers come because for the first time their vote decides whether the account is topped up. This is a referendum not "against someone" but about the rules of the game — a "national wedding".

**Why he would take the risk.** For a French politician "de Gaulle of the 21st century" is the ceiling of ambition; but the system is built for risk avoidance (ENA and Sciences Po teach one to be a systemic player; a lost referendum is a political corpse). The protocol breaks cowardice with mathematics: 70–80 % for the referendum, the legitimacy of the founder of the Sixth Republic, populists without a foundation — fear gives way to "historical inevitability".

## 8. The first 48 hours and the first year

**Decree on trust and transparency:** (1) the first tranche of the dividend in accounts within 24 hours — "we are not politicians, we are managers; said — done"; (2) the "digital guillotine" — abolition of the three most senseless and expensive procedures (paper tax returns for individuals, redundant SME inspections, company registration); (3) the "live balance sheet" — the country's public P&L from a phone: revenue, expenditure, debt, dividend forecast (§4).

**A year later** (the architect's estimate: the first ten laws save ≥10 % of the budget, the payment costs 1–2 %): digital audit of state corporations and a ban on "zombie subsidies"; merger of duplicating layers (communes/departments/regions); automation of benefits. The end of the strike culture — blocking a motorway damages your own profit; capital inflow; civil servants from "bosses" into "engineers of the system". 1–2 % on the dividend instead of ~20 % on "maintaining loyalty" through non-working programmes — the best deal in the history of politics. The president a year on is not a messiah but "the best chief engineer"; the next candidate is forced to promise not a "change of course" but "+0.5 % to the dividend through optimisation".

**The reformer's fear of a populist at the next election** is removed twice: the currency of trust is not words but the dividend ("I cut 50,000 bureaucrats — painful for them, but look at your account"); and the architect's clarification: even in hard times the populists *have no audience* — the reformer explains that the difficulties are temporary, shows the dashboard and asks for time for reforms that take time, like a manager before a board; "a seller of air in a world of sellers of results" looks like a raider, not a saviour.

## 9. Two flanks, one protocol

The pitch to the right (Lisnard): not "handing out money" (associated with the left) but radical de-bureaucratisation — "Robin Hood with a calculator": we transfer what is saved on officials directly to accounts; an objective KPI; a weapon against Le Pen and Mélenchon. The pitch to the left (Glucksmann, Place Publique): the "democracy dividend" as a shield against the far right (their electorate are those abandoned by the system), the end of autocracies (the army does not shoot at the people if it receives the dividend itself), restoration of trust through transparency. One flank sees management and the market, the other social protection and anti-tyranny; this is the consensual nature of the protocol (§39, 07). Forecast: not adopted whole, but campaign analysts looking for a Big Idea for 2027 will seize on elements (algorithmic audit, a direct dividend instead of complex benefits).

## 10. Weak point of the case

The percentages in §7 are intuitive estimates on top of current polls, not a model; the assumption "half of Le Pen's electorate goes to the technocrat" contradicts the empirical loyalty of RN voters and needs testing. Article 11 applies to "the organisation of public authority" — the Constitutional Council may dispute assigning payments to that category; no legal analysis has been done. 🟡
