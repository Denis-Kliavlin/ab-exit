### 67.1. Demand versus realisation: the "want/will vote" gap

Demand for a centrist party in the USA is declaratively enormous. 62 % of Americans believe a third party is needed (Gallup, September 2025) — a 20-year high. 45 % identify as independents — a record. 7 in 10 under 50 want a third party. A purely centrist new party is wanted by ~14 % of all voters; 33–34 % identify as "moderates".

But real readiness to vote collapses: only 15 % are "very likely" to vote for a third party, 55 % — "to some extent". A gigantic gap between "want" and "will vote". People want the option but do not believe in it and do not vote. This is learned helplessness in pure form: the demand is broad but shallow.

### 67.2. The graveyard of centrist attempts

The history of this genre is a string of failures: Yang's Forward Party (2022, centrist, RCV), No Labels (spent millions to get on the ballots, folded without a nomination), Manchin (flirted, did not run), Americans Elect, Unity '08, RFK Jr. — all by one script: attention, money, failure.

This is not the accident of individual campaigns. It is a structural regularity.

### 67.3. Inventory of centrist ideas (complete)

What centrists actually propose:

**Procedural reforms** — Ranked Choice Voting, open primaries, independent redistricting commissions. These are real ideas, RCV has even been adopted (Maine, Alaska). But they are procedural and abstract. "We'll change the way votes are counted" is not felt by an ordinary person as a personal benefit — it is "how they count", not "what you get".

**Fiscal responsibility** — Manchin, No Labels. Balance the budget, reduce the debt. This is not even a benefit for the voter — it is pain (cutting spending). Bitter medicine without sweetness, like Thatcher before the wall (section 66). Mobilises only economists.

**Bipartisanship / "across the aisle"** — "we'll make the left and the right agree". A process, not a result. The voter cannot touch an "agreement". It sounds like "we'll be less angry" — a virtue, not an offer.

**"Common sense", "the sensible majority"** — Manchin's slogan verbatim ("fight for America's sensible majority"). This is not an idea but a pose.

Summary of the inventory: procedure (RCV) + pain (budget) + pose (common sense). Not one proposal that an ordinary person would hear and think "I personally get something right now".

### 67.4. The structural curse of centrism

This is not the laziness of particular politicians. It is a built-in contradiction.

Centrism by definition is "moderation", "balance", "no extremes". Moderation does not generate bright ideas, because a bright idea is always radical in form (even if reasonable in substance). The centrist fears the radical form — it contradicts his brand of "calm and reasonable". Therefore he is doomed to bland ideas.

The left can propose "take from the billionaires" — bright. The right — "build a wall" — bright. The centrist structurally cannot propose anything brighter than "let's all agree". One cannot simultaneously be "moderate in positioning" and "bright in idea". That is the curse.

### 67.5. AB-EXIT resolves the contradiction

AB-EXIT is the only known idea that is RADICAL in form (paying for exit from voting — sounds explosive) and CENTRIST in substance (excludes nobody, takes from nobody, consensual, voluntary, reasonable — sections 62, 65).

This gives the centrist what is structurally inaccessible to him: radical form with reasonable content. Compare on one voter:

— The centrist says "we'll introduce ranked-choice voting" → the voter yawns, does not understand why. — The centrist says "we'll give you a choice — money on your card or your vote becomes three times weightier" → the voter startles: this is about HIM, his money, his power, now.

The first is a procedure for political scientists. The second is a personal benefit for everyone.

### 67.6. How AB-EXIT cures three centrist ills at once

**Ill 1 — blandness, no igniting idea.** AB-EXIT gives the centrist a radical, viral, paradoxical idea. Centrism stops being bland — it becomes centrism with a nuclear warhead of attention.

**Ill 2 — the "want/will vote" gap (62 % against 15 %).** This gap is learned helplessness: "the third party won't win, my vote will be wasted". AB-EXIT strikes exactly here: it gives the voter a CONCRETE personal benefit (money or weight) regardless of whether the party wins or not. Even if you think the party will lose, it still pays you to support AB-EXIT, because the benefit is personal and does not depend on the outcome. This converts "I want to, but won't vote" into "I'll vote, because it personally pays me".

**Ill 3 — nobody to canvass (centrists are limp).** AB-EXIT gives an army of the burned (section 66, Chichvarkin) — angry, active, with money, who will carry the idea for free. A centrist party + AB-EXIT for the first time gets passionate canvassers, which centrists never had.

### 67.7. Honest caveats

First: RCV is not "nothing". Centrists have one working idea with proven adoption (Maine, Alaska). AB-EXIT does not have that. By the criterion "proven feasibility" RCV is ahead — it has been tested. But by the criterion "does it ignite the voter with personal benefit" RCV loses outright: RCV is "how my vote is counted", AB-EXIT is "what I get".

Second: "centrists have no ideas" is an exaggeration. They have ideas (RCV, budget, anti-corruption); they are bland and abstract, not absent. More precisely: centrists have no idea that an ordinary person would feel as a personal benefit. That is their hole — and AB-EXIT enters exactly into it.

Third: AB-EXIT runs into criminal law (the whole work of the session). Therefore the party proposes not "we'll introduce it tomorrow" but "we'll put it to a referendum / change the law". This is legal and it is its differentiator, but not instant adoption. The structural advantage remains: even as a promise of reform AB-EXIT gives the centrist a brightness he does not have.

### 67.8. Conclusion

The centrist movement in the USA has been waiting twenty years for an idea it cannot give birth to itself, because giving birth to the bright contradicts its nature. Demand is enormous (62 %), but conversion is tiny (15 %) — and the reason is precisely the structural blandness of what is offered, not an absence of desire among voters.

AB-EXIT by construction fits this void perfectly: the only idea radical in form and centrist in substance, which cures all three centrist ills at once — blandness, the "want/will vote" gap and the absence of canvassers. A centrist party needs an idea from outside, because its own nature forbids it from giving birth to one. AB-EXIT is that missing element.
